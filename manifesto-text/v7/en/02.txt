The ten tenets that follow are methods I came to recognize gradually through building and revising the Folly Series. Some grew out of specific techniques; others are simply judgments that still hold for now. I write them as tenets to leave coordinates by which the next entry into a ruin can be recalibrated. The next remnant may yet rewrite them.

## First Tenet: Ruin Lexicology

No two ruins disintegrate in the same way.

Once second nature expands beyond a scale an individual can simultaneously predict and bear, many human-made things enter life without truly entering perception or understanding. Ruin lexicology preserves a chance to stop and recognize them again.

Ruin lexicology begins with repeated pauses that resemble acts of “discovery.” Entering an unfamiliar remnant, the Ruinwright first confronts not an old name but a specific condition: something still bearing load, taking in water, leaning, or being pierced by plants. Only when the general word “ruin” can no longer distinguish such differences does naming become part of knowing. Ruin lexicology borrows the posture of natural-history classification, but its objects are always changing and will never appear again in precisely the same way.[^7]

When architecture has left behind shelter and use and become something outside common sense, space no longer offers instinctive reassurance. Learning to recognize danger becomes part of learning the ruin. Long observation on site gradually gives the Ruinwright a “wasteland eye”: from terrain, surviving structure, and environmental change, they judge what is still carrying load, what has fallen out of balance, and how water, wind, plants, or the return of people may rewrite the site. Changes in form and environment already disclose what the ruin has undergone, what is happening now, and where danger may emerge.[^8]

As wandering and recognition accumulate, things once gathered under the single name “ruin” begin to show their differences. New words grow from the site and form a “ruin lexicon”—an index of remnant morphemes shaped by walking, measurement, and memory. Land, architecture, state, and nature overlap within the names, allowing each to carry information about terrain, remnant structure, change, and environment at once. Distant sites are linked by shared signs, gradually assembling a world atlas of ruins that continues to rewrite itself.

## Second Tenet: An Archaeology of Things Close at Hand

Once use withdraws, the ruin does not revert to nameless matter. Surviving structures and objects still carry traces left by life and so enter the archaeological gaze. This is especially true of modern ruins: destruction falls upon things close at hand; what remains is not yet ancient, yet the order that sustained its function has already departed. These relics belong to the same civilization as we do, but not necessarily to our memory. They therefore hang between ordinary things and cultural heritage, falling out of civilization’s narrative of continuous advance and leaving, at the break in that narrative, both the needs of the past and the question of why those needs are no longer carried forward.[^9]

Before these close-at-hand relics, the Ruinwright reads a cross-section of an interrupted age. In material and wear, the Ruinwright recognizes stories that once took place and traces the changes that broke them off. The remembrance and respect that arise from this need not restore the past into a complete picture. They begin by acknowledging that the lives now gone were once real, and remain worth remembering after their disappearance.

The Ruinwright carries this recognition into the work while leaving the gap in the narrative cross-section where it is. Scattered traces are not assembled into a complete personal story; once later hands fill the section in, the relic’s original silence is easily covered over as well. Ruinwork lets utility recede so that surviving forms and wounds can surface again. It makes neglected time perceptible and returns the viewer’s attention to those who made, used, cherished, and finally left these things.

## Third Tenet: Borrowing the Ruin

When architecture is intact and strong, people even dare to let nature approach its boundaries and absorb its scenery into their own order. Once maintenance withdraws and those boundaries loosen, the relation quickly reverses. The scenery once arranged beyond the window enters the interior along cracks; the boundary once used to “borrow scenery” becomes a passage through which nature crosses and moves.

“Borrowing the ruin” takes its name from this reversal. The Ruinwright understands the ruin along the paths by which nature returns to human-made space, and places their own work within a transformation that has not yet ended. So long as one remains outside the boundary, the changing ruin is still reduced to an object of view. Only after stepping onto the site does borrowing the ruin truly begin from the fracture already there.

In surviving structures, the Ruinwright reads histories and ambitions built up layer by layer. After use has withdrawn, the ideals once raised to great heights are not entirely spent, and the ruin has therefore not fully come to ground. Ruinwork occurs where relic and nature become entangled again; the Ruinwright, too, gradually moves from being an author outside the ruin to a participant in and recorder of this long process.

To “borrow” means that the work depends on structures, materials, and ecological relations already present on site; it also means that this dependence is always provisional. The Ruinwright takes as little new flesh and bone from the earth as possible, turning instead to discarded matter to find materials that still possess residual capacity and can be safely joined to the site. Intervention grants no ownership of place. The site continues to constrain and rewrite the design; ruinwork is only one phase in a much longer transformation. Borrowing the ruin can therefore take the form of an interstitial garden practice: entering through gaps overlooked by planning, working with existing watercourses, structures, and vegetation, without covering the remnant with a new total order.[^10]

When borrowing the ruin comes to an end, the Ruinwright must also be ready to leave. Exploration, documentation, and structural intervention carry different permissions: an unattended ruin is not ownerless land, and the name of art cannot turn unauthorized entry, removal, or abandonment into “coexistence.” Even where conditions for intervention have been secured, the burden placed on the site should be kept to a minimum. On departure, only what is permitted and able to coexist safely should remain; everything else leaves with the Ruinwright. Time is returned to the site.

## Fourth Tenet: Brutal Materiality

Modern ruins often appear as exposed concrete frames. White exterior walls and finishes that once softened their edges are the first to mildew and peel away, leaving the skeleton that divides space and carries load. As second nature sheds its skin, order and force meet directly. Ruination inadvertently strips the building down to an appearance akin to Brutalism, while exposing architecture once again as a material fact governed by weight, lifespan, and matter.[^11]

In the construction of certain Brutalist buildings, I read a modern impulse: calculation makes material relations visible, and structure organizes space through explicit boundaries. This precision displays the power of technical reason, but also leaves traces of an attempt to make the environment submit to order. The “brutal” here is not an essential definition of Brutalism; it is one force I read backward from the ruin.
