[^2]: The “Angel of History” and the wind called “progress” here allude to Thesis IX of Benjamin's “On the Concept of History.” Walter Benjamin, “On the Concept of History,” in Selected Writings, vol. 4, 1938–1940, ed. Howard Eiland and Michael W. Jennings, trans. Harry Zohn (Belknap Press of Harvard University Press, 2003), 392.
[^3]: The term “New Topographics” comes from the exhibition New Topographics: Photographs of a Man-Altered Landscape, curated by William Jenkins at George Eastman House in 1975. Its photography turned from idealized landscape toward ordinary environments altered by human beings, examining the built environment with a relatively cool, restrained gaze. See New Topographics: Photographs of a Man-Altered Landscape, exhibition catalogue (Rochester, NY: International Museum of Photography at George Eastman House, 1975).
[^4]: On the “untimeliness” of modern urban ruins—arriving too early and often disappearing before acquiring the aura of age—see Nick Yablon, Untimely Ruins: An Archaeology of American Urban Modernity, 1819–1919 (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 2010). On the intellectual history and traditions of representation through which ruins and modernity mutually constitute one another, see Julia Hell and Andreas Schönle, eds., Ruins of Modernity (Durham, NC: Duke University Press, 2010), https://doi.org/10.1215/9780822390749.
[^5]: “Anthroposcene” is reused and redefined here as a working concept; it is not the geological “Anthropocene.” Noel Castree has used Anthroposcene to describe the networks of knowledge, institutions, and publications that have formed around the Anthropocene. I use the term differently, treating it as a stratum: an accumulating layer of human-made reality produced through long processes of building, use, abandonment, and recording. Architecture, infrastructure, waste, and traces of everyday life overlap within it, while modern remnants remain upon it as residues not yet fully absorbed into history. In its material sense, the concept lies adjacent to discussions of the archaeosphere / anthropogenic deposits and technostratigraphy. See Noel Castree, “Changing the Anthropo(s)cene: Geographers, Global Environmental Change and the Politics of Knowledge,” Dialogues in Human Geography 5, no. 3 (2015): 301–316, https://doi.org/10.1177/2043820615613216; Matt Edgeworth et al., “Diachronous Beginnings of the Anthropocene: The Lower Bounding Surface of Anthropogenic Deposits,” The Anthropocene Review 2, no. 1 (2015): 33–58, https://doi.org/10.1177/2053019614565394; Jan Zalasiewicz et al., “The Technofossil Record of Humans,” The Anthropocene Review 1, no. 1 (2014): 34–43, https://doi.org/10.1177/2053019613514953.
[^6]: “Ruinwork” and “Ruinwright” are working neologisms proposed in this text. Ruinwright is itself coined here; wright is used in the sense of maker / builder, emphasizing making, constructive judgment, and sustained practice rather than the more general idea of a ruin artist.
[^7]: “Ruin lexicon” and “ruin lexicology” are working concepts proposed in this text. The former refers to the Ruinwright's personal vocabulary formed through long-term wandering; the latter to the method of recognizing, combining, and indexing its morphemes. Names compress structural states, temporal sediment, environmental intervention, and perceptual experience into semantic relations capable of further expansion; they do not refer to an established branch of linguistics.
[^8]: Here “wasteland” names the condition of modern ruins not yet situated by history and still being shaped by the continuing consequences of destruction.
[^9]: I render the Chinese term yicun here as relic, emphasizing an object that, after losing its original use, continues to carry cultural, mnemonic, and material traces. This is a working translation for this text, not the only archaeological meaning of relic.
[^10]: “Interstitial garden” is a working concept proposed in this text. It draws on guerrilla gardening's informal use of idle or neglected spaces, but does not inherit the element of unauthorized intervention; structural intervention here remains bounded by the permissions, safety requirements, and site memory described above. See Richard Reynolds, On Guerrilla Gardening: A Handbook for Gardening without Boundaries (Bloomsbury, 2008).
[^11]: “Brutalism” is reinterpreted here in relation to ruined structures and is not equivalent to the term's full architectural-historical definition. On early discussions of the ethics, form, and material of New Brutalism, see Reyner Banham, “The New Brutalism,” Architectural Review 118, no. 708 (December 1955): 354–61.
[^12]: “Force-flow view” is a working concept proposed in this text. “View” names a basic way in which the Ruinwright observes and understands things: architecture and ruin are treated as relational fields in which forces continually accumulate, transmit, turn, transform, and dissipate. The displacement of one component alters the whole along its connections; collapse thereby appears as a process in which paths of force are continually rewritten.
[^13]: “Destructive test-body” is a working concept proposed in this text: a finite testing body constructed by extracting key conditions from a building's materials, connections, mass, and relations of force. It is used to observe failure; it neither substitutes for the building itself nor justifies arbitrary destruction of real remnant structures.
[^14]: In the “Travel Souvenirs” section of One-Way Street, Benjamin writes of the Alcázar of Seville that human movement is absorbed by the “silent commotion” of ornament. I extend this spatial experience to the process by which cracks, plants, and nonhuman forces reoccupy the ruin. Walter Benjamin, One-Way Street and Other Writings, trans. Edmund Jephcott and Kingsley Shorter (New Left Books, 1979), 82.
[^15]: “Liminality” originally describes a transitional stage in which a subject has left a former identity without yet entering a new one. See Arnold van Gennep, The Rites of Passage, trans. Monika B. Vizedom and Gabrielle L. Caffee (University of Chicago Press, 1960), 10–11; Victor Turner, The Ritual Process: Structure and Anti-Structure (Aldine Publishing, 1969), 94–130. I extend the concept here to the spatial condition of the ruin.
[^16]: Graphic notation developed intensively in experimental music around the mid-twentieth century. See Earle Brown, December 1952, in Folio and 4 Systems (Associated Music Publishers/G. Schirmer, 1952–54), https://earle-brown.org/work/folio-and-4-systems/; John Cage, Fontana Mix (Henmar Press, 1958), https://www.wisemusicclassical.com/work/69276/Fontana-Mix--John-Cage/. The two works respectively demonstrate how open graphics and transparent layers can organize multiple realizations.
