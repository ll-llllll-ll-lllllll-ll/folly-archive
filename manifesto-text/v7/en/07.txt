Every Folly preserves a measure of the builder’s audacity: knowing that form cannot withstand time, the builder still makes something that does not yet exist stand for a while. Beginning from the desire to look at ruins, the Folly Series builds in reverse a temporary stage on which an event has yet to happen. It leaves behind a form for performance and remains open to further performances. From the moment the structure is built, it carries disintegration within it; the gaze therefore shifts from completed wreckage toward a ruin still coming into being.

As a maker within ruins, the Ruinwright must preserve the place of the “Fool” for themselves. They walk toward the cliff, allowing some potential to remain unspent by established experience and conclusion. Lightness comes from knowing clearly that there is no guarantee underfoot, not from forgetting what the broken stones once carried. The old measures have failed. They can only decide for themselves what remains worthy of belief, and keep walking, keep creating, where there is nothing to rely upon.[^26]

## The Backstage of Order — Roles and Performance

Second nature is like a stage that never stops running. People and things each assume roles, sustaining a coherent plot through their responses to one another. So long as these relations hold, performance coincides with life so completely that we rarely notice it. A destructive blow can suddenly sever that reciprocity: function loses its object, identity loses its response, and the fact of performance, previously hidden behind order, becomes visible. The absurd enters through the break.[^27]

The ruin resembles the backstage left after the stage has been struck. It still belongs to second nature and preserves traces of how the stage once operated, but the coherent plot can no longer assign a place to everything. Those who enter it almost instinctively acquire a strange sense of freedom. The choices left here are hardly abundant—only broken walls, fractures, and forces not yet spent. The rules have not disappeared; the old script has broken. Precisely because these finite things have left their former roles, people gain an almost absolute freedom of role, and postures that could never appear on the intact stage briefly become possible.

Faced with the unknown, second nature often uses naming, explanation, and knowledge to draw unfamiliar things into intelligible relations. This capacity allows life to continue, but it can also erase the intensity of unfamiliar experience too quickly. The Ruinwright therefore delays this closure slightly: performance brings them near regions not yet explained, allowing the sublime carried by fear to remain for a while longer, and letting both knowledge and the impulse to keep exploring and creating arise from the encounter with the unknown.

If the Ruinwright has a kind of “madness,” it appears in the moment when this role temporarily shifts out of place. Reason has not disappeared, yet action moves beyond the position ordinarily permitted by everyday order. The performer always knows that they are performing, so the deviation preserves the possibility of return. It allows one to approach, for a time, what has not yet been explained or given form, without immediately drawing it back into familiar measures.[^28]

# Final Resonance — Returning Sound to the Moment of Its Occurrence

Modern ruins often remain after the event but before the ending. The former order has withdrawn, yet its consequences continue; the past has not been closed by historical narrative, and the future no longer advances according to the old plan. What I call the “wasteland” names precisely this condition of remaining inside the aftershock.[^29]

Many urban ruins left by renewal, development, and expansion remain after the wave has receded. The speed and desire of construction have dispersed; people who once lived there gradually leave; accounts of why the building failed disappear with them. Time in the ruin seems arrested only because it no longer advances according to human plans, uses, or plots. What truly continues are consequences that have not yet ended. They replace complete stories and become the narrative subject of space. The postdramatic lies not in the event having ended, but in the ending’s refusal to complete itself.

## Postdramatic Space[^30]

Remnant structures preserve the evidence of events and also the disintegration left unfinished. The viewer walks along their traces, temporarily joining scattered clues through pauses and detours: the past is lodged in fracture surfaces, the present occurs with the body’s entry, and structures not yet stabilized carry the event onward into the future.

The Folly Series grows from remnant structures. The Ruinwright extracts fractured structures and residual forces from the site and organizes them through new connections, turning long erosion into a process the body can enter. Postdramatic narration moves from viewing into construction: the past is not restored, but fragments continue to change within new relations. The form is not organized in advance to express a theme; it is a temporary body formed by material, force, fragments, and time.

If ruin is understood as the form architecture leaves at the end of its time, the Folly Series writes that endpoint into its own construction in advance and enters it actively through self-destruction. While the work is still standing, the ending has already begun. The viewer can therefore look backward from the point where order is about to come undone and see how it was established, maintained, and how it bred cracks within.

A local rupture draws out the whole; a displacement before the eye calls up a much longer duration. Postdramatic space places the viewer at the edge of an ending without granting omniscience from outside. The story has not finished, yet space can already reveal how it arrived here. Contradictions, imbalances, and absurdities suppressed while human order remained intact rise to the surface with the fragments.

The Folly Series does not search for a pleasing voice for this process of failure. It brings mechanical structures, fragments, and terrain directly into a sounding relation. Noise that has been composed yet retains its abrasive grain gives the absurdity exposed at the fracture, for the first time, a form the body can hear. The Ruinwright transmits force among fragments through mechanical structures and traces change with graphic notation. Disintegration latent within the structure thus acquires a sounding body, and the Folly Series appears as “an instrument still playing after the curtain has fallen.”

## The Essence of the Instrument Is Noise

The most immediate image of a ruin is the fragment; the most bodily experience, however, often comes through sound. While a building functions normally, structure recedes into life and remains silent. Fracture tears this order open, and change begins to reveal itself as sound. The ruin appears as an immense instrument that has lost its humanly imposed musical order.

An instrument's capacity to sound does not mean that every sound becomes music. The boundary between musical sound and noise is drawn by tuning, rule, and auditory expectation, and changes with culture and modes of listening. Material first produces sound under force; culture then organizes part of that sound into instruments and music, calling the rest noise. When I say that “the essence of the instrument is noise,” I mean the material level at which sound precedes classification. Tuning and culture organize sound into music; once those rules loosen, sound exposes its material source again.[^31]
