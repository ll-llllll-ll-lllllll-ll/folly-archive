# Preface — After Architecture

Architecture shapes life almost without a sound. Long before we truly understand space, we have already internalized doors, windows, walls, and other basic architectural elements as common sense, and learned to inhabit them with ease. Since industrialization, people, architecture, and order have increasingly joined into a continuously operating system of life, together weaving what I call “second nature.” We are born into it, labour, build, and seek shelter within it; before long, we come to take this highly designed system for the world’s original face.[^2]

As second nature continues to expand, rationalism gradually draws what was once unknown, dangerous, and uncontrollable in nature into a territory of transformation. Because “control” and “predictability” once proved their absolute effectiveness in defending the threshold of survival, we came, almost as a matter of course, to elevate this reason into the highest measure of all things. Under its pervasive rule, the surplus space in which life once had room to breathe is quietly flattened; the contingencies of natural growth and the trial and error of experience are excluded in advance by calculations of efficiency. At this point, even care can imperceptibly harden into discipline.

Once we entrust everything bound up with life to second nature, the stability of the system acquires an almost inviolable status. The separation between human beings and nature is no longer merely physical distance, but a boundary of power drawn by order in order to fortify itself. Rationalization keeps patching the world before us, presenting a continuity maintained by force as though it were indestructible. In the end, we become caught in a net of our own weaving: we lose not only the courage to depart from the rules, but increasingly the ability to imagine that any departure from the given track is possible at all.

And yet, the ruin appears.

It emerges along cracks long accumulated in the darker folds of civilization, taking form wherever order can no longer sustain itself. As natural erosion steadily strips away the shell of order, the costs left unreckoned beneath its orderly surface finally become visible to the eye. The measures that once organized life, weighed value, and allocated resources now fall coldly back upon their own creations, deciding which are still worth caring for and which are exiled into time to spend themselves away. The cost of order is no longer hidden in unknown consequences; it stands exposed upon its own remnant structures.

Ruins briefly lend us the gaze of the “Angel of History,” compelling us to look back at the narrative that calls itself civilization and progress. With our backs to the future and our faces turned toward what has already happened, we see behind the spectacular achievements the objects cast aside and the ideals cut short. The storm called “progress” still drives us blindly forward, while wreckage continues to gather beneath our feet and behind us. As we grow accustomed to filing these costs under the losses supposedly necessary to development, the road of modernity becomes, inevitably, a road paved with ruins.[^3]

## Modern Ruins

The ruins accumulating along modernity’s road gradually form a new terrain. In the second half of the twentieth century, New Topographics photography turned its lens away from idealized nature toward landscapes altered by human beings, recording with a cool, restrained gaze the ways modern life marks the ground. The coldness of these images grows precisely from second nature: human order has profoundly transformed the environment, yet through sheer familiarity has receded into a landscape waiting to be read apart. When this austere way of looking extends to modern ruins, the costs long suppressed into the background by the dominant melody of “progress” begin to surface. The more a building once stood for stability and permanence, the more piercing its silence becomes after it falls into ruin.[^4]

Modern technology produces this new terrain at an unprecedented speed. Surging demand for space drives construction ever faster, allowing buildings to proliferate with expansion, while the work of caring for them one by one remains difficult. Records of buildings accumulate in ever greater quantities, yet what truly settles into collective memory does not increase with them. The landscape of civilization is therefore studded with remnant structures whose forms remain while the living contexts that once sustained them have already been stripped away. Living among them, we too have grown accustomed to keeping our distance from the death of our own creations, choosing forgetfulness with an ease that can feel almost innocent, and even accepting destruction as an unavoidable stage of development.[^5][^6]

Modern ruins, however, do not pass into silent dissolution on the day they cease to function. Their destruction no longer needs to be dressed up as a picturesque or spectacular event; the calmness of their material surfaces already bears the long aftershock of abandonment. As an “unfinished reality,” the modern ruin remains disturbingly close to us: the people and memories attached to it may not have disappeared, and consequences not yet reckoned with continue to ferment beneath the surface. Before history has lowered its curtain, every conclusion can only be provisional. We have no other way forward than to seek understanding through physical proximity—first recognizing the structures, traces, and anomalous sounds that remain on site, and only then allowing judgement to enter the work of memory.[^7]

Countless remnant structures lie scattered across a human depositional layer, residues of a human-made order that continues to accumulate. They are neither wholly forgotten nor easily admitted into memory. For me, a ruin is the shape that absent time grows upon the ground: a history in which I did not take part, a homeland that no longer coincides with memory, yet still remains in the world through its surviving form. Its fragments and broken narratives cannot restore the past, but they can keep me from being absent as it continues toward disappearance.[^8]

## Ruinwork and the Ruinwright

What the Ruinwright takes up is precisely these material bodies that continue to decay as they depart: modern ruins.

I call this creative practice that begins after architecture “ruinwork.” It works from the structures and residual forces still present on site, treating the very process of “becoming ruin” as a mode of construction. It does not seek permanence; it writes into the design the work’s fate of gradually converging with its environment and coming apart. The Folly Series is ruinwork given concrete form: the day a work is completed merely fixes it, for an instant, in a posture already collapsing; everything that follows is surrendered entirely to time.

The Ruinwright is a subject-position I have forged through long study of ruins, as well as a method for practising ruinwork. It seeks no worldly professional qualification; it is closer to a solitary law an independent researcher sets for themselves. When architectural collapse breaks apart the discipline embedded in real space, ideas with no place in ordinary order finally gain room to breathe. In this terrain stripped of established rules, my research and making have found a precise point of contact. Only here do I dare to grant this untamed, self-growing craft the title of wright.

Where architecture ends, ruinwork has only just begun.

# Ten Tenets of the Ruinwright

The “Ten Tenets of the Ruinwright” are creative principles distilled, revised, and tested through a series of ruinwork practices. Their movement begins with looking at wreckage and stepping into it, then passes through material fracture, the dissipation of force, and the residual sounds of structure, before arriving at the inevitable end of every made thing: how the Ruinwright bears judgement within the fate of disintegration. Some tenets have condensed from concrete techniques; others remain only provisional conclusions that still hold within the practice as it stands today. To set them down as tenets is both to leave experience for future work and to remain ready for the next site to rewrite them entirely.

## First Tenet: Ruin Lexicology

No two ruins disintegrate in the same way.

When second nature expands beyond the limits of individual perception and understanding, many human-made things are quietly installed into life and into our spatial common sense before they ever grant us “access” to their actual workings. Day after day we face these sealed system shells, excluded from their real operation, yet accept their existence as self-evident through a kind of lazy common sense. Only when destruction and abandonment arrive, when this thin screen of familiarity is shattered and the object has fully fallen into ruin, do we finally gain an opening through which to enter and recognize it again.

Ruin lexicology begins with this rediscovery after social function has been stripped away. Entering an unfamiliar remnant, the Ruinwright sets aside old names that have already ceased to work and confronts the building’s exposed physical reality: is it still bearing load, already leaning, or pierced through by plant roots? When the formula of “original name + ruin” can no longer contain these living differences of transformation, a new, bottom-up act of naming becomes the beginning of reconstructed knowledge. It borrows the rigorous posture of natural-history classification, yet what it attempts to anchor are objects always in collapse and evolution, objects that will never return in exactly the same form.[^9]

To anchor the reality of collapse, one must first confront the void that appears when common sense fails wholesale. Once a building betrays its promise of shelter and use and falls back into nameless, coarse matter, space no longer gives the body instinctive reassurance. To stand there is like being thrown into a wilderness at night: direction and support disappear at once. Yet it is precisely this weightlessness, charged with danger, and the long confrontation with the site that force the Ruinwright to temper a “wasteland eye.” Instead of relying on false common sense, it reads surviving structure and environmental change directly, judging what still bears load and what is nearing imbalance. The direction of a crack, the depth of rust, and the paths opened by water, wind, and plants already confess, at the level of matter, what this body has endured, what is happening to it now, and where fatal collapse may emerge.[^10]

Through long wandering and repeated acts of recognition, the minute differences produced by collapse grow increasingly clear. New words spring like weeds from between the rubble, eventually gathering into a “ruin lexicon”—an index of remnant morphemes woven from measurement, walking, and memory. Within it, land, architecture, physical state, and natural transformation overlap, so that each name can hold the site’s changing image with precision. Through this vocabulary generated from within ruins, the chaos and danger that once drifted beyond common sense are anchored again within our own map of knowledge. Isolated sites of abandonment begin to correspond with one another, gradually assembling an expanding atlas of the world’s ruins.

## Second Tenet: An Archaeology of Things Close at Hand

Once a building loses function and maintenance, it does not immediately revert to nameless matter. This condition is especially stark in modern ruins: destruction falls upon things still close enough to us to feel familiar, and the surviving objects are not yet old, yet have already been reduced to remains. Their surfaces still carry the patina and wear of everyday life—a familiarity we can easily recognize, though it may not belong to memories we personally lived. They are therefore left awkwardly suspended between “ordinary discarded objects” and “cultural heritage,” dropped from civilization’s triumphal narrative of continuous advance and leaving, at the fracture of an age, only the faint trace of what once was there.

The Ruinwright examines these close-at-hand relics with the cool gaze of archaeology, asking why things once bound so intimately to life could be abandoned in a single moment. In the wreckage we read cross-sections of an age violently cut short: scratches and peeling finishes hold traces of former negotiations with daily life, while layers of dust lead backward toward whatever event forced that life to stop. The respect produced by such looking has no wish to restore a broken past into a warm and complete picture. It is, rather, a clear-eyed act of witness: to face incompletion, acknowledge that the lives now withdrawn were once real, and grant them one solemn look before they disappear into total forgetting.[^11]

This restrained act of recognition enters ruinwork as well. Even if ruinwork extends construction into processes left unfinished, it must leave the break in the narrative exactly where it is, rather than assemble scattered traces into a complete personal story. Once a wound in the record is filled by later imagination, the remnant’s own dense silence is easily covered by cheap lyricism. Ruinwork therefore does not restore the function of objects or reconstruct the illusion of former life. It retreats to the narrative and material cross-section itself, allowing the forms and wounds of the remains to surface again and giving neglected time back its weight. It suspends, deep within the ruin, a kind of “spatiotemporal anchor” that asks the viewer to pause and, through this shell, notice once more the people who built, used, cherished, and finally left these things.

## Third Tenet: Borrowing the Ruin

When architecture is intact and strong, human beings habitually tame nature beyond a boundary and draw its scenery into their own order through the practice of “borrowed scenery,” displaying the confidence of an artificial will that believes it can master the natural world. Once the structures that sustain this order break, however, the subject-object relation slips and reverses. The view once framed beyond the window seeps indoors along cracks; the opening that once separated inside from outside becomes a passage through which nature crosses back and reclaims ground.

To “borrow” means that intervention can exist only by relying on structures, leftover materials, and ecological networks already present on site. “Borrowing the Ruin” begins from this premise. Where the traditional garden uses the wildness of nature to heighten the dignity of order, Borrowing the Ruin moves in the opposite direction: it uses remnants already eroded by nature to expose the vulnerability of what was built, along with the hubristic ambitions it once carried. The Ruinwright reads the remnant by following, in reverse, the routes through which nature retakes space, and reads in its broken skeleton the histories and ideals layered into it. Once the human-centred viewpoint has been displaced, what hangs in suspension is not only a narrative of decline, but the material structure itself. The people who once lifted everything upward are gone, yet the forces once locked into the building have not fully dissipated. The ruin is caught in mid-fall, unable to land.

In practice, this way of seeing becomes an exceptionally humble form of ruinwork. We borrow not only the viewpoint of erosion, but some of its methods. The Ruinwright avoids taking new flesh and bone from the earth wherever possible and, like water or roots, follows existing paths of disintegration toward the remains already on site. From discarded masonry and cracked concrete, we identify forces not yet spent and use that residual capacity as support. This is not only “borrowing matter,” but also “borrowing momentum”: following the drainage angle produced by terrain, the small ecologies that have developed in wall joints, and the tracks along which roots and cracks advance side by side. Material conditions continually constrain and rewrite the Ruinwright’s actions, turning intervention into a genuine form of “interstitial gardening”—entering through gaps left by planning and system failure, using existing structures as a skeleton and abandoned matter as components, without attempting to cover the collapsing remnant with a new total order.[^12]

If something is “borrowed,” it must eventually be returned. Anything that stands by relying on the residual strength of a site already carries the knowledge that its existence is temporary. When a phase of Borrowing the Ruin comes to an end, the Ruinwright must leave with dignity. Exploration and intervention have strict ethical limits: an unattended ruin is never a lawless place free of responsibility, and the name of art cannot turn unauthorized dismantling or damage into “coexistence.” Even when permission to intervene has been secured, the additional load placed on the site should be reduced as far as possible, and departure should leave only traces capable of coexisting with the natural processes already there. This withdrawal is a final yielding of the subject-object relation: human intention disappears, the space is returned to nature, and wind, rain, and time take over once more, returning the place to a wilderness judged by forces that existed before us.

## Fourth Tenet: Brutal Materiality

Modern ruins often present themselves as exposed concrete frames. Exterior finishes that once enclosed interiors and softened boundaries are the first to mildew and peel under weathering, leaving only the skeleton that bears load and divides space. This irreversible stripping resembles the removal of second nature’s false skin, exposing the most direct posture of structural force. Ruination can inadvertently create an aesthetic resemblance to Brutalism; in truth, it simply exposes architecture again as a physical fact governed by gravity, corrosion, and finite material life.[^13]

Within such exposed, brutal structures, I often read a powerful modernist impulse. Once people mastered precise structural calculation and synthetic materials, they began to believe they possessed the ability to command space. This precision displays the confidence of technical reason, but also the desire to draw the environment entirely into human order. Yet when the same skeleton becomes ruin, its “brutality” begins to produce the opposite tension: matter appears to struggle free of the will once imposed upon it.

The “brutality” of the modern ruin is born from this fracture of contradiction. The skeleton left by exact calculation may still be standing, yet the materials that compose it have returned to the rule of gravity, corrosion, and microscopic failure. Highly developed technical inheritance and the most primitive, blind physical forces meet violently in the same body, giving the remnant a wildness that resists domestication. As the skeleton of order begins to fail under erosion, the “brutal” beneath the lost skin deepens into true “brutal materiality”—the flesh exposed after control has withdrawn. When walls lean and force paths slip out of balance, rational calculation is forced to face material weight, error, and finite lifespan. Reason has not ceased to work in the ruin; it can simply no longer stand above material reality.

Ruinwork therefore strips away added symbols and formal embellishment, allowing the work to carry, bare and visible, the physical traces of its formation, loading, and eventual decay. The “brutal” here is not an aesthetic attachment to dilapidation. It comes from the uncertainty deep within matter that geometry can never fully contain. Where, exactly, do the materials modernity once took pride in reach their true load-bearing limits? At what point do they resist, and where do they yield? The answer is not contained in drawings or calculations. It can only surface from material itself, through the long struggle of collapse.

## Fifth Tenet: The Force-Flow View

The displacement and disintegration of ruins under force are not accidental system failures. Traces of structural decay not only signal the impossibility of restoration; spreading cracks act like pointers tracing backward toward the origin of destruction. Under continuous erosion, weak nodes and material fatigue make the moment of fracture unpredictable, yet the accumulation of force and the stress concentrations toward which it gathers remain legible. The ruin therefore contains, from the moment of construction, a chain of causality that can later be traced backward through the surviving structure.

Collapse inside a ruin is rarely an isolated local event; it travels through chain reactions and pulls the whole with it. Fragments that fall violently may, by chance, form new relations of overlap, bracing, and suspension, assembling a damaged yet still active system of force transmission. From this the Ruinwright develops a “force-flow view”: broken walls, hanging beams, and leaning columns are read not as dead matter at rest, but as a dynamic relational field in which residual energy continues to move and dissipate. Even in wreckage that appears still and balanced, countless connections and cracks charged with hidden movement have already inscribed the long history of destruction within the structure.

When ruinwork connects a mechanical transmission apparatus to these active or tensioned relations, invisible internal stresses are translated into observable displacement, vibration, and fracture. The apparatus becomes a means of tracing causality: latent force flows are made tangible, and struggles hidden deep inside matter can be read back along their paths. Yet this connection is never a manipulation from above. Once ruinwork takes up the residual forces of the ruin, it is irreversibly drawn into the same fate of disappearance. The Ruinwright may appear to arrange fragments, but in fact remains entangled in the same causal web: a seemingly trivial alteration can reshape a local field of tension, and the slightest error can change the outcome of the entire structure.[^14]

## Sixth Tenet: The Destructive Test Body

Beneath the illusion of stability—even permanence—woven by second nature, destruction is often abstracted into something like an ordinary metabolism. We have not learned to resonate with the death of our own creations; instead, we become numb to the process by which they decay. This numbness comes from a mismatch of perception. Microscopic fatigue and damage in materials unfold too slowly for the eye to register, while catastrophic collapse is so violent that the energy it releases can easily crush the human body. Between the “imperceptible minor change” and the “unbearable major event,” we lose any bodily sense of a building’s lifespan. The destructive test body begins here: it compresses a long, hidden process of decay into a surrogate body capable of real-time feedback, returning destruction to a scale the eye and nervous system can directly feel.

The “destructive test body” extracts key relations of material, joints, mass, and gravity from the ruin and reconstructs them as a body made to be destroyed. Displacement markers, load sensors, strain gauges, and vibration pickups become “sensory prostheses” that enter danger in our place. They translate hidden injuries the eye cannot see and lethal tensions the body cannot approach into visible and audible trauma, turning structural disintegration into a material “death by a thousand cuts” that can be watched.[^15]

Just as human beings have used the figure of the android to ask what it means to be human, the destructive test body probes the ultimate limits of second nature. It carries the structural logic by which second nature sustains itself, yet precise calculation is used to drive that same logic to the brink of collapse, taking our place in approaching and crossing a boundary of death that human-made order is designed to keep at bay. When the reason that builds order exposes, by its own hand, that order is not permanent, the test body becomes a cruel mirror. In its wreckage we see reflected the finitude shared by human-made things and by the human subject itself.

Ruinwork turns this verification of finitude into a central creative method. The Folly Series is designed to move toward disintegration. Once its planned loosenings begin to break open, environmental intervention and uncontrollable error quickly take over, dragging the wreckage beyond what calculation and prediction can contain. Uncomposed randomness blooms through the tearing, and with it arrives a brutal beauty. This beauty does not stand against reason; it appears precisely where reason has reached exhaustion and can no longer provide life with an answer, in the final resonance of a ruin.

## Seventh Tenet: Return to the Suspended Realm

Modern ruins often resemble shells whose souls have died before their bodies. Complex spatial frameworks once pushed to the limits of human use lose guidance, light, and occupation, and become dark, unfamiliar labyrinths. Doors no longer indicate entrances; corridors no longer guarantee arrival. Broken partitions and stairs that end in midair betray the promise of shelter and strip the building of the safety it once offered. These remains cannot reassemble the whole that has disappeared; instead, isolated limbs make absence newly visible. The wanderer moves through them facing structures that have lost meaning yet still carry load. With people gone, these “residual structures” turn from background into agents and establish a posthuman spatial order.

The ruin, evolving on its own, gives off an attraction charged with pressure. It seizes the gaze and draws the viewer into an abyssal counter-gaze, unsettling the assumed boundary between person and space. The building no longer receives its visitor hospitably; whoever enters must move carefully under the threats of leaning, falling, and obstruction. The “central position” human beings once imagined themselves to occupy proves, at the scale of the ruin, to be only a temporary vacancy left by time. Ornament, cracks, and plants begin to reorganize space according to their own rules, while human movement is in turn absorbed by the site. Suspended between the human-made and the natural, between former shelter and present abandonment, one is left without ground. From this weightlessness, the ruin’s liminality emerges.[^16][^17]

Ruinwork brings separated fragments back into contact and returns the gaze to a space from which people have disappeared. The pieces still carry memories of former life, yet can no longer fit back into a living context. Reassembled, they form an unfamiliar body that holds a grotesque, fragile balance under the continual pull of gravity and corrosion. At first it appears as a foreign object forced into the site. As the environment continues to consume it, however, it becomes tightly entangled with the ruin’s own texture, eventually mutating into a “spatial monster” that can no longer be separated cleanly from the remnant around it.

Yet the most conspicuous intruder on site is not this constructed body. Once space no longer turns around human use or human safety, the subject’s position is stripped away; what now appears strange and out of place is the person who still insists on finding a place for themselves there. Human being and space are left together in a condition without ground. This is the Suspended Realm.

Faced with this suspension of the subject, the Ruinwright refuses to rebuild a false sense of mastery. Once the work is planted on site, it is already prepared to be exiled, altered, and carried away from its original intention. Its fragments anchor memory toward what existed before destruction, while its grotesque body directs the gaze toward a future buried beyond recognition—yet neither end can finally be reached. I build the Folly Series precisely because I cannot foresee the shape of that other side. The work never prepares an answer for the unknown. It allows familiar things, once stripped of their old relations, to condense briefly into a nameless body, and then sacrifices that body without reserve to time.

## Eighth Tenet: Dissipation into Sound

When a dull cracking sound erupts from deep within a load-bearing structure assumed to be permanent, it punctures a psychological defence I call “civilizational dissonance.” As a precursor to collapse, the anomalous sound is the first path by which that dissonance reaches the senses: the building has not yet physically fallen, but the shelter and safety it held in the mind have already given way.

This bodily unease comes from irreversible material imbalance within the ruin. Residual tension and gravity are released through friction and fracture and converted into vibrations in the air; dripping water and the howl of cross-drafts interweave into sonic traces of erosion. These sounds mark the structure’s continuing death after it has left human order behind, and at times they seem to send an uncanny summons beyond the ruin itself. The space keeps people out through the threat of collapse, yet seizes their attention through sound and draws passers-by back into a force field not yet spent.

Ruinwork inscribes this paradoxical listening experience back into the structure of the work. The Folly Series is set within an irreversible process of collapse, guiding the residual aftershocks of the remnant into another sounding construction already charged with tension. Precisely because these sounds violate the expectation that architecture should remain “silent,” passers-by stop and listen, trying to locate where the fracture came from and when the next break will arrive. Listening to the ruin ceases to be passive reception and becomes an immersive tracing of destruction, driven jointly by fear and curiosity.

## Ninth Tenet: The Blueprint Becomes a Score

From the first line of the design, ruinwork binds the movement of fragments to the sound of structural fracture, so that the work carries the rhythm of collapse from the outset. When the Folly Series finally produces a real sound field through bodily distortion and material tearing, the engineering blueprint that once organized its mechanisms becomes a latent acoustic score.

A conventional score disciplines sound through standardized notes and durations; the blueprint of ruinwork gives the nodes and lines on the drawing a more open reading. Anchors, load-bearing axes, and levels no longer promise stability, but become latent marks of stress, displacement, and spatial deformation about to crack into anomalous sound. When cold geometry becomes a set of threshold lines anticipating fracture, the blueprint crosses the boundary of engineering and turns into graphic notation for the rhythm of destruction.[^18]

Yet as the work’s disintegration drifts away from its preset path and the original mechanical logic no longer fits the wreckage before us, the blueprint falls from “instructor” to “that which is recorded.” Following the collapse, the Ruinwright draws the actual trajectories of falling components and the positions of sound sources back into the sheet. Dust, water stains, and rust spreading across the work are likewise surveyed and marked. These records of physical decay force their way into the abstract drawing, warping the once-rigorous engineering symbols through repeated staining and redrawing.

Natural erosion continues to rewrite the drawing throughout the Folly Series’ “performance,” turning it into a palimpsest of layered traces. Once fixed instructions are covered by new trajectories, the blueprint that once presumed to choreograph the ruin completes its own ruination.

## Tenth Tenet: The Meaning of Futility

Technically, ruinwork operates much like conventional architecture, yet its motive retreats into a realm of absolute solitude: I often carry it forward alone, deep within a ruin. Here the social instructions are stripped away—no client’s brief, no supervisor’s intervention, no calculation of capital return. Attention contracts around the physical reality of the ruin itself. Building is no longer a way of selling “shelter,” but a means of giving a concrete form to a fleeting process of destruction and guiding it toward a carefully composed overturning. The final collapse of the Folly Series may appear to be triggered by years of external force, yet the seed of destruction was already buried on the first day of construction. Everything ruinwork cultivates simply follows the irresistible laws of nature, escorting the work toward a threshold of disintegration from which it cannot return.

When society measures the value of labour by “duration” and “output,” pouring effort into a work destined for destruction appears economically senseless, almost absurdly futile. This utilitarian measure arrogantly wagers “beauty” on solidity and permanence, while dismissing fracture, weathering, and disappearance as “failure.” Ruinwork takes its position precisely where this scale of value breaks: in the ruin, it listens for the material reality exposed after function has been wrung from matter and tests the last bearing capacity a structure releases as it nears failure. The Ruinwright gives part of their labour over to the dissipation of meaning and value, practising an almost stubborn conviction: anything that has received concentrated attention deserves respect, even if it can never reach permanence.

The “meaning of futility” preserves dignity for labour that cannot be settled in terms of return, while also drawing an ethical boundary that cannot be crossed. Accepting destruction does not mean worshipping it; wanton damage does not receive absolution simply by calling itself art. Even if destruction is the work’s unavoidable destination, it cannot erase the dense bodily experience of building, or the aftershock left in the heart by witnessing collapse. Such labour needs no claim to eternity in order to justify itself. If it truly took place in this groundless place, it remains worth carrying through to completion.

## The Boundaries of the Ruinwright

Modern ruins cannot simply be absorbed into aesthetic or utilitarian systems of value, because they are never shaped by nature and time alone. War, engineering accidents, and failed decisions leave consequences that remain materially present; traces of labour and human tragedy are entangled with the wreckage in the same space. Weathering may eventually change the form of the remnant, but it cannot erase the causes of those histories. Ruinwork therefore holds to an ethical boundary: it refuses to turn real disaster into an abstract beauty of destruction, and refuses to let aesthetic experience conceal responsibilities and wounds that still demand to be faced.

Here, art sharpens perception of the site, while architecture offers rational means for working with matter and space. Yet when the Ruinwright carries both into a ruin, restraint becomes a discipline. The ability to reorganize material and extract meaning must be matched by a harder resistance to the impulse to “reshape” the ruin. Weathering and erosion should not be casually interrupted in their slow takeover, nor should our limited understanding of beauty be imposed upon a disintegration that has not yet taken form. We must also remain wary of the discourse we build around the work. When concepts begin to summarize the ruin in its place, ruinwork itself risks becoming another layer of “second nature.” Theory may provide a path into the site, but never the right to look down upon it.

Recognizing this limit, the Ruinwright respects both nature’s long, ungovernable force and the enormous costs sedimented by human order, and enters alone into a collapse that has not yet reached its end. The site must be faced in its loss of control and danger: sharp fragments may cut the hand, thorns must be crossed, mud entered. Only by keeping the body in this terrain long enough to survey and listen can one truly take up the ruin’s residual resonance.

The Ten Tenets do not exhaust the understanding of ruins. They are only provisional rules established through a small number of present practices, and they must continue to revise themselves through future exploration. As the study of ruins deepens, ruinwork begins to reveal more than historical traces and the causes of destruction. It also begins to expose why I am drawn back to these remnants again and again, why they compel me to return, and why I am willing to remain for so long in this boundless desolation.

# The Broken Frame — From the Picturesque to the Sublime

At first, ruins often enter aesthetic vision through the gentle appearance of the “picturesque.” Under the combined erosion of time and nature, intact architecture loses its polish and moves toward fracture, yet among rubble, moss, and mottled surfaces it can acquire a fleeting romantic posture. Each time we stop to admire a ruin, the “picturesque beauty” we seize is therefore only one moment of destruction, accidentally fixed into a composition of beauty.

## Picturesque Beauty: The Making of the Frame

Picturesque beauty not only extracts a view “fit for a picture” from the ceaseless changes of broad nature; it also answers the modern fascination with ruined landscapes torn by time, full of irregularity and sudden breaks. The gaze turns toward ruins because it wants to glimpse danger and loss of control, yet it also wraps them in layers of romantic filtering and retreats into the safety of aesthetic experience. Through this psychological distance, an invisible “frame” is established: fracture is taken into the picture, danger is temporarily tamed by composition, and destruction hardens into a landscape outside the self, as though collapse occurred only within the frame.[^19]

The artificial ruins—the follies—of traditional European gardens once attempted to domesticate damage, collapse, and natural invasion into a process that could be designed and watched. Vast durations of decay were compressed into the miniature stage of the garden, as though the fleeting beauty of ruin could be held indefinitely at the moment most agreeable to the gaze.

Real ruins, however, do not obey this false frame. Even when natural damage is deliberately directed through a surviving structure, we cannot stop or reverse the force that continues to strip it apart. A single storm, or the loosening of a load-bearing joint, is enough to drag the ruin violently away from the perfect state in which it was easiest to admire.[^20]

Expanding cracks, leaning members, and mottled corrosion record the continuous reorganization of force. The surface may appear dead, yet structural change underneath never stops. Once we understand that the picturesque form cannot be held still, and that human efforts cannot call a halt to the ruin’s own disappearance, collapse enters the image with real physical weight and shatters its calm.

The channel of looking and the safe distance sustained by picturesque beauty collapse with it. The sublime appears beyond the broken frame: the form before us may be taken away by the next wave of destruction at any moment, and the viewer’s former sense of safety begins to tremble before this raw physical violence.[^21]

The “Broken Frame” (fig. 1) gives concrete form to this failure of aesthetic distance. The frame itself has become ruin and has lost its capacity to contain change as scenery. With the picture inside it entirely absent, the damaged skeleton that once merely marked a boundary turns into the object of the gaze. Exposed breaks and displaced layers force the eye inward, compelling it to trace the force that tore the whole apart.

## The Folly Series: Letting Time Cross the Frame

Traditional classical gardens often used “borrowed scenery” to draw nature into an order of viewing: domesticated nature was carefully selected and cut into a framed view. Yet when this self-proclaimed “domestication” fails under time and architectural order collapses beneath natural erosion, the subject-object relation reverses. Wildness once fixed outside seeps through cracks into the building and, through continual stripping and counter-erosion, retakes spatial sovereignty from the inside.

The Folly Series begins from this reversal. Intervention starts from the condition of ruin; nature is led back inward along existing cracks and allowed to erode the shell of human order from within, bringing a quiet deconstruction to the building. The series also borrows the name of the historical garden folly, but strips away the warmth of its picturesque function and amplifies the force it once contained. The “corpse of the object” that was formerly arranged for pleasure no longer remains a safe scene. In collapse that continues to move beyond control, it forces the viewer to confront their own smallness.

To reach this point, the Ruinwright buries the “seed of disintegration” directly in the work’s constructive logic, making destruction part of the work itself. Unbalanced structures that sway, materials scored in advance with fracture lines, and suspended relations all contain possible paths of loosening and collapse, so that the installation stands precariously while remaining charged with the physical tension of what is waiting to fall.[^22]

This preconstructed disintegration becomes visible over time. Faced with the unavoidable weakening of a human-made thing, we experience the anxiety of survival again within this loss of control, and touch a sublime that lies beyond the safe boundary of the picturesque, a sublime produced by material disorder itself. The psychic tremor of this “civilizational dissonance” carries us into an unstable threshold: the building still retains the shell of space, but no longer promises shelter or safety.

The frame loosens beneath our feet.

## Ruins of the Mind: The Failure of Distance

The Folly Series pushes the viewer all the way to the point where the frame begins to loosen. At first, the picturesque viewer lingers before the ruin while keeping destruction sealed inside the damaged scene, preserving the security of life outside the frame. This boundary feels stable because second nature not only assigns order and destination to things; it also fixes the human subject in what appears to be an unshakable position within the world.

Only when the cracks in the wall visibly spread beneath one’s feet does collapse reveal itself as an act of physical violence still in progress. And once the viewer realizes that the same erosion could unfold in any familiar place, the sublime in nature breaks through the aesthetic boundary and begins to consume the very order through which the world had been understood. In this general failure, the humanized space on which life depends collapses inwardly as well, while objects once used, separated, and cherished lose their former weight in an emptiness that no longer answers them. Familiar architecture becomes a “lost space.”

As the grand narratives attached to space withdraw, the psychological shelter provided by crowds and everyday life evaporates with them. What remains is a cruel form of solastalgia: home may still stand where it always did, yet it no longer coincides with memory—familiar enough to be recognized as home, but too estranged to carry the years from which one was absent. Here one is forced to face histories never personally lived and countless possible lives that never came to pass. The absolute loneliness of this encounter comes from a real and silent absence: familiar life has departed, while the cold space remains.

The inner sublime erupts from this loss of distance. Once the viewer can no longer mourn as an observer, the “lost space” throws body and ruin into the same irreversible current of time, forcing the realization that the viewer too is subject to disappearance. Things that still possess weight and warmth will one day recede into unnoticed residues on a historical cross-section; when that same fate turns toward the self, helplessness travels from the past into the present. Under the pressure of a void that seems to strip both body and mind of support, one instinctively clenches one’s hands, trying to grasp some final, tangible sense of still being here.

## A Mirror of the Modern Mind: Recognizing the Self in Fragmentation

In this void, where nothing offers support, consciousness becomes unusually clear. Once we understand that matter cannot be returned to the past and time cannot be reversed, the one who still feels their own smallness and continues to respond after meaning has fallen away is the same “I.” Will then appears as the act of affirming oneself within finitude and deciding how to continue. One begins to recognize oneself again—small, but real; finite, yet still continuing.

The damaged beauty of the “broken frame” thus becomes a mirror for the mind. When lost space withdraws every familiar response, loneliness opens a moment of almost absolute self-scrutiny, and a question begins to surface: beyond the faces we have gradually formed in order to meet the world, what still belongs to us?

The fractures we see are not only cracks scored into objects in the external world. Within the worldview that has formed us over time, we have learned to wrap both self and things in a skin woven from order and identity, treating the world as a mirror whose parts recognize and fit one another. The rupture in the ruin breaks through that mirror. What it opens is not only matter, but the gap where the “world of identity” no longer fits the reality before us. Loneliness does not return a purer self. It merely removes, sometimes violently, the voices of order that usually answer in our place, allowing desire, fear, and impulses not yet discovered to rise again along the fractures of disintegration.

# Ruin Imagery — Where Desire Goes

Born into modern society, we have inherited ways of judging things through order, efficiency, and use, and have absorbed these measures into an almost instinctive gaze. The modern ruin is a break in that logic: once value is withdrawn, the utilitarian gaze reaches into the remnant and finds nothing to grasp. Yet when a moment of “beauty” strikes from the fracture, we do not simply leave. We follow the crack further, searching for the source of the desire that made us look.

## The Broken Prism

If the mirror of the “broken frame” notices a mismatch when the gaze turns back upon itself, the “broken prism” looks through that mismatch to ask how second nature refracts a disorderly reality into a legible spectrum. Ordinarily we scarcely notice the glass that does the refracting. Only when the prism breaks do its thickness, stress, and fragility appear at the fracture. A ruin works in much the same way. Once the order and maintenance that sustained a continuous surface are cut off, matter returns as weight, corrosion, and lifespan; the loosening of a single joint can expose the structure that depended on it, just as the failure of one building can draw into view the civilizational order that sustained it.

The broken prism grants no omniscience. It only allows relations suppressed by the intact surface to reappear through the break. Its beauty therefore remains in an unsettled displacement: the whole has collapsed, yet the fragments catch light differently. The viewer is drawn to that reflection, but what emerges is no longer a romantic explanation laid over damage. Poetry comes from the materiality of the fracture itself.[^23]

The fear of collapse and a cool kind of consolation occur together in this tremor of beauty. As the old city continues to fail, it reveals its material body and reflects the fragility of the order built by human beings. The tensions that remain after destruction exceed what a utilitarian measure can account for; so too does the brilliance that destruction sometimes exposes. The ruin thus reveals the limits of human-made things in both life and will: we want meaning and beauty to endure, yet some ideals carry new tragedies within their realization, while others lose their echo when the bodies that bore them disappear.

## The Ruin Romantic[^24]

Looking inward along the crack, a deeper tragedy of modern life comes slowly into view. The order that provides shelter and predictability has also, almost invisibly, compressed the room in which contingency and perception can breathe. When what can be measured, verified, and used occupies the centre of judgment for too long, experiences that resist quantification are pushed to the edge. The ruin gives those marginal experiences physical weight. In disintegration, it shows that our capacity to feel beauty has not been completely assimilated by the system.

The desire that is drawn toward destruction and cannot help moving closer to ruins is what I call the “ruin complex.” It is fascinated by time’s ruthless carving of matter and by the violent entanglement of decay and new growth within the same form, giving disappearance a melancholy attraction that is difficult to refuse. What we recognize in the wreckage is not cheap tenderness, but an almost stubborn form of care: a longing for the residual warmth of a world not yet entirely gone, and a determination to stop for things that have lost their use. Such care cannot reverse disappearance, but it drags the remnant forcefully back into the present and, in doing so, lets us confirm that our capacity to feel beauty has not gone numb.[^25]

The Ruin Romantic takes its position in the fissure between destruction and survival. It wants to sacrifice every residual force to the instant of collapse, yet at the same time tries, futilely, to stitch some glimmer of life from the fragments. “Redemption” here refuses the fantasy of complete repair. Fragments cannot restore a dead age; at most, before a nihilism that cannot be resisted, they wrest a small foothold for the will to live.

This stubborn care therefore exceeds nostalgia and becomes a point from which creation can begin again after inherited narratives have failed. As collective grand narratives dim and leave the stage, remnants become scattered oracles for those still searching for direction, preserving a final possibility of feeling. No one can demand from them an ultimate explanation of life’s purpose. One is forced instead to look again among the debris: what is still worth attending to, what is still worth exploring, and what, even within the void, is still worth building.

This care for the incomplete ultimately becomes another starting point for building on a narrative wasteland. Once grand narratives collapse, scattered remnants resemble broken lines of verse, preserving for those still searching for direction one last possibility of feeling. No final answer can be recovered from them. One can only measure again within the ruin: what deserves to remain, what deserves to be explored. The search for such meaning is bound to be a solitary journey without guarantee, carrying the modern tragic paradox that beauty feeds on pain and destruction. Nihilism offers no way out. It only strips away, then throws the problem of how to keep living among the broken things, bluntly, back to everyone who remains.

## The Bearing of the Fool

When the desire to rebuild has receded, the ruin remains suspended in a time that refuses resolution. The walls neither return to life nor arrive at an ending through total collapse. In the tension woven from splendour not yet exhausted, unfulfilled wishes, and destruction that comes late, the ruin rises from the background of the story to become its narrative subject. It wakes from a long, unattended suspension and silence, reclaiming the centre of attention through the violent transformations growing inside destruction, and provoking an almost hallucinatory expectation—as if something within that mixed body has not yet been spent and is still waiting to be found.

To approach the ruin at this point is to encounter pity, curiosity, and the thrill of looking where one perhaps should not, all entangled together. We search the mixed fragments for what may have been concealed, and are deeply drawn to the moment when order loosens and established form begins to fail. This exploration moves beyond everyday common sense and utilitarian purpose. Unrelated fragments are allowed to meet by chance, and creative desire long held down begins to surface again. What happens here is not free-floating fantasy, but an immersion—almost an initiation—inside the actual weight of the ruin: facing the force that tears order apart among the remains of massive structures, feeling the world withdraw in abandoned corners, even accepting bruises and pain as an unspoken cost of entering this wasteland. Only after such an initiation, through listening to and discovering the site, can making touch what has long lain beneath the cover of ordinary explanation, waiting to be awakened. These things have been forming their own narratives and postures in the desolation for years. Ruinwork does not stitch the fragments back into the past. It follows the force that has settled within them, reconnecting residues so that what still sleeps in that mixed body can answer the present and become visible again.

In response to this listening and answering within the texture of wreckage, the Ruinwright enters the site in the guise of the “Fool.” The Fool is an active departure from social utility and prescribed identity: in the barrenness of the ruin, he looks for freedom among possibilities not yet exhausted by existing conclusions, and reawakens a more primitive impulse to build. Some resistance to the established order may remain at first; yet after living for long enough with this “freedom of having nothing,” what endures is the pleasure and momentum of discovery and creation itself.

The Fool is the role temporarily assumed by the Ruinwright; the Folly is the material form taken by that performance. The former enters the wasteland in a posture not fully disciplined by utility. The latter, through an act of “folly” indifferent to gain and loss, gives material pause to an action that knows it cannot endure and chooses to answer anyway. This is the Ruinwright’s stubbornness: knowing form cannot resist time, yet insisting that something not previously there should stand, however briefly, and be given the dignity of appearing.[^26]

The Fool walks toward the cliff so that potential is not exhausted in advance by experience and conclusion. On this passing stage, the desire that first drew the eye to ruins can finally acquire a body, building in reverse a place worthy of emotional investment. Above the wreckage, the Fool’s step remains light—not because he is ignorant, but because he knows perfectly well that nothing beneath his feet is guaranteed, and has not forgotten the weight those stones once bore. In such an end-time landscape, he chooses for himself what to believe, and where there is nothing to rely upon, keeps walking, keeps creating.[^27]

## Backstage

Second nature resembles a stage that never stops running. People and things assume roles and sustain a coherent plot by answering one another. So long as these relations remain intact, “performance” overlaps with life so completely that we seldom notice it. But a rupture violent enough can cut that reciprocity in an instant: function loses its object, identity loses its response, and the performative structure once hidden behind order becomes visible. The absurd enters through the crack.[^28]

The ruin is like the backstage left after the stage has been struck. It still belongs to second nature and preserves traces of how the performance once ran, but the larger plot can no longer assign a fixed place to every thing. Those who enter almost instinctively feel a strange freedom. The options here are not abundant—only broken walls, fractures, and echoes not yet spent. Order has not vanished, but the old script has broken. Precisely because these finite things have slipped free of their former roles, people gain an almost absolute freedom of role, and postures that could never appear on the formal stage can briefly take form here.

A ruin does not offer a shortcut to understanding. It preserves the unfamiliar thing in something closer to a pre-understood state, still waiting to be dismantled and read. Faced with the unknown, second nature tends to classify and explain before it has properly looked, drawing strangeness quickly into a controllable network of relations. Such rational ordering allows life to continue, but it can also erase the physical and psychological intensity of unfamiliar experience too soon. What the Ruinwright does is delay that closure slightly: through “performance,” one approaches a region not yet fixed into meaning, allowing the fear, sublimity, and curiosity released by the unknown to remain a little longer, and drawing from the confrontation the knowledge and momentum needed to keep exploring and making.

If the Ruinwright has a “madness” of his own, it appears precisely when the role shifts temporarily out of place. Reason has not gone dark; action has simply crossed a boundary drawn by everyday order. The performer knows that he is still performing, and therefore keeps open the possibility of returning to ordinary life. This temporary deviation makes it possible to approach what has not yet been explained or given definite form, without rushing to drag it back into familiar measures.[^29]

# Final Resonance — Playing After the Curtain Falls

Faced with the absurdity of the world, standing on the unsupported freedom of the ruin and within the Ruinwright’s lucid madness, the Folly Series becomes a material projection of an impulse toward ruination. Each act of building in the wasteland is treated as a final dance before the curtain falls, releasing the tensions not yet spent in the wreckage into a “carnival of self-destruction.” Its visual and sonic language follows the desire to look and builds, in reverse, a series of sites already destined to collapse and waiting to be discovered again: a beating heart set into a sunken ruin overtaken by stagnant water; an invisible clamour sounded across scorched, silent ground. Through the posture of these ruinworks, we follow anomalous sound, fragment, and crack back toward the modern ruins in which we ourselves are standing.

## Postdramatic Space[^30]

Many modern ruins abandoned by development and expansion remain in the afterwash of the wave called “progress.” Time in the ruin seems arrested only from a viewpoint that continues to race with that wave. From within the ruin, the stranded remnants were never carried forward by progress at all: the wave’s speed and inertia failed to take them with it and instead tore them into damaged bodies. Change continues within the ruin itself. The aftershocks and consequences left unfinished when the wave receded replace the images of life before abandonment and become the narrative subject of the space in the present.

In this derailed time, modern remnants continue to hold an unfinished disintegration. Past upheaval remains lodged in fractured surfaces, while unstable structures carry the event onward into the future. Out of walls stranded by the current of the age emerges a distinctive “postdramatic” condition: no script is laid down for what comes next, nor is the past asked to repeat itself. Residual stress and invasive natural forces entangle fragments from moment to moment, producing a stage and an aesthetics that belong to the ruin itself.

If the ruin is the form architecture leaves at the end of the current of time, the Folly Series writes that endpoint into its own construction in advance and enters it actively through self-disassembly. It carries within itself a tendency to dismantle the form of death, and in doing so acquires the viewpoint of a “residue of the symbolic order.” The work is built from the signs of civilization, yet as it disintegrates and sheds that outer skin, it stands as a body stripped of civilization’s appearance within civilization’s own interior, piercing back through the symbolic order whose completeness has already begun to fail. By entering this exchange of gazes, the viewer too can step outside the narrative of order for a moment and look back at how civilization is built, maintained, and finally taken apart.[^31]

## Becoming an Instrument through Disintegration

The most immediate image of a ruin is the fragment, but its most bodily experience often arrives through sound. While a building remains in use, it keeps a stable silence. Yet on the day that stability can no longer support itself, a sudden crack—perhaps somewhere beyond the eye, deep within the structure—can break the long quiet. The anomalous sound pierces the established order of space precisely because it belongs to no timbre that everyday experience can easily classify.

If “not sounding” can also be understood as an instruction of order, then the inhabited building is a thing designed to “remain silent.” A conventional instrument, in essence, joins silent materials to a fixed order and maintains a precise set of relations in order to produce predictable timbres. The logic of the ruin runs in the opposite direction. Matter is already capable of vibration and sound; its silence during habitation is partly an effect of architectural order suppressing that material possibility. Once the building becomes a ruin, collapse brings sound with it. Matter released from order reveals its own resonance, and disintegration itself becomes the instrument’s true performance.

To restage this process of stripping away order and releasing sound, the Ruinwright reconnects dispersed fragments through transmission structures added to the site. The intervention does not tame sound; it gives stresses hidden within the structure a body through which to vibrate. The Folly Series thus becomes “an instrument still played by collapse after order has left the stage.” Although the apparatus appears rationally designed, its sound is abrasive and unstable. The absurdity released when order breaks acquires, for the first time, a physical impression that can be heard with the body. Its score is no longer governed by human intention, but improvised by the relations of force within the collapsing site, each sound corresponding to an actual transformation inside the structure.

## From Detuning to Return

If physical materials continue to vibrate whether order is intact or broken, then the categories we have built from auditory expectation, tuning, and music theory are themselves extensions of order in the realm of hearing. Seen again through the fracture, even the Chinese word for “instrument,” yueqi (乐器), carries a double discipline: yue, “music,” is established by cultural habit and listening conventions, while qi, “vessel” or “implement,” is delimited by purpose and structural function. When the ruin tears these two supports apart, both “music” and “instrument” lose their footing, and the thing is forced to reveal the roughest physical fact of the site: sound itself—the “noise” that precedes classification and has not yet been domesticated by institutions.[^32]

Every instrument uses tuning and structural constraint to hold wood, metal, and physical tension within a system that can be played. When environmental erosion, deformation, or a loss of tension occurs, sound crosses the subtle boundary maintained by listening habits and systems of tuning and is judged “out of tune”—an error that exists only under particular human rules. At the scale of the material itself, however, this need not be understood as damage. The components are leaving restraints imposed by human beings and moving toward another material state. Ruins undergo the same transformation: once social function and cultural narrative withdraw, the institutional order that arranged matter begins to break apart, yet the components do not become inactive. Under gravity and weathering they continue to collide, vibrate, and fracture, releasing sounds no longer held inside a score.[^33]

What the Folly Series constructs is precisely such a “collapsing instrument,” one that passes step by step through disintegration on site. It produces sound through fracture while irreversibly consuming the very body that makes sounding possible, gradually slipping free of the rules by which an instrument is ordinarily recognized. The instant we hear it go “out of tune,” we are abruptly stripped from familiar expectation and drawn back into an absolute present, facing a sound not yet stained by established meaning or concept. Site and sounding body become one; performance is the full course of disintegration. Sound approaches its limit through expenditure, waiting only for the final instant when structure tears through and cuts open the present.

## The Irreversible Threshold

As the performance nears its end, stage and instrument have already converged in the same disintegrating physical body. The Folly Series meets destructive energy with its own structure, dismantling the wholeness of order before the ear through a sound that is itself coming apart. The work enlarges the gaps in order to a bodily, material scale, and at times we use that rupture to test whether we have touched something closer to the real; the body losing its form turns back toward us like a mirror. The Folly Series bears the pain of collapse in our place, transferring destruction to a body able to register and measure its force, so that we can recognize again the final limits shared by ourselves and the human-made order we inhabit.

To make a ruin from intact materials, send it deliberately toward destruction, and let disorderly noise erupt from the collapse: only an act this close to “madness” by ordinary utilitarian measures can cut a finite opening in the dull surface of order and give long-suppressed natural contingency a moment in which to become publicly visible. Measured against social reality, staking the work’s own existence carries a kind of resolve; it makes irreversibility into a cruel measure. The Folly Series borrows this absolute inability to return. By accepting a fate of deliberate destruction, it gives heavy physical weight to a despair that cannot be calculated and that seems to dismantle every order around it.

When accumulated physical energy finally crosses the threshold, the last act of sounding condenses into a “self-destructive final resonance.” Sound leaves the score entirely and survives only as the material’s final vibration under extreme force. The instant of sounding is also the instant in which the structure that makes sounding possible is destroyed; because these events coincide, the note is severed from the possibility of ever returning at the moment of its birth and becomes an unrepeatable material event. The stage withdraws itself as the performance ends, and the rules that sustained both instrument and stage collapse with the structure. This final resonance does not force destruction into a sentimental climax. It simply lets the work arrive quietly at the terminal state of ruination that had been written into its fate from the moment of construction.[^34]

# Epilogue: After Ruinwork

When that “self-destructive final resonance” finally sinks into silence, what remains on site is not only wreckage, but a lingering sense of withdrawal and vacancy that spreads through the mind. Our fascination with this act of self-destruction inadvertently tears open a deeper fatigue of the postmodern city: when systemic failure cannot be repaired, people learn to live beside decay, drawing from the ruin a lucid kind of despair through a posture of “detached acceptance.”

This romantic immersion in decline is born from a psychic break. It places the inner life in absolute solitude and tries to reduce the world to ruin, treating destruction as a paradoxical form of redemption. Once such immersion in failure hardens into a habitual state of mind, nihilism begins to grow at the boundary between despair and aestheticization. The true danger of nihilism lies in mistaking “destruction” for the only honest response to falsehood. If the real can be proved only by breaking things apart, action must become increasingly violent until negation itself turns into a new rule and encloses the person once more in a prison repeatedly reinforced by self-destruction.[^35]

Yet beyond this negative logic of destruction and its cycle of refusal, the ruin still exposes another, more romantic possibility. We continue to stop before things that have lost their use; we still find beauty in fragments that can offer no return; we may even give time to something we know cannot endure. Such experiences neither conceal the absurd nor repair reality, but they show that feeling has not been completely occupied by utility, identity, and signs. Beauty here is not an answer used to escape. It is a kind of innocence in facing what is actually before us: proof that we can still approach the world bodily and directly, without making use, obedience, or revenge the condition of that approach.

The “innocence” meant here is not naivety or an ignorance of how the world works. It is the decision to return after having seen the calculations behind the rules—a capacity to open the mind again before things are once more brought under the law of utility. It knows that the inherited meanings attached to decaying things will eventually disappear, yet still chooses to stop for what has lost value and to labour for forms that promise no return. This return of both mind and body becomes an awakening to existence. It offers no final salvation and has already moved beyond self-destruction driven by revenge. What it breaks open instead are the limits of the body and the boundaries that established rules have written as final. Once both material composition and social image have been stripped away, the ruin reveals the inner life and unresolved possibilities of matter itself. Beauty erupts from this exposed field of life, imagination, and creation.

The human body, life, and capacity all have limits, and we do not need to flee that finitude. Precisely because time and existence have an end for us, where we choose to give them is what makes choice our own. Ruinwork, at its core, is the attempt, through each event of ruin, “to give beauty to imperfect destruction.” Such beauty does not defend destruction, nor does it beautify the pain behind it. It only bears witness that within a finite life we were still able to encounter beauty, choose beauty, and, in doing so, exist truthfully.

[^14]: The “force-flow view” is a working concept proposed in this text. “View” names a basic way in which the Ruinwright observes and understands things: architecture and ruins are treated as relational fields in which forces continually accumulate, transmit, turn, transform, and dissipate. [^15]: The “destructive test body” is a working concept proposed in this text.
