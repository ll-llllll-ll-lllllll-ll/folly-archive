# Preface — After Architecture

Architecture shapes our lives almost imperceptibly. Long before we understand space, we have already absorbed doors, windows, walls, and the basic elements of buildings into common sense, and learned to dwell among them with ease. Since industrialization, buildings, technical infrastructures, and institutional rules have become ever more closely interwoven, gradually forming a continuously operating environment of life—what I call “second nature.” We are born, work, build, and seek shelter within it, until we come to mistake this extensively designed and maintained system for the world as it was given to us.[^2]

As second nature expands beyond us, rational calculation and technical means bring more of nature’s unknowns and hazards within the reach of prediction and intervention. Control over our surroundings and foresight about what lies ahead have repeatedly helped human beings resist danger and sustain life; it is precisely through those successes that the pursuit of certainty became embedded in the logic of civilization’s construction, eventually serving as a basic measure of whether life could be considered secure. Yet as that measure moved beyond the demands of survival and entered the organization of everyday life, the room for chance, deviation, and trial and error—the conditions in which life might grow of its own accord—steadily narrowed. Maintenance and care, once undertaken to protect life, gradually acquired the character of discipline under the demand that the system continue to run without interruption.

When we entrust everything bound up with our lives to second nature, maintaining the system’s stability gains an almost unassailable legitimacy. The distance established between humanity and nature to fend off danger has long since hardened into boundaries of order that now constrain human beings themselves. Rationalized operations continually repair damage and failure within the system, concealing the inherent fragility of human-made order beneath the appearance of perpetual maintenance, until it seems indestructible. We become entangled in a net of our own making, increasingly accustomed to understanding and arranging life by prescribed rules, and less and less able to imagine what might lie beyond the assigned course.

And yet, the ruin appears.

Ruins emerge along cracks long gathering in civilization’s hidden recesses, taking form wherever order can no longer sustain itself. As natural erosion strips away the surfaces of buildings, damage and fragility once concealed by their solid appearance are exposed anew. The costs of construction, use, and abandonment come to rest upon the surviving material forms. The same measures that once organized life, assigned value, and distributed resources now fall back upon civilization’s own creations, deciding which buildings deserve further care and which must be abandoned to dissolve in time. The ruin exposes the choices this order makes among its own works, and the costs those choices leave behind.

For a moment, ruins lend us the gaze of the “angel of history,” compelling us to look back upon the narrative that calls itself civilization and progress. Facing what has already happened, with our backs turned toward the future, we glimpse discarded remains and ideals cut short behind the spectacle of achievement. But the wind called “progress” continues to drive us forward, while wreckage piles up beneath and behind our feet. We have learned to account for these losses as the necessary expenditure of development; the road of modernity thus becomes inescapably strewn with ruins.[^3]

## Modern Ruins: The Strata of Progress

The road of modernity stretches onward, while the ground beside it accumulates the marks of what has been left behind: housing, industrial facilities, and roads interweave into a landscape so ordinary that it scarcely attracts notice. In the latter half of the twentieth century, New Topographics photography turned its gaze away from romanticized wilderness toward such everyday terrains, recording the traces of human construction with a cool, restrained eye. Those austere images reveal the second nature we have come to take for granted: human order has profoundly changed the face of the land, while daily familiarity has slowly dulled our sensitivity to those changes.[^4]

@caption Fig. 1. Contemporary man-made landscape: a city development zone still partly wild, where a road leads toward a new district through a conspicuous height-restriction gate. Photograph by the author, 2024.

When this detached gaze reaches modern ruins, the losses long concealed by narratives of progress come into view. Yet confronted with these remains, so close to everyday life, we have become accustomed to treating their disappearance as the inevitable price of development. Why a building was erected, why it was abandoned, and what was lost as it passed away—questions that ought to provoke reflection—are displaced by ready explanations of efficiency, renewal, and progress. Over time, these explanations form a layer of structural ignorance, allowing us to witness the death of our own creations without asking seriously about its causes or costs, and to keep destruction at a reassuring distance.

Meanwhile, the road of modernity goes on, and abandonment accumulates as construction and renewal accelerate. Modern technology has greatly enlarged humanity’s capacity to alter its surroundings, while demands for spatial efficiency and changing functions have grown increasingly exacting. Buildings are erected and replaced in ever shorter cycles to meet needs that expand and shift with equal speed; amid repeated development and changes of use, many spaces lose the grounds for continued care long before their material lives are exhausted. Records of buildings proliferate, yet the experiences of life that are recognized and allowed to settle into collective memory are not necessarily preserved alongside them. The landscape of civilization is thus studded with remains that retain their forms even after the contexts of life have been withdrawn from them.

These creations, still short of the end of their material lives yet already deprived of social purpose, lie dormant beside the prosperity we inhabit. They give modern ruins their piercing untimeliness. Often they die too soon: before time can confer the halo of age, they face the prospect of being erased again.[^5] Such premature deaths resist being settled into the image of a melancholy or magnificent spectacle. Traces of lives not yet dispersed and material changes still underway leave a prolonged “aftershock” across their cold surfaces.

The ancient ruins familiar to us may be only a small fraction of countless structures that happened to survive. Whether a building endures depends on its materials and surroundings, but also on whether later generations protect it, repair it, and recognize its historical worth. More ordinary remains have vanished through erosion, demolition, and repeated building, leaving scarcely any way to recover even the appearance they had as they disappeared. Modern ruins, by contrast, linger for a time before that selection has run its course. Reinforced concrete still retains sharply defined human-made contours, though its functions and maintenance have departed; the structure gradually loosens under erosion and may be erased altogether by the next round of construction. We can thus draw close to an experience that has largely escaped the historical record: how structures never granted the halo of age, unattended through the years, disappear together with the very forms of their ruin.

The dust of history has therefore not yet settled over modern ruins. Their abandonment belongs to a relatively recent past, and their remains still linger near daily life. Their unfinished stories have not fully retreated into history, while surviving structures continue to change through erosion and collapse. Modern ruins remain so *close* to us: when we witness a familiar creation lose the order that sustained it, the confidence that the world around us might remain secure for a long time begins to loosen along the same cracks. The memories and costs that have not yet faded, together with this unfinished material transformation, remain entangled with our present. We cannot declare these works devoid of all value or meaning simply because their social purpose has ended.

Along the road of modernity, new construction presses ahead, while abandoned creations remain where they were left. Overlapping with buildings still in use, infrastructures, and traces of daily life, they gradually form a stratum of humanity’s own making, use, and abandonment—what I call the “Anthroposcene.”[^6] This stratum bears the recognized achievements of construction alongside residues not yet wholly forgotten, yet seldom admitted into collective memory. For me, the ruin is the form that absent time grows upon the earth: a history in which I took no part, a homeland that no longer coincides with memory, yet remains in the world through what survives of its body. Its fragments and broken narratives cannot bring the past back; but as it passes toward disappearance, they allow me, at least, not to be absent.

## Ruinwork and the Ruinwright

I have long felt an affection, difficult to find a place for, toward modern structures abandoned before their time. Human labor, everyday life, and aspirations once gathered within them; many have lost the reasons for their continued care long before their material lives have run their course, while the histories and memories lodged in them have no certain resting place when the buildings disappear. This tenderness toward what remains has kept me from leaving ruins to photographs and commemoration alone. I return to the sites, follow fractures that have not yet closed, and ask how these remnants came to take their present form and how we might still respond to them.

What should become of modern ruins is far more complicated than a choice between repair and reconstruction. Demolition and removal take still-usable materials out of circulation and consume further resources in transport and disposal. Repair may extend a building’s use, while restoration seeks to recover a former state; each intervention can alter the material and historical traces that survive, without ensuring that the lives once lived there will return. Some interventions even dress genuine suffering as scenery. What happens to those displaced by abandonment and redevelopment? Who inherits the traces of people who lived and worked here, and who may determine the future of what remains? Each question brings its own claims and costs. Even when answers can be found separately, they may resist a satisfactory resolution at one site; when a new order is laid over the old wreckage, other unsettled costs may be buried in a deeper stratum.

I once followed the customary paths of architecture and engineering, trying to find solutions that could be implemented within actual requirements of function, technology, and construction. I did not, however, continue along lines of inquiry primarily concerned with meeting established needs, maintaining structural stability, or restoring usefulness. Modern ruins contain much that these aims cannot fully accommodate. What conventional research consigns to damage, failure, and waste kept drawing my attention, until my inquiries gradually moved to the margins of architecture and engineering. I continue to borrow their knowledge, techniques, and methods, but put them toward explorations that need restore no function and may yield no definite result. Some of these attempts are obscure and rough, almost inadmissible by established professional standards; they have nevertheless allowed me to depart for a while from prescribed procedures and seek another way of knowing and answering the ruin along the course of its unfinished transformation.

Faced with the architectural remains left to decay by modern life, I have decided to let making enter a process of disintegration already under way: to build anew by borrowing structures and residual forces from the site, admitting loosening, imbalance, and possible collapse into the work’s construction, so that the object goes on becoming after its form first stands complete. I call this practice, carried out after architecture, “ruinwork.” *Folly* is its first concrete form. “Ruinwright” is the identity of intervention I have gradually found for myself through years of entering, surveying, and building among ruins. It corresponds to no established professional qualification and is difficult to contain within any single discipline. It is closer to a role I have taken on as an independent researcher, working across art, architecture, and engineering. Fantasies with no place in conventional construction can at last be tested against the surviving space. My research and the things I make have found a definite point of contact here; only here have I dared to call myself a *wright*, continuing to discover a craft that still grows wild among ruins.

I have also chosen to set down these reflections as a free-ranging exploratory essay—an *ensaio*. The *Manifesto* does not seek the format of an academic article or a formal research report. Notes taken while surveying sites, studies across disciplines, and personal sensation are allowed to cross as the thought unfolds, so that the writing itself becomes an intellectual expedition still in progress. For now I put these fragments into words as one might assemble a provisional structure in a ruin, allowing scattered experiences to touch one another while leaving room for revision. The questions I have yet to answer will be reasons to enter the ruins again.

# Tenets of the Ruinwright

The “Tenets of the Ruinwright” arose through repeated building on site, and through the testing and gradual revision of initial ideas as materials and spaces underwent actual change. They follow the experience of entering a site and building within it: from first looking upon the remains and stepping inside, through the mechanical relations exposed by fractured materials, to the resonance of collapsing structures and the recording of time. These accumulated encounters eventually lead ruinwork to a question it cannot evade: knowing that every creation will disintegrate, why do we continue to build? Some tenets have been tempered through specific working methods; others remain judgments awaiting further trials. I set them down as tenets to leave a measure that future interventions can consult repeatedly, and revise whenever necessary.

## First Tenet: Ruin Lexicology

As second nature expands beyond the limits of individual perception and understanding, the everyday spaces organized by buildings and facilities become part of our lives before we truly understand their interiors. They even prescribe the spatial experiences we have come to regard as self-evident. We know what these spaces look like and what they are used for, but rarely understand the structures behind their walls and shells, or the complex rules that keep them operating. In time, we mistake familiarity for understanding. Only when the apparent solidity of a building fractures in ruin do the limits of the order that has long governed inside and outside begin to appear beneath its once-intact exterior. Through the exposed openings, we can enter and, among remaining structures and traces of operation, recognize anew a space we had taken entirely for granted.

Upon entering unfamiliar remains, their former names and scattered historical accounts can still identify what they once were, but they cannot explain what is happening there now. If we take a partial view and the recovery of past events for a complete understanding of the ruin, we turn it into an object: a historical relic imprisoned in memory’s “past tense,” flattened into a safe, static image that conceals the material weight still being borne, sinking, and breaking apart. Ruin lexicology attempts to return our gaze to the ruin’s “present progressive,” confronting the bare physical reality of the building: whether it continues to bear a load, has begun to tilt, or has already been pierced by roots. When the formula “former name + ruin” can no longer account for these continually changing differences, new names formed from the site itself become a starting point for rebuilding our understanding.[^7]

To locate this disintegration while it is happening, one must first confront the weightlessness felt when ordinary assumptions about architectural space fail altogether. When a building betrays its promise of shelter, walls and floors that once seemed firm may conceal danger. Familiar routes are cut off by rubble; neither the load beneath one’s feet nor the supports overhead inspire confidence. To stand among them is to be cast into wilderness in the dark. Yet it is in this perilous wasteland, through long wandering and close inspection, that the Ruinwright gradually tempers a pair of “wasteland eyes”: an ability to discern signs of instability hidden within the remains from cracks, rust, and erosion, and to remain almost instinctively alert to changes still to come. Sensibility repeatedly honed in dangerous places allows the Ruinwright to look beyond a building’s former appearance and purpose, to perceive the forces its surviving structure is bearing now, and the disintegration drawing near.[^8]

Through repeated observation and recording, the subtle differences in loading produced by collapse become clearer. New words are drawn from among the rubble and gathered into a “ruin lexicon”—an index of lexical elements recording the material conditions of remains and their evolution. Four kinds of elements overlap within it: terrain, architecture, physical state, and natural transformation. A site can thus receive a new name through its present spatial relations and material changes. The lexicon gradually assumes something of the rigor of natural-history classification, seeking to record with measured precision the ever-changing and irreproducible scenes of disintegration. Yet naming cannot fix a ruin in place; the vocabulary, too, must be revised as the site evolves. Ruin lexicology reconnects remains scattered across the world, allowing traces that resist ordinary spatial categories to disclose their relations through a growing vocabulary, until they form an expanding atlas of the world’s ruins.

## Second Tenet: An Archaeology of Things Close at Hand

When a building loses its function and maintenance, it does not immediately become nameless matter. In modern ruins this predicament is especially stark: destruction reaches things close to our own lives, and objects not yet old fall out of everyday use. The functions and signs that once belonged together begin to separate. Some objects retain a functional structure, although their original use can no longer be identified; others preserve only a name or recognizable form, while the practices to which they belonged have disappeared.[^9] Yet the patina and wear left by daily life remain, calling forth a familiarity we can readily recognize without necessarily possessing the memories from which it arose. These things close at hand hang awkwardly between “everyday refuse” and “cultural heritage.” Before they have had time to become ancient, they have lost the context of life that once made them self-explanatory. They belong to a past not far removed from ours, but forgetting has already made it necessary to approach that recent life almost as an archaeologist would.[^10]

![Figure](manifesto-423-assets/fig-04-a.jpg)

@caption Fig. 4. Things close at hand: ferns growing through the cotton wadding and foam of an abandoned chair in a deserted corridor. Photograph by the author, 2026; *Ruin Atlas: Relic Archive*, ruin-archive.site.

The Ruinwright examines these remains with the cool gaze of “archaeology,” asking how things once intimately bound to daily life came to be abandoned. Lives that seemed close at hand must now be traced through surviving scratches and wear. Even when an object’s appearance is familiar, we may no longer recognize its use, much less recover the full experience of those who lived with it from scattered evidence. Forgetting has made objects that are scarcely old appear strange, while the severed ties between function and sign have turned what once went without saying into a history that must be excavated. The respect inherent in this gaze comes from bearing clear-eyed witness to incompleteness: acknowledging that lives now withdrawn truly took place, and that some part of them remains beyond our reach—deserving, before it disappears from memory altogether, to be regarded with gravity.

The Ruinwright may continue to build within surviving structures, even reconnect components long broken apart, but cannot thereby restore the lost connections between objects and the lives once lived with them. If, with limited knowledge and even the best intentions, we presume to fill gaps in the story that the evidence cannot resolve, the dense silence of the remains may be covered by facile sentiment. Ruinwork therefore restrains the impulse to reconstruct former lives, returning the work to the very breaks in narrative and matter, where surviving forms and scars may surface again. Deep within the ruin it suspends only a “spatiotemporal anchor,” a point at which one might pause and, through the remaining body, attend once more to those who built, used, cherished, and eventually departed from these things.

## Third Tenet: Borrowing the Ruin

When a building is intact and strong, human beings habitually domesticate nature’s inconstancy through boundaries and technology, bringing it down to the scale of a garden in which to dwell and take pleasure. Even mountains and rivers far beyond the grounds can be drawn through the window by means of “borrowed scenery,” becoming views within a human-made order. In such leisurely contemplation, people also admire their own power to define space and hold nature at bay, as though landscapes once encountered with awe had finally been brought within human measure. Yet this composure depends upon architectural shelter and continual maintenance. As the structures maintaining the boundary begin to break, what was framed beyond the window enters along the cracks. Rain wears away the walls, roots pry apart the masonry, and nature—once regarded as scenery—begins to occupy and rewrite the garden from within.

“Borrowing the ruin” arises from this reversal. The Ruinwright reads the remains by following nature’s return into space, borrowing the very force that garden order once sought to tame and exclude, and turning it upon architecture and the human will behind it. Through the fractured frame, the limitations of layered histories and ambitions become visible. Those who raised everything aloft have gone, but the surviving members still carry loads; their resistance to gravity has not ceased, as though a fall were suspended halfway to the ground. Here the Ruinwright seems, for a moment, to borrow nature’s inconstant gaze, looking along erosion’s openings at the finite nature of works that once proclaimed themselves imperishable.

This way of looking eventually enters the act of building. To “borrow” means that ruinwork must arise from structures, remaining materials, and ecological relations already present at the site. Just as guerrilla gardening finds usable ground and nutrients in neglected plots, the Ruinwright searches discarded bricks, cracked concrete, and remaining supports for forces not yet exhausted. Making begins by “borrowing matter” and arrives at “borrowing force”: responding to each ruin’s particular terrain and surviving form, making use of the existing load-bearing relations among fragments, and allowing openings made by natural erosion to participate in the formation of the work. Material conditions continually constrain and revise the initial conception, so that the new creation attaches itself to the ruin’s unfinished transformation. *Folly* takes form through such borrowing: it grows along the fissures of an order that has ceased to work, then borrows the force of nature’s continued incursion to carry the garden itself toward its final deconstruction.[^11]

![Figure](manifesto-423-assets/fig-05-a.jpg)

@caption Fig. 5. Borrowing the ruin: an intervention in a guerrilla-grown plot, attached to the structure of an existing pool. Photograph by the author, 2025; construction record of *Folly I*; Ruinwork Mechanics Archive, ruin-archive.site/mechanics.html#heart-ventricle-structure/1.

To borrow is also, inevitably, to return. The first return in *Folly* takes place in its own deconstruction: spaces once bounded and domesticated by human beings gradually pass back to rain, plants, and time as the garden’s structures loosen and fall apart. The second return belongs to the Ruinwright. We borrow the remains’ materials and residual forces to build within them, but this gives us neither possession of the ruin nor the right to supply, from our limited knowledge, the missing histories of those who are absent. As this period of borrowing draws to a close, the Ruinwright must answer for the consequences of intervention and then make a dignified departure, neither covering the remains’ scars with a new order nor deciding their fate in the name of art. The work’s further transformations are yielded to nature; the space bearing its unfinished histories is returned, at last, to the ruin itself.

## Fourth Tenet: Brutal Materiality

Modern ruins often present themselves as bare concrete frames. The exterior finishes that once concealed the interior and softened its edges deteriorate and peel away under weathering, until little remains but the rough skeleton used to bear loads and divide space. This irreversible stripping is like tearing the deceptive skin from “second nature,” exposing the construction and material weight once hidden beneath a building’s intact appearance. Ruination thus produces an aesthetic appearance resembling “Brutalism,” though this exposure was not deliberately arranged as architectural expression. Rather, as maintenance withdraws, the building involuntarily reveals the physical reality of its subjection to gravity, corrosion, and finite material life.[^12]

A powerful modernist impulse remains embedded in those exposed structures. With increasingly precise structural calculations and synthetic materials, human beings came to believe themselves capable of mastering space, driving buildings ever higher and extending them across wider tracts of land. Behind this achievement of technical reason lies an older, more elemental ambition: to conquer space through human-made works, gathering nature’s uncertainty within the limits of a designed order. Those towering concrete skeletons are at once achievements of modern construction and solidified expressions of humanity’s desire to claim space and impose its will upon the earth.

Yet as the buildings bearing that ambition become ruins, another kind of wildness begins to emerge from within. Their precisely calculated frames remain standing, while the connections that sustained their stability gradually loosen under erosion. The legacy of advanced technology and the most primitive, blind physical forces meet violently in the same wounded body, lending the remains a wildness difficult to tame. The “brutality” of modern ruins changes direction in this encounter: the will to conquer once fixed within the structure meets matter’s irreducible weight and finite life. It is this actual materiality, breaking through the interior of human-made order, that I call “brutal materiality”—the exposed flesh left when the power of control begins to retreat. Reason has not ceased to operate within the ruin; it can simply no longer place itself above the reality of matter.

Ruinwork accordingly shifts its gaze from a brutal appearance to the material struggle still unfolding beneath it. Rather than using a deliberately rough form to symbolize force, the work allows its own structure to bear weight, displacement, and loss, exposing the traces of its making, loading, and eventual decline. Here “brutality” arises from material changes that no predetermined form can fully contain. Where do the true limits of materials once hailed as modern achievements lie, and where will they resist or yield? Drawings and calculations can estimate what they bear, but cannot exhaust the effects of time and erosion. When a structure departs from its former equilibrium, the forces once precisely arranged do not vanish with the loosening of order. They continue through surviving connections and newly opened breaks, driving a long disintegration toward the unknown.

## Fifth Tenet: The Force-Flow View

From the moment a building is completed, its immense material weight is organized into relations of mutual support and restraint. Masonry is laid course upon course; tensioned cables hold members in place. The forces enlisted to fix space in position do not fall to zero when a building ceases to be used. Even remains on the verge of collapse may still contain loads and tensions not yet released, sustaining their precarious stance. Traces of structural decay resemble a set of pointers leading back toward their source: by following spreading cracks and loosening connections, we can retrace how loads were once carried and where support gradually gave way. Erosion and material fatigue make the timing of fracture difficult to foresee, while the conditions that enable instability have often developed throughout construction, use, and abandonment. The ruin’s destruction thus exposes a chain of causes that has not yet ended. Forces once used to hold space in place continue to seek paths of release and redistribution within the remains.

Collapse within a ruin is seldom confined to a single isolated member. Failure at one connection may draw other parts into motion; fragments falling with a crash may unexpectedly come to rest against, across, or suspended from one another, forming a broken but still operative system of load transfer. From this the Ruinwright establishes a “force-flow view”: fractured walls, hanging beams, and leaning columns are no longer regarded as motionless dead things. They continue to carry weight through surviving supports, their mutual loading altered as cracks spread and members move. Even when the remains reach a new momentary balance, the tension held in place may not have dispersed. Countless connections and cracks spreading beneath an apparently quiet surface have already inscribed the long process of destruction within the structure.

@caption Fig. 7. Force-flow view: the tension cable of a newly erected antenna tower passes through an old factory wall, which fractures along the line of loading. Photograph by the author, 2024; Ruinwork Mechanics Archive, ruin-archive.site/mechanics.html#tower-antenna-array/1.

Yet the unresolved tension within the remains also calls us back toward histories still without an explanation. Why did the ideals and ambitions that once supported a vast building lose the power to sustain it? Did a fracture originate in a defect embedded during construction, or in the consequences of neglect and long exposure to weather? Choices, mistakes, and contingencies belonging to the past have already exacted material costs, although the remaining evidence may not allow us to assign their causes fully. The ruin therefore holds another kind of tension in suspension: an event is over, yet its consequences persist, and questions of causation and responsibility not yet settled remain in the present along with its surviving form. Structures not yet fallen reach back toward construction and abandonment at one end, and toward a collapse not yet arrived at the other.

Ruinwork attempts to bring both tensions into the work. As a mechanical transmission device is connected to remaining load-bearing relations, subtle deformations and vibrations are amplified through new links, giving forces concealed within the structure a form that can be seen and heard. Ruinwork borrows actual material tension and uses it to enact historical chains of cause and consequence that remain unsettled. The effects of former ambitions, mistakes, and abandonment gain palpable weight again in a creation that bears a load, trembles, and finally breaks apart. But this connection itself changes the original distribution of forces. The Ruinwright appears to arrange fragments, while already deeply entangled in the same web of causation: new supports and loads may alter local tensions, and an accidental loosening may send the work toward an outcome beyond the design. The forces borrowed by ruinwork will in turn act upon the work itself, making the creation that sought to trace destruction part of destruction’s ongoing course.[^13]

## Sixth Tenet: The Destructive Test Body

Beneath second nature’s appearance of stability, even permanence, damage, demolition, and renewal are often treated as a perfectly ordinary metabolism. We have grown accustomed to the departure of things around us, scarcely finding time to feel how a building that once sustained lives actually loses its form. This numbness arises from a mismatch of perceptual scales: fatigue and damage within materials are often too minute and prolonged for the eye to follow, while a sudden, violent collapse can release enough physical energy to crush a human body. Between “imperceptible small changes” and “catastrophic changes we cannot withstand,” we gradually lose our bodily sense of the lifespan of buildings and the forces of their destruction. The “destructive test body” is born from this gap. It reorganizes key conditions from a building’s long passage into ruin, accelerating otherwise hidden modes of failure within a responsive material “body,” so that destruction can again be felt on a scale accessible to the eyes and nerves.

Like an automobile crash test or a product endurance test, the destructive test body brings damage and failure into a deliberately arranged experimental process. Such tests ordinarily examine load limits and the results of damage in order to improve the next design. The Ruinwright borrows the method but directs its purpose toward the felt experience of destruction itself. From the remains, the test body extracts materials, joints, masses, and loading relations relevant to failure, reconstructing them as a body built expressly to be destroyed. Displacement markers, strain gauges, and vibration sensors serve as “prosthetic senses” attached to it, approaching changes too hazardous for human bodies and turning deformation and vibration into observable traces. Through its own fractures, the test body makes what had been abstracted into loss and obsolescence appear again as actual weight, sound, and material injury.[^14]

We require a surrogate outside our own bodies precisely because, within second nature, we have learned to rely on calculation, maintenance, and renewal to prevent failure, keeping the possibility of order’s collapse beyond everyday experience. We must therefore borrow the same reason once employed to preserve stability and use it to build a body capable of bearing destruction. Just as the cultural figure of the “android” has allowed human beings to question the boundary of “what it means to be human,” the Ruinwright uses a body constructed according to architecture’s own logic to test how far the order supporting space can reach. Reason makes it stand through calculation, then employs calculation again to drive it toward the threshold of collapse. As joints loosen and form loses balance, forces once marshaled to sustain the structure gradually take part in its disintegration. When the reason that made order possible exposes, by its own hand, that order cannot endure forever, the test body becomes a cruel mirror: in its shattered remains we confront the limits that human-made things and human subjects alike cannot surpass.

Ruinwork carries this logic of the test body into *Folly*, incorporating “becoming a ruin” into the work’s structure from the first stages of design. When the anticipated loosening finally occurs, fragments do not necessarily fall along the directions set out on the drawing; new points of contact and support may arise by chance. Rain and wind continue to enter through broken edges, and life may return to abandoned fissures over a longer span of time. Such unscored changes allow an experiment originally directed toward destruction to give rise, in its disintegration, to forms no one expected. A strange, cruel beauty emerges—not from destruction itself, but from contingencies and living things granted room to appear as the former order comes undone. Reason carefully constructs the test body, yet as it disintegrates, the body gradually slips beyond the maker’s complete command of what will follow. Vibrations and lingering resonances released from within its structure let us feel force at close range and, at the same time, inhabit a stretch of real time opened by destruction.

## Seventh Tenet: Return to the Suspended Realm

Modern ruins resemble hollow bodies whose souls have died before their flesh: elaborate spatial structures once carefully arranged for human use, deprived of guidance, light, and inhabitants, gradually become dark, unfamiliar labyrinths. A door no longer reliably marks an entrance; a corridor offers no assurance of arrival. Broken partitions and stairs ending in midair quietly betray architecture’s old promise of shelter and safety. These remains cannot restore the vanished whole; their severed limbs instead mark the absence of people with disturbing clarity. Wandering among them, one encounters “residual structures” that have lost their assigned use yet still bear weight. As the spatial order organized around human movement comes loose, gravity, corrosion, and the relations between surviving members begin to shape the space more directly. These structures seem to seize the initiative, giving the ruin a posthuman condition in which human beings are no longer its center.[^12]

A ruin evolving on its own exerts a compelling, almost oppressive attraction. It has slipped the measures of time once given to it by human activity and yielded to the prolonged transformations of nature, revealing itself at a pace that neither our lives nor our senses can readily grasp. Traces of former habitation capture the eye, yet a long, unanswering silence presses back upon the viewer and unsettles the old boundary between person and space. The building no longer welcomes us into a place prepared for our needs. The “central position” humanity once claimed is, at the scale of a ruin, only an interval temporarily left vacant by time; ornament survives upon the walls while cracks and plants continue to redraw the boundaries of rooms, silently absorbing human activity into the site.[^13] The former spatial whole can scarcely be reconstructed, and its future form cannot be drawn in advance. This land, still bearing human-made outlines, remains suspended between past habitation and present abandonment even as nature writes over it. The ruin’s “liminality” appears in this condition: unable fully to return to what it was, and not yet arrived at a determined destination.[^14]

Ruinwork joins scattered fragments again within this field, drawing the gaze back toward a place deprived of its former destination. They still carry marks of the past and forces not yet exhausted, though they can no longer be fitted into a living context of use. They are assembled into an unfamiliar body, held in a strange and precarious balance by new relations of contact and support. The work cannot recover the fragments’ previous functions or restore the lives that have left the ruin. It never promises permanence, and even its eventual destruction has not been prescribed as a fixed shape. We cannot know what lies beyond the future, yet still wish to build again with these remaining forces, attempting through an object destined to change further to regain a sense of the ruin.

The Ruinwright spends the energy of making within this place and then “sacrifices” the work to the ruin, “exiling” it to time. At first the assembled body resembles a foreign object driven awkwardly into the site. But once it is placed within the remains’ unfinished decay, new fractures become entangled with existing scars, and the clear boundary between work and site begins to disappear. Eventually it becomes a “spatial monster,” difficult to separate from the place itself. Its components remain recognizable as elements of a building, yet their relations have imperceptibly come apart; the better we know their former uses, the more unsettling this body appears.[^15] The fragments still retain some warmth from before their destruction, while the newly standing form has already entered changes whose ending cannot be arranged in advance. Through observation and waiting, the Ruinwright experiences the work slipping away from his control, as a time no longer measured by human life surrounds the senses and forces him to recognize a familiar place anew. As this alien body joins the ruin’s time through its own expenditure, the person who still tries to read the site through its former coordinates becomes the most untimely intruder of all. The “suspended realm” appears in this return.

## Eighth Tenet: Dissipation into Sound

The ruin’s most immediate image is the fragment, yet its most intimate experience often arrives through sound. While a building is inhabited and used, faint vibrations at its joints are usually masked by everyday noise, and the structure is maintained in an almost solid silence. Then a dull crack comes from deep within a load-bearing member expected to endure, and the components that quietly carried weight betray their imbalance through an unfamiliar noise. It departs from our expectation that a building should remain “silent.” The source may lie beyond sight, while sound reaches us before the form has begun to fall, loosening our confidence in shelter and safety. Through the ear comes an unease of “civilizational dissonance”: the psychological defense maintained for so long by second nature breaks down before the building itself collapses.

This unease arises from material transformations still unfolding within the ruin. Members rub together under compression and strike each other as joints loosen; accumulated tension is released in a brief vibration when something splits. Dripping water and wind passing through empty rooms leave audible traces of corrosion and decay. Some sounds break suddenly out of long silence; others return from an unseen corner, making it difficult to judge whether a small displacement has occurred or a larger failure is approaching. The ruined space holds us at a distance through the threat of collapse, yet draws our attention and hearing through noises whose origins elude us. We can still recognize wind, drops of water, and the friction of materials, but they come from corridors and rooms that no longer answer to human life. Their familiarity deepens the strangeness; aware of possible danger, the passerby still cannot help wondering where these lingering sounds begin.

A fracture lasts only an instant, but leaves behind a long period of waiting. Following its echo, the listener searches for the point at which a joint may have loosened. When it dies away and silence returns, one begins to wonder when and where the next sound will emerge. These accidental tremors and prolonged silences provide no certainty that the changes within the structure have settled. Each brief noise may be the prelude to another break. In the alternation of sound and quiet, the ruin’s rhythm gradually appears, drawing people to stop repeatedly and pursue transformations that the visible form has not yet disclosed.

Ruinwork turns this impulse to follow sound back into the sounding construction of *Folly*. Borrowing forces not yet spent within the remains, the work makes the movement, friction, and fracture of fragments into conditions of sound, so that stresses once imperceptible within materials become audible as its own structure loosens and loses balance. The Ruinwright may arrange loads and transmission, yet cannot entirely decide what sound each rub, impact, or fracture will finally make. Some tension gathers at a joint over a long period before a sudden release; elsewhere a falling member unexpectedly finds support, producing another sequence of unplanned resonance. The work thus entrusts disintegration to its own performance. Every vibration consumes a little more of the structure, and once the sound has faded, the form can no longer return to its earlier place. The echoes that once summoned the listener from deep within the ruin now take place again through this continually expended creation.

## Ninth Tenet: The Blueprint Becomes a Score

From its first drawn line, ruinwork binds the motion of fragments to the sounds of structural fracture. Lines marking anchor points, loads, and suspensions originally direct an object to stand according to prescribed relations. Yet because loosening and falling are already admitted into its design, the forces holding its shape also become the forces that will one day undo it. Anchor points, axes, and dimensions that once certified stability begin at the same time to point toward possible future displacements and breaks. Even before a single note is written upon the drawing, the conditions of sound have been placed inside the blueprint along lines already full of tension.

Conventional plans and elevations can mark the geometry of a building at one moment, but a fixed drawing alone can scarcely record the long displacements, wear, and fractures that follow. For an object destined to go on disintegrating, completion is merely a passing state. To follow its transformation in time, the drawing must make room for accumulating traces and new marks. As the events on site escape prediction, the drawing gradually sheds its original authority to prescribe: an elevation still measures position, but may also become the starting point of a record of subsidence; a load-bearing axis shows how weight is held, but also where movement may begin when its connections loosen. Lines first drawn to fix a form gradually serve to follow the places from which friction, impact, and fracture might sound. Once cold geometry begins to mark the thresholds of possible noises, the blueprint crosses beyond engineering and becomes a graphic score of the rhythms of destruction.[^16]

The blueprint can arrange the conditions in which sounds might occur, but it cannot determine the moment when they will be heard. Fragments need not fall along their proposed paths; an unforeseen obstruction may postpone an impact indefinitely, while a noise may arise from a joint no one thought to watch. The Ruinwright must redraw according to the displacements, breaks, and sources actually encountered on site. The original lines remain, though they can no longer account for the wreckage before the eyes. Thus the blueprint slowly passes from a director of movement into a record revised as the site itself changes.

Faced with an increasingly crowded drawing, the Ruinwright begins to bring into the score the noise and disorder that established signs cannot wholly contain. The unclassifiable texture of a breaking sound, the spreading shapes of dust and rust, and irregular edges left by water are transcribed as wandering marks or written over the original trajectories in layer upon layer. Engineering signs become stained, twisted, and deformed through repeated drawing. The lines once laid down for construction remain on the paper, but gradually lose their former purpose beneath these successive inscriptions.[^17] Chance and disorder, once excluded from engineering drawings, find a place upon the page.

## Tenth Tenet: The Meaning of Futility

Ruinwork retains the methods of measurement, design, and construction used in architecture and engineering, yet its motives lead it into an almost solitary territory. Often I find myself alone among the remains, working quietly with the changes of material and site. On entering the wreckage, the chains of efficiency that govern ordinary life begin to loosen. There is seldom a client’s brief here, nor a clear standard by which expenditure and return can be weighed; attention contracts toward the physical reality of the ruin itself. Building no longer aims to provide a use or secure a reward. It gives a fleeting process of destruction a definite form, leading it toward a carefully composed overturning. The work may collapse through a sudden loss of balance or give way gradually under corrosion and wear, yet the seed of destruction was planted on the first day of work. All this cultivation of ruinwork does is borrow the forces that will ultimately undo the structure and carry it toward a threshold from which it cannot readily return.

In a society accustomed to judging labor by what survives and what it produces, devoting oneself to a work destined to perish seems profoundly unprofitable, almost an absurd exercise in futility. Such measures quietly favor the sound, complete, and serviceable, while consigning fracture, weathering, and disappearance to “failure.” Ruinwork lingers in a fissure within this system of value: it listens for the material reality exposed when an object loses its assigned use, and follows the loads and tensions still held by a structure on the verge of failure. Things built with care pass, once completed, beyond the maker’s control, surrendering to time the labor that went into them. Even where endurance and output offer no recognition, the Ruinwright is willing to finish the building with care; the actual years and bodily effort consumed by that labor lie beyond the repayment of any judgment of value.

The certainty of the work’s destruction does nothing to lessen the effort needed to build it. Forms that appear fragmentary must still be raised through the joining, fixing, and loading of materials. An error at a connection can require completed parts to be dismantled, while earlier calculations must be revised again and again as site conditions change. Conventional building calculates toward stability; ruinwork must allow a form to stand for a time while making room for its future loosening and collapse. It tries to hold a delicate balance amid nature’s contingencies, and to let anticipated fractures and displacements occur within chance itself. Longer even than the futility of building a single ruin are the years I have spent traveling among remnants: surveying, measuring, recording, then carrying what I learn into another inquiry that may likewise lead nowhere.

It is as though I had invented for modern ruins a discipline that has no place in the world and then committed myself, with all the care of a craftsperson, to practicing it: building for places no one inhabits, drawing plans for things that will soon be rubble, and spending, little by little, the years that might have gone to other work and other forms of life on a craft without a clear end. Yet the cities beneath our feet were themselves raised by generation after generation of hands, sustained through endless repair, replacement, and rebuilding. If the cities of the future, too, will one day be ruins, where are we to seek an account of the labor and lives exhausted in raising them?[^18] A disintegrating object may leave remains; the years already lived will not return, however carefully the work is preserved.

Yet amid this repeatedly obstructed labor, much of it apparently fruitless, I began to notice another change that I could scarcely name. A connection that held together on paper might turn against my expectation when it met the resistance of real material. I had to put down my tools and look again at constructions I thought I understood, correcting my judgments through dismantling, adjustment, and beginning anew. For years I had tried to discover some elusive significance within ruins. Gradually I came to suspect that what I had been seeking was not simply stored among the remains; through those years of dealing with material, it had entered the very way I came to know things.[^19] Maker and made are thus never wholly apart. In trying to understand the ruin, I have already been changed by the labor itself.

The “meaning of futility” leaves room for labor that cannot be settled in the currency of return. What that labor teaches cannot reimburse the years it has consumed, nor does it make the work’s disappearance reversible. Knowing that what we build must disintegrate, why do we still build? Looking back on the years spent moving among ruins, I find that the question has long been entangled with my own life. The ruin has promised no final answer. I have nevertheless entrusted a portion of my life to this still-unsettled craft. Such labor needs no claim to eternity to justify its existence. Even knowing that the things I have built with my hands will vanish, I am willing to return to this ground without a guarantee and finish the work before me.

## The Boundaries of the Ruinwright

Destruction among modern ruins often bears consequences in the real world that remain unexplained. War, engineering accidents, and misguided decisions leave particular injuries in buildings. In cities renewed at a rapid pace, changing functions and long neglect may also lead to the abandonment of things whose material lives are far from spent. Natural erosion continues to strip away their form, but cannot erase the origins of these choices and consequences. Traces of labor and memories of life that have not yet vanished remain with the wreckage on the same ground. The Ruinwright cannot gather all of this into an abstract spectacle of destruction merely because it is beautiful to look at. Beauty may emerge from the fracture; it cannot replace the question of how the building came to be a ruin.

Ruinwork borrows perception from art and the means of organizing matter from architecture and engineering. It can therefore establish new connections in a ruin and allow its remaining forces to participate in the formation of a work. Yet having entered the site and acquired the ability to rearrange material and draw out meaning, the Ruinwright can no longer regard himself as a mere observer, much less treat that ability as a license to remake the remains at will. An intervention must acknowledge the limits of materials, space, and time that may be borrowed, disturb the place as little as possible under lawful conditions of access and work, and take responsibility for the real consequences of every alteration. A work may continue to grow along the site’s transformations; its maker acquires no right to decide the ruin’s ultimate fate.

The Ruinwright’s own knowledge must be revised repeatedly as well. Lexical elements first used to recognize fragments, calculations that traced forces, and blueprints that registered disintegration all offered precise means of entering a site. Yet as conditions kept changing, each method encountered things it could not accommodate. If provisional measures of observation are elevated into fixed laws capable of explaining every ruin, ruinwork risks building another “second nature” over the remains, allowing its own order to conceal a reality that has yet to settle into form. Names, drawings, and judgments continually corrected on site remind the Ruinwright of the limits of knowledge itself: theory opens a path inward, but gives no one the right to look down upon the place.

Knowing these limits, the Ruinwright continues to enter ruins again and again, correcting what he thinks he knows through surveying, labor, and long waiting. The weight of remains, obstacles in the terrain, and ever-changing structures remind the body of the distances it can reach. Experiences that drawings and images have not captured gradually reveal themselves only in actual proximity. The Tenets end here for now, leaving measures that the site may put to the test once more. After years of building and recognizing, the question turns from the ruins’ structure and history toward the self: what makes me stop again and again for things deprived of their use, and why, amid their continual disappearance, am I still willing to look, feel, and search for beauty?

# Statements of the Ruinwright

The Tenets have now provisionally set out a scale by which to examine how one enters a ruin, recognizes its remains, and borrows forces not yet spent within them. Yet the impulses that bring someone back to the wreckage cannot be fully explained by a method of building. Why do ruins fascinate us? Why, knowing that their disappearance cannot be reversed, do we still wish to look, to feel, and even to build among them? These questions gradually turn the gaze from the ruin toward the person who is looking.

The perception of beauty in ruins may have been the first opening into this long inquiry. The beauty that emerges unexpectedly from a damaged form often reaches the senses before any reasoned judgment. But the same attraction can make another person’s loss into a spectacle, even offering the furtive pleasure of curiosity to a viewer who feels safely above the wreckage and its history. Such a gaze seldom gets beyond the surface of the scene. An aesthetics of ruins cannot stop at admiration for the beauty of destruction. The lives once lived here, the acts of abandonment, and the pain that actually occurred remain bound to the material before us, altering in turn the ways we can stand before it.

In correspondence with the Tenets’ movement outward from practices on site, the statements that follow begin with this first sensation of beauty. As the ruin continually disturbs perception and understanding, they trace the desires concealed within looking, and how those desires take shape in modern life. They arise from years of wandering and building, but also touch a spiritual condition that some others living in modernity may recognize. As the ruin turns the gaze back upon itself, the order that once helped us feel at home begins to disclose hairline fractures.

# The Broken Frame — From the Picturesque to the Sublime

At first, ruins often enter aesthetic vision through the gentle appearance of the “picturesque.” Under the shared erosion of time and nature, an intact building loses its polished surface and moves toward damage, yet among rubble, moss, and mottled weathering it can produce a fleetingly romantic posture. Each time we stop to admire a ruin, the “picturesque beauty” we capture is, in essence, only a composition accidentally frozen within the process of destruction.

## Picturesque Beauty: Bringing the Ruined Scene into the Frame

The “picturesque” seeks scenes fit for a picture amid nature’s transformations, and finds particular appeal in things rough, fragmentary, or resistant to regular form. Faced with bodies that keep changing, we are at once drawn toward forces we cannot master and eager to preserve one beautiful posture that appears by chance. Painting, photography, and the making of gardens have all given these brief encounters a form that can be looked at again. Through the search for and selection of a view, an invisible “frame” begins to take shape. From a relatively secure distance we can contemplate scenes of rupture and sudden change, placing for a moment what we cannot control inside a composition.[^20]

The classical ruins we are accustomed to admiring are already forms preserved by time and by the choices of later generations. The picturesque gaze makes a further selection among these survivors, extracting the moments most agreeable to view. Ruins removed before they could acquire an antique elegance, and the continuing loss of form as old walls fall apart, rarely find their way into the frame. The beauty of ruins familiar to us thus becomes, almost without notice, a carefully selected image of destruction.

Certain decorative buildings made in the likeness of ruins—*follies*—once placed broken walls, the atmosphere of age, and the appearance of natural erosion within European gardens designed for strolling and looking. One need not wait centuries for a real building to decay in order to possess a ruin haloed by time in one’s own grounds. Such deliberately constructed remains condense long destruction into a newly made garden, as though the fugitive beauty of the picturesque could be held indefinitely at the most pleasant instant of viewing.[^21]

Yet to invite a ruin into a garden is also to invite its collapse, shadow, and rotting leaves. Real remains continue to bear their own weight, and natural erosion does not halt to preserve a dignified antique pose. Once the damaged appearance borrowed from a ruin is released from continual trimming and maintenance, existing injuries may continue to change, gradually drawing the garden’s carefully arranged scene away from its original conception.

The frame did not disappear with the classical garden; it survives in modern photography and in a gaze that makes landscapes of ruins. This kind of looking seeks to intervene in a site still disintegrating, compressing a long collapse into a visual slice that can be captured at will, and bestowing an illusory stability upon the remains. When the most picturesque instant comes to stand for the entire experience of a ruin, the depth of space, the weight still carried by the structure, and the changes that escape the lens recede beyond the composition. Destruction seems safely placed inside a picture, removed from time and becoming, leaving an image to which we may return while life outside its frame proceeds undisturbed.[^22]

The ruin, however, does not always submit to the frame. Even where natural erosion is deliberately introduced into surviving structures, no one can long preserve the condition most agreeable to view. A storm or the loosening of a single load-bearing joint can wrench the object out of that carefully chosen present. When the picturesque pose can no longer be held, and the remains once gathered into scenery continue along their fractures toward disappearance, collapse brings the true force of gravity down upon the picture and shatters its stillness.

## The Folly Series: Releasing the Frame

Traditional gardens brought nature into an ordered field of human viewing through enclosure, pruning, and borrowed scenery. In a ruin deprived of maintenance, that relation gradually reverses. Wildness once kept outside the frame enters the building through its cracks, erodes walls and supports built to resist it, and draws spaces made for human passage, dwelling, and contemplation away from their former arrangement.

*Folly* begins with this reversal. The Ruinwright borrows surviving structures and residual forces to build anew, incorporating looseness, imbalance, and eventual disintegration into the work’s construction, so that the attainment of form is only a passing state within its transformations. The work borrows the name of the garden *folly*, but no longer seeks to keep a decorative “corpse of an object” in a pleasing state of picturesque damage. Changes that gardens once repeatedly trimmed and tried to confine within the frame are given space to continue, bringing about a quiet deconstruction of architecture.

To make this possible, the structure must stand for a time while leaving conditions for its future failure. Swaying members, anticipated fractures, and arranged force relations place the possibility of destruction within the object from the beginning. Yet while the Ruinwright can arrange the conditions of fracture, the way materials will finally leave their equilibrium cannot be fully prescribed. An unexpected contact, or a displacement that fails to follow its intended path, may support the work for another moment before later wear alters it again. The object no longer needs to answer indefinitely to its maker’s desire for an intact shape; material and environment carry it toward an ending no one can draw in advance.

When something carefully made by human hands gradually escapes human command through its own becoming, the frame that once held a landscape in place begins to fail. The ruin before us was made through human labor and intention, yet can no longer continue entirely according to those intentions. Its real weight, expenditure, and duration expose a material existence that the maker cannot exhaust through understanding. The attraction and fear awakened by destruction exceed picturesque contemplation, recalling forces vastly greater than our scale and beyond our complete control. An experience of the “sublime” may emerge from this disturbance of sense and knowledge.[^23]

The “Broken Frame” (fig. 15) gives this change a visible form. As the frame itself begins to become a ruin, it loses the capacity to arrest ceaseless change as a static landscape. The scene once enclosed recedes, and the damaged boundary takes its place as the object of attention. Fractures spread through the frame itself, exposing the vulnerable body of what once served to freeze a view.

The breaking of the frame also compels viewers to reconsider their relation to ruins. The distance once maintained outside the picture no longer offers a secure place from which to understand what happens before them. An unfamiliar sound from a support may suddenly reveal that the judgments through which we trusted a space can themselves fail when its structure gives way. The scene preserved by the frame remains available, but the ruin has a longer time in which to continue beyond the image.

## Ruins of the Mind: A Fractured Frame

The Folly Series pushes the viewer toward the leading edge of a loosening frame. The picturesque observer who first lingered before ruins was accustomed to sealing destruction inside the damaged image, preserving the safety of life outside it. That boundary could feel secure because second nature not only assigned order and destination to things; it also marked out for the human being an apparently immovable position as subject within the world.

Only when the cracks in the wall visibly spread beneath one’s feet does collapse reveal itself as a physical violence still moving forward. Once the viewer understands that the same erosion could occur in any familiar place, the sublime in nature breaks through the aesthetic boundary and begins to consume, in reverse, the order by which the viewer understands the world. In this comprehensive loss of ground, the human space on which life depends collapses inside consciousness, while objects once carefully cherished lose their weight in an emptiness that answers no one. Familiar architecture becomes a “lost space.”

As the grand narratives attached to space withdraw, the psychological shelter provided by crowds and everyday life evaporates with them. This becomes a brutal form of “homesickness while still at home” (solastalgia): home remains where it was, yet can no longer coincide with memory. It is familiar enough to be recognized as home, yet too strange to carry the years one did not live through. Here, a person is forced to face histories in which they did not participate and countless possible lives that came to nothing. The absolute solitude of this encounter arises from a real and silent absence: familiar life has left the stage, while the cold space remains.

The inner sublime erupts precisely from this deprivation in which distance can no longer be maintained. Once observer-like mourning is gone, “lost space” forces viewer and wreckage into the same irreversible physical time, confronting us with the fact that we too cannot escape disappearance. Things before us that still possess weight and warmth will eventually decay into unregarded residue on a historical cross-section; when the same fate reaches the self, the pressure of helplessness spreads from the past into the present. Under the void’s pressure on both body and mind, one instinctively clenches the hands, trying to pull from emptiness one last tangible sense of continuing to exist.

## A Mirror of the Modern Mind: The Phantom of the Empty Frame

Some creations become strangely familiar only after they are damaged; while intact, they may have seemed nothing of the kind. Once they entered everyday life with clear functions and complete appearances, their walls and skins concealed their structures and shut off the minute traces of former lives. When their use withdraws and their surfaces peel away, hidden parts, scratches, and wear come into view. Along these fracture planes we discover a belated familiarity: even without sharing their past, we have lived within the same human-made order, subject to the same weight of the body and passage of time. Their intact appearance once let us overlook them; broken, they approach the body again through their actual material weight.

Yet familiarity brings no lasting peace. Rooms and corridors can still be recognized, but the relations of passage that once connected them have been broken. We seek a destination by habitual routes, only to be turned back repeatedly by debris and collapsed walls. Our steps seem always to lead forward, while what we experience accumulates into no definite progress. The time once divided by daily life has also withdrawn, leaving only the slow advance of corrosion. The remaining traces of everyday life offer moments of comfort, then awaken fear and loss amid an emptiness that does not answer. The ruin remains suspended between nearness and estrangement.

This wandering without a place to arrive has a concealed correspondence in the mental life of modern people. When a building falls away from the track of social development, the functions and needs that sustained it disappear, yet its material body remains, bearing loads not yet exhausted. A person who has lost a familiar social identity or a place of belonging may likewise remain subject to habits long internalized. The goals that once directed action have lost their ground, but the demand for progress and achievement goes on working deep within consciousness, urging the person to seek another position even when there is nowhere to go. The social coordinates by which the self was once confirmed begin to fail, while the measure that demands life move endlessly forward remains inside one who no longer belongs.

![Figure](manifesto-423-assets/fig-16-a.jpg)

@caption Fig. 16. Phantom of the Empty Frame: a lost space that still retains the scale of former everyday life. Photograph by the author, 2024.

The liminality of the modern ruin becomes especially vivid in this parallel loss. A building has left its former social function but continues to change in time; a person may lose a familiar identity and still understand the self by standards that no longer apply. We seem suspended in a narrow interval of life: the former order can no longer give us a home, while freedom beyond it has not yet become intelligible. To those who have encountered a similar loss at the edges of modern life, the ruin’s desolation may feel disturbingly familiar. The meanings that once pointed the way gradually vanish, though the inertia of discipline still presses us toward an unknown direction.

The damaged beauty of the “Broken Frame” thus becomes a mirror of the modern mind. As the lost space withdraws its familiar responses, solitude brings into view contradictions usually concealed by social identity and routine. We once knew ourselves through our position in the world and learned to understand our surroundings through relations already arranged for us. When those relations come loose, we begin to see how much of the seemingly self-evident self was formed through years of assuming roles and receiving responses.

When familiar life and identity cease to answer for us, an almost absolute solitude appears in their absence. Beyond the faces gradually shaped to meet the expectations of the world, what remains our own? The rupture in the ruin seems to expose a gap in which familiar social identities no longer meet the reality before our eyes; it does not, however, restore a purer self. The scene inside the picture has vanished, yet the boundaries that once framed it linger in consciousness, so that even an awareness of order’s illusions and limits cannot free us at once from the ways it has shaped us.

We continue to live within second nature, and must sometimes borrow its measures even to understand a world losing certainty. Desires, fears, and impulses hidden by roles and routines emerge again along the cracks of disintegration. The beauty that first drew us toward ruins grows less easy to explain in the midst of this loss of direction.

# Ruin Imagery — Where Desire Goes

Born into modern society, we have inherited ways of judging things through order, efficiency, and use, and internalized them into an almost instinctive gaze. The modern ruin is the break left by that logic. Once value disappears, the utilitarian gaze reaches for purchase and finds none in a remnant from which no profit can be drawn. Yet when “beauty” strikes from the fracture in that instant, we do not simply leave; we continue along the crack, looking for the source of the desire that made us look.

## The Broken Prism: Reading the Fracture Plane

If the mirror of the “Broken Frame” notices a mismatch when the gaze turns back upon itself, the “Broken Prism” looks through that mismatch and begins to reveal how “second nature” refracts a disorderly reality into legible spectra. Ordinarily, the glass that performs the refraction is scarcely noticed. Only once the prism breaks do its actual form, stress, and fragility appear at the fracture. A ruin works in the same way. When the order and maintenance that sustain the surface are cut off, matter reappears as weight, corrosion, and lifespan; the loosening of a single node can expose an entire structural system. By the same logic, a failed building can pull into view the civilizational structure that once sustained it.

The broken prism grants no omniscience. It only lets relations suppressed beneath an intact surface appear at the break. Its beauty therefore remains in a dislocation that is difficult to settle: the whole image collapses, yet the fragments acquire new reflections. Drawn to this reflected light, the viewer encounters not a romantic explanation laid over damage, but a poetry that erupts from the materiality of the break itself.[^23]

Fear of collapse and a cool kind of consolation occur together in this tremor of beauty. As the former city continues to fail, it exposes its material body and reflects the fragility of the order people built. The tension not yet exhausted after destruction cannot be fully accounted for by utilitarian measures, nor can the brilliance that sometimes appears within destruction. The ruin thus reveals the finitude of human-made things in both life and intention: we want meaning and beauty to endure, yet some ideals generate new tragedies in the act of being realized, while others lose their echo when the people or structures that carried them disappear.

## The Ruin Romantic: Tenderness of Fragments

Looking inward along the cracks, the tragedy of modern life slowly comes into view. Some ideals entrusted by their makers to buildings have scarcely had time to be realized before those buildings lose the grounds for their continued care; ways of life that once flourished may also lose their echoes as their inhabitants depart. We hope that meaning and beauty will endure, yet certain ideals give rise to new tragedies as they are realized, while others fall silent when the forms that bore them disappear. When losses carefully concealed finally lie open before us, we still feel tenderness for their damage. At the same time, we cannot deny that an elusive beauty is emerging from those exposed planes.

Such awareness of suffering, together with the experience of beauty, lends the contemplation of ruins a contradiction belonging to modern tragedy.[^26] The losses of the past are not compensated for by the new form of the remains. Yet transformations once suppressed beneath an intact exterior disclose another kind of life after destruction. Fear of collapse may therefore contain an unexpected measure of relief: an apparently invulnerable surface has shown its limits, and abandoned remains continue to grow after the withdrawal of order. We cannot rejoice unreservedly in this beauty, but neither can we deny being moved by it merely because it arises from damage.

This desire, attracted to destruction and unable to keep away from ruins, might be called a “ruin complex.” It is fascinated by time’s unsparing work upon matter and by the strange posture in which decline and new growth are entwined within a single form, giving disappearance a melancholy attraction difficult to resist.[^27] Yet what draws us here is more than curiosity about destruction. The lingering warmth of former days and the ambitions and ideals once placed within a building make us willing to stop for things now deprived of use. This care cannot reverse their disappearance, but still tries to preserve a light not wholly extinguished in the fragments, allowing things forgotten by their age to enter our present once more.

Perhaps this is a romantic spirit peculiar to modern ruins. It knows that fragments cannot restore an era that has passed and expects no complete redemption, yet continues to find beauty in desolation. Ideals once left unfulfilled cross the forms that have unexpectedly grown from fractures today, as though the ruin held at once an unrealized past and a future that may never arrive. They cannot be reassembled into their former whole, but leave imagination innumerable openings not yet prescribed. The Ruin Romantic lingers between destruction and survival, seeking new relations in broken lines of verse and gradually carrying this tenderness for incompletion toward the desire to create again.

## The Bearing of the Fool: A Utopia of Fragments

As the wish to reconstruct withdraws, the ruin remains in a time not yet exhausted. Broken walls have neither returned to life nor reached an end in collapse. Amid the continuing tension of splendor not yet extinguished, unrealized wishes, and destruction belatedly arriving, the ruin wakes from its long, unattended suspension. Materials once placed high by a building’s assigned purpose now lie mixed among rubble; through gaps opened by erosion and new points of support, the possibility of joining them again begins to appear. Things waiting to be awakened have long been gathering within this desolation, developing narratives and bodily forms of their own.

To approach at this point is to find tenderness, curiosity, and the thrill of looking where one perhaps should not, entangled. We search the mingled fragments for what remains concealed. This exploration exceeds common sense and practical ends, letting unrelated pieces form new connections through chance encounters and bringing a long-suppressed desire to create into view. Something like a Surrealist impulse arises here: once rational order ceases to assign things their single proper use, the imagination long held back by habit can link what once had no relation. Following the force sedimented among the fragments, one connects them anew, answering the expectations not yet exhausted within this mixed body and allowing its silent energies to take form again in a present encounter.[^28]

Creation gradually departs here from the purposes of repair and utility. The Ruinwright need not make fragments perform their former duties or supply a justification for the destruction that has already occurred. The ruin has undergone an irreversible dismantling; materials and forces once constrained within an intact building gain, as the old order loosens, the possibility of being understood and used otherwise. Even an impulse to destroy established forms may change direction in this making. There is no need to break what is still whole; the fractures already present in the remains can turn a resistance to prescribed form into the wish to build again. Fantasies without a place in conventional architecture can at last meet the test of matter in surviving space.

We cannot, in the end, return to a primordial world untouched by civilization. Even if everyday rules briefly lose their force, the memories, experiences, and habits guiding desire have long become part of life. This does not render the impulse to create unreal. Among abandoned works we may still recognize curiosity, tenderness, and the pursuit of beauty not yet spent within ourselves. Though desire has been shaped by second nature, it can continue to grow along a path not previously laid out when order grows loose. Perhaps a return to what is genuine consists in this willingness, after admitting we can never stand outside the world, to meet feelings long set aside and give them a material body with our own hands.

For this making, still unfolding deep among the remains, the Ruinwright enters under the aspect of the “Fool.” Here the Fool sets aside calculations of results and rewards, taking up materials long judged useless with an almost innocent stubbornness. *Folly* becomes the material form of this role: the desire to build despite knowing that form cannot last is given a brief body within the ruin.[^29] This lucid, single-minded devotion requires no withdrawal from the world. Knowing that the form cannot resist time, the Fool nonetheless insists that something which did not exist before should stand for a little while, and makes it with care and dignity.

The Fool walks toward the cliff so that potential is not spent in advance by existing experience and conclusions. In this desolation from which order has departed, on a stage that will vanish in an instant, the desire to look upon ruins assumes a body and raises, in return, something worthy of affection. Fragments can neither restore a lost age nor promise that a true utopia will arrive. Yet dispersed splendor and old aspirations may briefly answer one another in an act of folly unmeasured by profit and loss. The Fool’s step upon the ruin remains light—not through ignorance, but through the clear knowledge that nothing beneath it is guaranteed, coupled with the memory of the weight once borne by the stones. The Ruinwright continues to play this role on ground that offers no support, bringing the ruin’s silent remains into a creation whose performance has yet to begin.[^30]

# Final Resonance — Playing After the Curtain Falls

Second nature resembles a tightly run theater in which people and things keep to assigned scripts. But when the chain of order breaks, abandoned ruins become the backstage left behind after the theater withdraws. The larger drama can no longer assign each thing its place; functions and identities lose their settings. The ruin does not hurry to provide a new answer. It simply exposes the wreckage left by the collapse of order as it is—a chaotic place not yet classified or gathered back under reason, where everything retains an unnamed, almost primordial appearance.[^28]

In this groundless field of freedom, the Ruinwright finds a stage on which to release a “madness” of their own. This madness is not mindless destruction. In the backstage of failed order, it means deliberately taking on the role of a fool who does not calculate gain or loss, resisting second nature’s instinct to annex the unknown and smooth away strangeness. The Ruinwright delays rational judgment so that the loss of control, fear, and sublimity awakened by the unknown can remain for a while. In that brief departure from order, we can draw closer to a chaos that has not yet taken shape and slowly build our own understanding and rules through sustained negotiation with the ruin. The Folly Series becomes, within this lucid madness, a material projection through which the impulse toward ruination can be released.[^29]

## Postdramatic Space: Backstage

Many modern ruins abandoned in the wake of development and expansion remain in the afterwash of the wave called “progress.” Their time appears to have stopped only because they have fallen out of the viewpoint that races along with that wave. Seen from the ruin itself, however, the stranded wreckage was never carried forward by progress. The speed and inertia of the passing wave did not take it away; they tore it into damaged bodies. Here, the ruin continues to change. Aftershocks and consequences that did not end when the wave receded replace the image of life before abandonment and become the narrative subject of the space in the present.

Within this derailed time, modern remnants retain an unfinished disintegration. Past upheaval lodges in fractured surfaces, while unstable residual structures continue to push the event into the future. Among walls and wreckage stranded by the current of the age, a distinctive “postdramatic” condition emerges: there is no script prescribing the future, and no attempt to restage the past. Residual stress and invasive natural forces entangle fragments in moment-to-moment change, producing a stage and a site-specific aesthetics that belong to the ruin itself.[^30]

If a ruin is the form architecture leaves at the far end of time, the Folly Series writes that endpoint into its own construction in advance and enters it through self-disintegration. The work carries a tendency to dismantle the form of its own death, and in doing so begins to see from the position of a “symbolic remainder.” Built from the signs of civilization, it gradually strips off that skin as it breaks apart and remains in civilization’s interior as a body that has lost civilization’s appearance, tearing open the once-seamless surface of symbolic order. The viewer, placed within this act of looking, can likewise step partly outside the narrative of order and watch how civilization moves from construction toward dissolution.[^31]

## Becoming an Instrument through Disintegration: A Deconstructive Performance Method

If “making no sound” is itself an instruction of order, a building during ordinary habitation resembles an actor required to “remain silent.” We are accustomed to the steadiness of floors beneath our feet and beams at our sides, as though these immense material bodies had only to carry weight without speaking. Yet when a dull rubbing or a crack from deep within a support suddenly becomes audible, the building leaves its role as “background scenery.” Unfamiliar sounds within the ruin unravel the tacit pact of silence, abruptly touching our trust in shelter and safety.

The building’s former silence was only the long restraint placed by architectural order upon vibration and displacement. Through one abnormal sound after another, the structure enters the condition of becoming a ruin. Ruinwork takes that departure from order apart once more, shaping it through disintegration into a “collapsing instrument.” A conventional musical instrument is, at root, silent material organized into stable relations, maintained in order to produce recognizable tones. The ruin, by contrast, comes into its accidental musicality through the absence of that order and through matter that was never absolutely silent. In the abandoned backstage, the vibrations of its own material body become apparent. Collapse itself becomes the instrument’s true sonority.

To restage the stripping away of order and the release of sound on site, the Ruinwright reconnects scattered fragments through added transmission structures, drawing their abnormal sounds into the work so that motion and failure become audible events. Within a mechanical arrangement that appears rationally designed, the sounds remain harsh and disordered. The absurdity disclosed by a broken order receives, for the first time, a physical impression that can be heard directly. Its score no longer obeys civilization’s intentions; the sounds expose the death of architecture and open onto a deeper dismantling of the nature of order.

## From Detuning to Return: The Note That Burns Itself Out

Seen again along this fracture, the very word “instrument” contains a double discipline and arrangement. Music, through the habits of listening, makes material vibration enter recognizable timbres and rhythms; the instrument keeps such vibrations within relations that can be repeated. When the order holding those relations begins to loosen and fail, familiar pitch and tone may depart from expectation. An instrument out of tune still sounds, but can no longer answer the experience by which we recognized it.

So, too, with the ruin. If a building can be imagined as an instrument continually required to remain silent, the strange sounds it gradually makes as a ruin are a performance that never stops falling out of tune. To hear a scrape as the failure of order, or a vibration that defeats expectation as disturbing noise, is still to depend in some measure upon the memory of an intact building. These sounds are neither the bustle that once surrounded us nor the peaceful stillness of home. Nature has composed no other correct score for architecture. It changes the positions and connections of components until sounds once compliant with ordinary listening reveal qualities that their old context can no longer contain.

Such sound does not pass, the instant it departs from listening habits, into an absolute authenticity beyond language and experience. We still instinctively seek its source and hear danger in the signs of failure precisely because we recognize the familiar process of “detuning”: sound crosses subtle boundaries maintained by listening habits and rules of musical order. Through weathering, the ruin returns toward the physical ground of its materials, and makes us suddenly aware that the building we treated as a fixed form is escaping human restraint and returning toward a more natural material state.[^34] When social function and cultural narrative withdraw, the institutional order that once arranged materials breaks apart, but its components have not ceased to act. Under gravity and erosion they continue to collide, vibrate, and fracture, releasing sounds no longer conducted.

The ruin’s performance as a “collapsing instrument” therefore remains full of suspense. A sound may come from a joint that has resisted loosening for a long time, or erupt after prolonged silence. Time slips away from the viewer’s measures, depriving us of the judgments experience would ordinarily provide. We are left in the absolute present of the ruin’s own time, facing a noise that gradually loses its received meaning, held between sound and silence as we wait for the last instant when the structure tears to its limit and cuts across the present.

## Collapse at the Threshold: A Self-Destructive Finale

As the performance approaches its end, the final support nears the threshold of failure. The “collapsing instrument” sounds its last note through loosening and falling, and in the sound’s own dissolution the order that held it together is dismantled in our hearing. The work’s damage brings failures of order and breaks in narrative to the bodily scale of matter. In an irreversible fracture, the listener understands that the form before them can no longer return to its earlier state. This body losing its shape reflects back to us the final limit shared by our own bodies.

Ruinwork carries the “collapsing instrument” of the ruin past its last trembling support. Together with the structure’s dismantling it brings forth one final dramatic note—the “self-destructive final resonance.” At the very instant of sounding, the mechanism that allows the note to exist breaks down. Because these events coincide, the note severs its own possibility of return at the moment of birth, becoming a material event that cannot be performed again. It suddenly reveals the conventions of instrument, performer, and stage: as music nears its termination, it makes visible the material body upon which it has always depended.[^36] The noise exceeds the discipline of listening, for no established note could contain the unarranged sound released through disintegration. This final resonance answers ruinwork’s original desire: the form made by hand undergoes its own dissolution, exposing the absurdity and limits concealed by an intact appearance in a performance that cannot recur.

The Ruinwright knows that old roles and old settings no longer answer one another, yet still approaches the uninhabited remains with the seriousness of a builder and adds a new creation. Measured against ordinary social life, such a wager of the work itself carries something of the resolve called “madness.” It also evokes the extreme image of dying for love: when a conviction can no longer obtain any eternal assurance from the world, one still commits something finite to it, giving the conviction form through an ending that cannot be undone.[^37] Ruinwork borrows this irreversibility of self-destruction, placing the material life of the work itself at stake. The “beauty of dying for love” lies between lucid knowledge of finitude and the determination to entrust the work nonetheless.

Yet when a carefully arranged overturning reaches its end, the Ruinwright cannot take its destruction as final proof of conviction. Through the work’s disintegration, he sought to strip away order’s claim to permanence, but the curtain call he designed also reveals his own limits. He can create conditions for fracture without deciding where materials will finally go. He can choose to complete a work through destruction without, by that act, freeing himself completely from the world that formed his knowledge, craft, and desire, or entering some purer world. The order that once gave architecture its purpose has ceased to answer, while the maker searching for freedom among ruins still bears its traces. Having recognized the stage’s absurdity, the Ruinwright cannot stand outside the stage. Making briefly resisted the erosion of conviction through destruction, only to discover, when the work itself disappeared, that destruction could offer no eternal guarantee to any conviction.

When the last sound has faded, stage and instrument fall silent together. What remains is the same ground that was there before, and wreckage brought about by the maker’s own hands. The former roles have left the stage, and the new performance has lost its body as the work breaks apart. The Ruinwright still stands there, knowing that labor and conviction cannot find an eternal guarantee in this irreversible ending. And yet, within this unpromised condition, he freely chose to build and lived through the departure of what he made. How, then, can the freedom found among ruins be carried back into a reality that continues to operate? When the roles and objects through which we performed have withdrawn, how can we face a self that is equally finite and equally imperfect?

# Epilogue — After Ruinwork

The curtain has fallen. The work remains among the wreckage, but its maker must return to a world that continues to function as before. The freedom and delight found in building and disintegration have not vanished with the final fracture, yet neither can they fill the strange estrangement and emptiness that come after a performance ends. We tried to use destruction to strip the claim to permanence from order, only to encounter the same absurdity and finitude in the things we made. When the forms through which we once resisted the world finally break apart, reality before us has not thereby changed. We, too, are products of that world, carrying what we have inherited from it as we continue to live within its order.

This romantic absorption in decay may also conceal a nihilism shaped by the prolonged exhaustion of the modern mind. It lodges the self in absolute solitude and tries to reduce the world to ruins, treating destruction as a paradoxical release. Once the familiar world ceases to command belief, breaking it can seem the most truthful response to what is false. If only the repeated shattering of established forms can make us feel something real again, negation itself may become another rule demanding obedience. Each collapse seems obliged to be more thorough than the last, merely to preserve the shock once felt at a fracture, until the demand for destruction hardens into a habit that cannot stop. As immersion in failure becomes a settled disposition, nihilism quietly grows where despair meets aesthetic pleasure. Its danger lies precisely in mistaking destruction for the sole honest answer to falsehood. If we go on demanding that things break in order to escape order, we may end up obeying another prison we have strengthened with our own hands.[^38]

Outside this cycle of breaking, however, ruins retain another beauty that still draws us near. Objects stripped of their former use continue to decay where they stand. An unexpectedly exposed fracture plane or a trace of life not yet erased can still make us stop. We know that fragments cannot restore the life once lived there, or compensate for what was truly lost. Yet we can feel beauty in their unfinished changes, and even give our time to something we know cannot endure. Such feeling requires no escalation of destruction, no more complete collapse to prove itself. Materials not yet spent continue their passage through time, and from these finite remains we can still perceive beauty.

Perhaps a return to simplicity is possible only after arriving at such knowledge. We once tried to strip away the faces that human order had placed upon things, seeking a purer material truth within the ruin, and in the course of that long search discovered that the very gaze through which we look at the world has already been shaped by second nature. We cannot strip these experiences and memories entirely from ourselves or return to a primordial world untouched by civilization. Still, after discerning more of the world’s complexity and admitting that we, too, are implicated in its contradictions, we may meet what stands before us with the openness we had at the beginning. That openness is not made more remote by understanding. It now carries hesitation, tenderness, and self-knowledge, allowing us to approach a thing even when we know it will lose its form, and to feel the brief, real changes through which it passes.

The body, life, and abilities of a human being have limits, and there is no need to flee them. It is because time and existence have an end for us that the choice of where to give them becomes truly our own. Looking back on years spent wandering and building among ruins, I cannot reclaim the time consumed, nor may my works leave behind a result that could account for it. I nevertheless still understand ruinwork as a craft of “giving beauty to imperfect destruction”: borrowing forces not yet exhausted within irreversible decay, bringing something that did not previously exist to stand for a while, then surrendering its later transformation to time in full knowledge that it will pass. This creation cannot perfect the world or turn the Ruinwright into a self exempt from reality. But the materials touched by hand, the years spent on site, and the perception of beauty found among broken things have all truly occurred. They have become an experience that cannot be erased from my life.

When the performance is over, the Ruinwright must, in the end, leave the ruin and return to the world that never stopped working. There is no longer any need to demand a more thorough destruction to prove that I once saw its absurdity, or to abandon the feelings that first made me stop. The world remains complicated, and I remain small and imperfect; the questions still unanswered will unfold with the next journey and the next act of building. Perhaps the freedom one may gain after ruinwork lies in knowing that none of this carries an eternal guarantee, yet still being willing to wander toward the things that awaken curiosity and beauty, and to create, in places not yet prescribed, things even I had never imagined.

Eliot’s original line is “These fragments I have shored against my ruins.” In the Chinese epigraph I use 碎片 (material fragments) in place of 片段 (passages or fragments), to retain its physical associations.

[^2]: “Second nature” here names an environment jointly produced by architecture, infrastructure, and social practices, then taken for granted in everyday life. See Henri Lefebvre, The Production of Space, trans. Donald Nicholson-Smith (Oxford: Blackwell, 1991), 409.

[^3]: The imagery of the “angel of history” and the wind of “progress” is adapted from section IX of Walter Benjamin’s “On the Concept of History,” in Selected Writings, vol. 4, 1938–1940, ed. Howard Eiland and Michael W. Jennings, trans. Harry Zohn (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press, 2003), 392.

[^4]: “New Topographics” turned its attention to ordinary landscapes altered by human activity. See New Topographics: Photographs of a Man-Altered Landscape, exhibition catalogue (Rochester, NY: International Museum of Photography at George Eastman House, 1975).

[^5]: On the “untimeliness” of modern urban ruins, which emerge before their time and may be cleared away before receiving the status of monuments, see Nick Yablon, Untimely Ruins: An Archaeology of American Urban Modernity, 1819–1919 (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 2010). I extend this observation to contemporary processes of renewal and abandonment.

[^6]: The “human stratum” is a working concept of this essay, not the name of a geological epoch. Noel Castree uses “Anthroposcene” for networks of research, institutions, and knowledge production associated with the Anthropocene, emphasizing the interpretive frameworks through which we understand environmental change. I bring this question to urban remains: humans leave buildings and waste, while processes of recording, valuation, and renewal continually decide which creations enter public history. The “human stratum” thus comprises both the material accumulation of human-made objects and the historically selective ways in which they are known. Its material aspect also touches studies of anthropogenic deposits and technofossils. See Noel Castree, “Changing the Anthropo(s)cene: Geographers, Global Environmental Change and the Politics of Knowledge,” Dialogues in Human Geography 5, no. 3 (2015): 301–16, https://doi.org/10.1177/2043820615613216; Matt Edgeworth et al., “Diachronous Beginnings of the Anthropocene,” The Anthropocene Review 2, no. 1 (2015): 33–58, https://doi.org/10.1177/2053019614565394; Jan Zalasiewicz et al., “The Technofossil Record of Humans,” The Anthropocene Review 1, no. 1 (2014): 34–43, https://doi.org/10.1177/2053019613514953.

[^7]: The “ruin lexicon” consists of naming elements gathered from the material conditions of remains and the experience of sites; “ruin lexicology” denotes a method of recognizing, combining, and revising these terms. Both are creative working expressions in this essay, not established branches of linguistics.

[^8]: Frank White’s “overview effect” serves here as an analogy for a change of viewing scale: dispersed remains become connected in an atlas, revealing the limits of an order that appears stable at the everyday scale. Formal resemblance, however, cannot replace inquiry into the particular histories of individual places. See Frank White, The Overview Effect: Space Exploration and Human Evolution (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1987).

[^9]: “Implement” and “thing” describe two aspects through which this essay approaches surviving objects. The former emphasizes construction and use; the latter emphasizes appearance, naming, and meanings in life. These aspects, once mutually reinforcing, may separate after abandonment. They do not designate mutually exclusive categories of actual objects.

[^10]: *Folly* is one site-based form of ruinwork. It borrows from European garden follies and from the English word’s associations with futility, fantasy, and foolishness, without being confined to imitation of existing architectural ruins. “Guerrilla gardening” serves only as a methodological analogy for finding conditions for growth at an existing site. See Richard Reynolds, On Guerrilla Gardening: A Handbook for Gardening without Boundaries (London: Bloomsbury, 2008).

[^11]: The image of the “android” is linked here to the distinction, within philosophia perennis, between human form, personality, and a ground of being. In the tradition surveyed by Huxley, there are dimensions of existence beyond the observable body and psychological persona. I borrow only the question of whether reproducing a form exhausts what it is, redirecting it to the relation between a destructive test body and architecture; I make no claim that buildings possess a sacred essence. See Aldous Huxley, The Perennial Philosophy (New York: Harper & Brothers, 1945), “That Art Thou” and “Personality, Sanctity, Divine Incarnation.”

[^12]: “Posthuman condition” here describes a space in which, after relations of use have disappeared, materials, plants, and environmental forces no longer fully obey human arrangements. It is a spatial analogy for the loosening of human centrality. See Rosi Braidotti, The Posthuman (Cambridge: Polity Press, 2013), chap. 2.

[^13]: In One-Way Street, Benjamin describes the Alcázar, where human movement seems absorbed by the “soundless tumult” of ornament. I borrow this image for the experience of human activity being surrounded by environmental change within a ruin. Walter Benjamin, One-Way Street and Other Writings, trans. Edmund Jephcott and Kingsley Shorter (London: New Left Books, 1979), 82.

[^14]: “Liminality” originally describes a transition in which someone has left a former identity but has not entered the next. I borrow this sense of suspension to understand surviving structures. See Arnold van Gennep, The Rites of Passage, trans. Monika B. Vizedom and Gabrielle L. Caffee (Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1960), 10–11; Victor Turner, The Ritual Process: Structure and Anti-Structure (Chicago: Aldine, 1969), 94–130.

[^15]: I borrow the German unheimlich, a disturbing familiarity, to describe the failure of spatial recognition: doors, walls, and stairs remain identifiable, while their former relations of use and shelter have loosened. Freud explores how the familiar may become uncanny, and Vidler extends this experience to architecture and modern space. This is an aesthetic analogy, not an identification with the “uncanny valley” hypothesis concerning humanlike figures. See Sigmund Freud, “The ‘Uncanny’” (1919), in The Standard Edition of the Complete Psychological Works of Sigmund Freud, vol. 17, ed. James Strachey (London: Hogarth Press, 1955), 219–52; Anthony Vidler, The Architectural Uncanny: Essays in the Modern Unhomely (Cambridge, MA: MIT Press, 1992).

[^16]: For precedents in organizing musical performance through open graphic systems and transparent overlays, see Earle Brown, December 1952, in Folio and 4 Systems (Associated Music Publishers/G. Schirmer, 1952–54), https://earle-brown.org/work/folio-and-4-systems/; John Cage, Fontana Mix (Henmar Press, 1958), https://www.wisemusicclassical.com/work/69276/Fontana-Mix--John-Cage/.

[^17]: In Walter M. Miller Jr.’s A Canticle for Leibowitz, a monk copies a circuit blueprint that has lost its original technological context, turning it into an elaborate manuscript. I borrow the image of a drawing whose purpose changes through copying. Walter M. Miller Jr., A Canticle for Leibowitz (Philadelphia: J. B. Lippincott, 1959), pt. 1, “Fiat Homo.”

[^18]: Arata Isozaki’s Incubation Process (1962) places a futuristic megastructure among classical remains, linking the future city to the ruin. I borrow this image to think about building, labor across generations, and the eventual disappearance of cities. See Arata Isozaki, “Incubation Process,” Bijutsu Techo (April 1962); MISA SHIN GALLERY, “Isozaki Arata: PROCESS,” exhibition press release (2011), https://www.misashin.com/en/exhibitions-en/isozaki-arata-process-2/.

[^19]: I borrow Robert M. Pirsig’s reflections on maintenance, attention, and “Quality” to suggest that understanding may take shape through the sustained encounter between a worker and material. See Robert M. Pirsig, Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance: An Inquiry into Values (New York: William Morrow, 1974), chaps. 24–26.

[^20]: Two early strands of the picturesque are William Gilpin’s interest in scenery suited to pictorial composition and Uvedale Price’s attention to rough and irregular forms. See William Gilpin, Three Essays: On Picturesque Beauty; On Picturesque Travel; and On Sketching Landscape (London: R. Blamire, 1792), 3; Uvedale Price, An Essay on the Picturesque, as Compared with the Sublime and the Beautiful (London: J. Robson, 1794), 44–45.

[^21]: On the historical relation among British picturesque aesthetics, the viewing of ruins, and artificial remains in gardens, see Malcolm Andrews, The Search for the Picturesque: Landscape Aesthetics and Tourism in Britain, 1760–1800 (Stanford, CA: Stanford University Press, 1989), 41–50.

[^22]: The discussion of spatial experience arrested by photography draws on Robert Morris, “The Present Tense of Space,” Art in America 66, no. 1 (January–February 1978): 70–81. I extend his consideration of bodily, temporal, and spatial experience to the continuing transformation of ruins.

[^23]: Here I draw chiefly on Burke’s account of danger and aesthetic distance and Kant’s dynamical sublime to understand the forces that make one sense the limits of one’s own scale within a ruin. See Edmund Burke, A Philosophical Enquiry into the Origin of Our Ideas of the Sublime and Beautiful, 2nd ed., ed. Paul Guyer (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2015), pt. 2, secs. 2, 7; Immanuel Kant, Critique of Judgment, trans. Werner S. Pluhar (Indianapolis: Hackett, 1987), §§28–29.

[^24]: “Solastalgia” originally refers to the distress caused when a familiar home environment changes although its inhabitant has not left. Here it serves as an analogy for the mixture of intimacy and estrangement felt by a later visitor to a lost space, not as an equation of that wanderer with the residents studied in the original research. See Glenn Albrecht et al., “Solastalgia: The Distress Caused by Environmental Change,” Australasian Psychiatry 15, suppl. 1 (2007): S95–S98, https://doi.org/10.1080/10398560701701288.

[^25]: Benjamin relates the fragmentary nature of allegory to ruins. I borrow this association to understand the “broken prism” as a way of seeing material and historical relations anew after an intact exterior fractures, rather than as a direct restatement of his theory of allegory. Walter Benjamin, The Origin of German Tragic Drama, trans. John Osborne (London: Verso, 1998), 176–78.

[^26]: “Modern tragedy” juxtaposes two lines of thought in this essay: Nietzsche’s Apollonian and Dionysian understanding of how tragedy can contain both beauty of form and suffering in life, and Lacan’s discussion through Antigone of the relation between tragic beauty and the limits of desire. I extend them to the contradiction of being drawn toward the beauty of a ruin while facing real loss; “modern tragedy” is not presented as a technical term of Lacan’s. See Friedrich Nietzsche, The Birth of Tragedy, trans. Douglas Smith (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2000), §§1–4; Jacques Lacan, The Seminar of Jacques Lacan, Book VII: The Ethics of Psychoanalysis, 1959–1960, ed. Jacques-Alain Miller, trans. Dennis Porter (New York: W. W. Norton, 1992), 243–90.

[^27]: The “ruin complex” is related to the fascination called Ruinenlust, while also asking how the pleasure of looking can obscure the real losses behind the remains. See Carlos López Galviz et al., “Reconfiguring Ruins: Beyond Ruinenlust,” GeoHumanities 3, no. 2 (2017): 531–53, https://doi.org/10.1080/2373566X.2017.1374874.

[^28]: I borrow Surrealism’s exploration of chance juxtapositions and unconscious associations to understand new connections arising when fragments lose their former uses. See André Breton, Manifestoes of Surrealism, trans. Richard Seaver and Helen R. Lane (Ann Arbor: University of Michigan Press, 1969), “Manifesto of Surrealism” (1924).

[^29]: In architectural history, a folly may also refer to an ornamental garden structure departing from practical purpose. I connect this word creatively to the similar form and meaning of “Fool”; the connection is not an established architectural-historical category. James Stevens Curl and Susan Wilson, The Oxford Dictionary of Architecture, 3rd ed. (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2015), s.v. “folly.”

[^30]: The image of the Fool approaching a cliff in the tarot is borrowed here as an image of creative potential not yet bounded by established measures of value. See Arthur Edward Waite, The Pictorial Key to the Tarot (London: William Rider & Son, 1911), pt. 2, “0. The Fool.”

[^31]: For the metaphors of roles, frontstage, and backstage, see Erving Goffman, The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life (Garden City, NY: Doubleday, 1959), chap. 3. The “backstage after the curtain falls” in this essay instead names a loss of correspondence between roles and space, and does not adopt Goffman’s specific definition of the backstage.

[^32]: Derrida uses “specters” to think through unfinished historical responsibilities, while Fisher considers “lost futures”: possibilities once imagined but later deprived of the conditions for realization. I borrow these approaches to understand expectations and unfinished histories lingering in modern remains. See Jacques Derrida, Specters of Marx: The State of the Debt, the Work of Mourning and the New International, trans. Peggy Kamuf (New York: Routledge, 1994); Mark Fisher, Ghosts of My Life: Writings on Depression, Hauntology and Lost Futures (Winchester: Zero Books, 2014).

[^33]: I borrow Lehmann’s discussion of the “postdramatic,” in which plot no longer governs the entire experience of theater, as an analogy for the relative independence of time, space, and material events after the former uses of a structure withdraw. “Postdramatic space” is my own creative application. See Hans-Thies Lehmann, Postdramatic Theatre, trans. Karen Jürs-Munby (London: Routledge, 2006).

[^34]: Ryuichi Sakamoto described the detuning of a piano submerged by the tsunami in terms of material departing from human tuning. I use this to imagine the noises made by buildings as they weather, without claiming that nature possesses some other correct tuning system. See Ryuichi Sakamoto: Coda, dir. Stephen Nomura Schible (Cineric and Borderland Media, 2017); Kevin Nguyen, “Themes and Variations: An Interview with Ryuichi Sakamoto,” GQ, July 18, 2018, https://www.gq.com/story/ryuichi-sakamoto-interview.

[^35]: This figure is conceptually adapted and redrawn after Pierre Schaeffer, “Sound and the Century: A Socio-Aesthetic Treatise,” trans. and intro. Donna Zapf, Vanguard 9, no. 1 (February 1980): 6–12, fig. 4. The four quadrants and their English names interpret architectural sound for this essay; they are neither verbatim translations of the source figure nor four stages in the evolution of sound.

[^36]: Kagel’s Konzertstück für Pauken und Orchester was composed in 1990–1992, and its score was published in 1995. See Mauricio Kagel, Konzertstück: für Pauken und Orchester (Frankfurt am Main: Henry Litolff’s Verlag/C. F. [^37]: “Dying for love” is borrowed as an image of an irretrievable ending linked to conviction. In *Folly*, what is surrendered is the work’s material life; it does not follow that actual self-destruction could prove a belief.

[^38]: I call the tendency to demand ever more intense negation in pursuit of a feeling of reality the “cycle of nihilism.” On how revolt, when absolutized, can become a new confinement, see Albert Camus, The Rebel: An Essay on Man in Revolt, trans.
