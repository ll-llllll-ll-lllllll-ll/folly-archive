# Preface — After Architecture

Architecture shapes our lives almost imperceptibly. Long before we begin to understand space, doors, windows, and walls have already become common sense to us, and we have learned to live between them. Since industrialization, buildings, technological infrastructure, and institutional rules have grown increasingly intertwined, forming an environment in constant operation—what I call “second nature.”[^2] We are born into it, work and build within it, and seek shelter in it. In time, we come to mistake a world we have made for the way the world has always been.

As second nature expands, we use rational calculation and technology to bring more of the uncertainties and dangers of the natural world within the reach of prediction and intervention. There are good reasons for this pursuit of certainty: time and again, our ability to control our surroundings and anticipate what lies ahead has helped us survive. Yet as the means of maintaining stability become the measure by which life itself is organized, reason, which once helped us understand the world, begins to prescribe how the world ought to appear. Within these increasingly elaborate arrangements, there is less and less room for the accidents of trial and error, or for things to grow of their own accord.

As we entrust so much of our lives to second nature, the need to keep the system stable scarcely seems to require justification. The boundaries we established between ourselves and nature for our protection may gradually come to confine us in turn. The systems we have devised continually repair their own failures, concealing their fragility through constant upkeep until the order we have made seems indestructible. Caught in a web of our own weaving, we grow accustomed to understanding and arranging life according to its rules. It becomes difficult even to imagine what might lie beyond them.

And yet, ruins appear.

Ruins emerge along cracks that have long been forming in the shadows of civilization. They take shape wherever an order can no longer sustain itself. As weathering strips away the surfaces of a building, the damage and vulnerability concealed beneath its once solid appearance come into view. The decisions behind its construction and abandonment also leave their costs inscribed on what remains. The very standards by which we organize life, judge value, and distribute resources are now applied to the spaces we have built, deciding which buildings deserve further care and which are left to disintegrate on their own. In ruins, the costs that once found little place in the story of progress acquire a form that remains on the ground.

Looking back from a ruin, our gaze, once carried forward by civilization’s narrative of progress, can rest for a moment on the wreckage left behind. If we turn our backs to the future and face everything that has already happened, we see discarded objects and stillborn ideals behind all those celebrated achievements. Yet the wind we call “progress” continues to drive us forward, piling debris at our feet and behind us.[^3] When such costs are readily accepted as necessary losses in the course of development, the road of modernity inevitably becomes strewn with ruins.

## Modern Ruins: The Strata of Progress

The road of modernity stretches ahead, while the land along it accumulates the traces left in its wake. Housing, industrial facilities and roads overlap to form a landscape utterly familiar to modern life. The New Topographics movement that emerged in the latter half of the twentieth century turned the camera away from romanticized natural scenery and toward these ordinary landscapes, recording with a cool, restrained eye the marks that building had left on the land. These photographs reveal precisely what we have grown accustomed to in second nature: human-made order has already profoundly altered the landscape, and the alterations themselves often pass unnoticed in the familiar background of everyday life.[^4]

In my own experiments with this kind of photography, I deliberately left this seemingly small but conspicuous “dotted-line gate” in the frame. Yet there are no walls on either side of the gate to separate the city from the wilderness. Enormous ruined structures still stand, retaining much of their form and the relationships between their load-bearing elements. They once housed complex technologies and particular social purposes, yet those who look at them may find it difficult to imagine the lives that unfolded inside. When the camera turns from facilities still in use to modern ruins, the losses passed over so readily in accounts of construction and renewal begin to acquire a recognizable outline. We are accustomed to treating a building’s abandonment as the cost of development, and seldom ask why it was abandoned before its material life had run its course, or who bears the consequences. Once a thing is pronounced obsolete, the labor and life that once gathered around it can disappear from discussions centered on utility and returns. In time, this becomes a habitual failure to see.

Meanwhile, modernity continues down its road, and the things left behind accumulate as construction and renewal accelerate. Modern technology has continually increased our capacity to transform the environment; expectations of efficient land use and continual renewal have risen accordingly, though the resources invested do not necessarily correspond to actual needs. Some places still lack essential housing and public facilities, while costly buildings of questionable usefulness continue to appear elsewhere. A great deal of material, labor and aspiration may be invested in something that is no longer deemed worth using or maintaining before it has even had a chance to participate fully in life.[^5] In this imbalance, the social life of a building sometimes ends before its material life, leaving the landscape of modern civilization dotted with structures that survive long after they have fallen out of daily life.

There is something glaringly untimely about modern ruins whose material lives are far from over. Their outlines, components and traces of use are still clear, but the lives that once took place around them have withdrawn. They linger beside the prosperity in which we live, unable to find their way easily back into the complex relations of society, or to find a place in new experiences and collective memories. They often die too young: before they have had time to become ancient, they already face demolition and erasure once more.[^6] This premature death makes it difficult to see them simply as melancholy or magnificent historical spectacles. The lingering traces of life and the material changes still taking place leave a long aftershock upon their surviving forms.

The historical ruins we can recognize today may represent only a fraction of all that has ever been destroyed. Some survive in recognizable architectural form, like the Parthenon after centuries of alterations, damage and repairs. Others, like Yinxu, long ago lost the appearance of their original buildings, yet have re-entered history through sites, foundations and excavated objects. Still others, such as the Old Summer Palace in Beijing, became important places through which later generations reckon with history and memory after their destruction in war. Their survival owes as much to subsequent repair, reuse, rediscovery and historical judgment as it does to their materials and surroundings.[^7] Countless ordinary things that once existed alongside them may already have vanished through erosion, demolition and successive cycles of building, leaving us no means of recovering even their ruined appearance or the lives they once held. Those ruins that did not survive, together with their unfinished histories, have receded into a past beyond our reach.

Looking back at the ruins that have survived, we begin to realize that many modern ruins beside us have reached a point where their fate remains undecided. With ancient sites, we rarely have the opportunity to witness how an ordinary building became a ruin, or how it subsequently lost the last of its form. Modern ruins bring this gradual passage toward disappearance within the span of our own lives. The lives and wishes they once held may vanish with them before they have even entered the memory of future generations.

The dust of history has not yet settled on modern ruins. They were abandoned not so long ago, and many remain embedded in familiar neighborhoods and surroundings. They are close to us in time and space, but also in the way we understand our own lives: those things for which there is now no place were once part of the same second nature we inhabit today.

Surviving ruins overlap with buildings still in use, infrastructure and traces of everyday life, gradually forming a stratum made by our own acts of construction, use and abandonment—the “human stratum.”[^8] This landscape preserves the material forms left by human building, while continually revealing which creations are allowed to endure and which recede into oblivion as new arrangements of life emerge. To me, in this landscape of overlapping layers, a ruin resembles a form that absent time has left growing on the ground: a history I never took part in, a hometown that no longer matches my memory, yet which still remains in the world through its surviving body. The fragments and broken narratives cannot bring the past back, but at least they allow me to be present for its disappearance.

## Ruinwork and the Ruinwright

I have always felt a tenderness for prematurely abandoned modern structures that I have never quite known where to put. They once held people's labor, lives, and aspirations, and the histories and memories attached to them may find no place to go when the buildings disappear. This tenderness makes me reluctant to leave ruins solely to photographs and remembrance. I return to them again and again, following cracks that have yet to close, asking how the remains before me came to be this way, and how we might still respond to them.

![Fig. 4a](manifesto-440-assets/fig-04-a.jpg)

![Fig. 4b](manifesto-440-assets/fig-04-b.jpg)

This chance arrangement did not last. As the fragments loosened, the tree too fell among the ruins. Photographs by the author, 2023.

Tenderness for these remains, however, cannot in itself tell us what should be done with them. Demolition and removal consume still more resources. Repair and reuse may prolong the life of the materials and labor already invested, but they may do little for the lives and memories displaced by abandonment; preserving every ruin as it stands may, in turn, dress real suffering up as scenery for others to admire. These contradictions gradually led me to realize that the questions left by ruins extend well beyond architectural form.

I once followed the conventional paths of architecture and engineering, looking for workable solutions to problems of use, maintenance, and the environment. Over time, I came to understand that architecture can improve actual conditions through repair, design, and technology, but it cannot on its own determine how resources are distributed or whose lives deserve continued care. My wish to arrive at a satisfactory answer through a design proposal repeatedly ran up against questions that a proposal alone could not settle. Why do we keep building, renewing, and abandoning spaces, and how do the things we make in turn shape our lives, perceptions, and memories?

![Fig. 5](manifesto-440-assets/fig-05-a.jpg)

@caption Fig. 5. From architecture to ruinwork: Dolomite Stonehenge. A pavilion proposal for Jackson Park, Chicago, made during my architectural studies, as I was beginning to move away from conventional architectural design. Reading Juhani Pallasmaa’s The Eyes of the Skin, particularly his reflections on matter and time, led me to question modern architecture’s pursuit of a perpetually new appearance. Stone, brick, and wood acquire traces of age through use and weathering, allowing our senses to reach into years beyond the span of an individual life. At the time, I wondered whether architecture, as it makes space habitable, might also allow us to inhabit time.[^9] That question gradually led me toward the study of ruins, where I began to attend to the ways time continues to shape buildings long after their use and care have ceased. When I returned to my hometown after nearly ten years away, the familiar city had changed through continuous demolition and reconstruction. Between old buildings not yet entirely demolished and new ones never completed, I recognized scenes from my childhood, when I watched the city expand. What disoriented me was that I was only in my twenties, yet the spaces that held the memories of my life already had to be rediscovered as though they belonged to a distant history. The streets and buildings were still clear in my mind, but the places that once gave those memories a home were difficult to find. In just ten years, even the years I had lived through seemed to have become a past in need of investigation. This feeling brought to mind solastalgia, a kind of homesickness at home: I had returned to my hometown, but the things that made it feel like home had been disappearing in place.[^10] As a child, I had taken these recurring scenes of construction, stagnation, and disappearance for granted. Returning after my architectural training, I could recognize more of their structural and spatial relationships. I began to wonder how these buildings had housed people's lives, and why, in so few years, they had ceased to be needed. This gradually became the reason I kept returning to ruins.

![Fig. 6](manifesto-440-assets/fig-06-a.jpg)

@caption Fig. 6. Returning to the ruins of my hometown: a low building stands on vacant ground between fields and roads near an old cement factory. Housing and a communications tower remain visible in the distance. Former industrial land and ongoing everyday life meet within the same view. Photograph by the author, 2025.

Walking into these decaying structures again, I began to pay attention to the forces still held among the rubble and to the traces of life buried beneath it. Surveying allowed me to follow the fractures in the material and piece together how these buildings had reached their present condition. Their predicament made it increasingly difficult to believe that I could answer everything with a new design. Instead, I began with the surviving traces and the forces still at work, practicing the craft of building I had learned long after the architecture itself had withdrawn from daily life, in the hope of approaching the histories and unfulfilled wishes concealed beneath the rubble.

Where ownership and site safety permit, new structures draw on existing supports and tensions. Changes brought about by weathering and the forces still at work in a ruin pass through new connections into the work itself. The slow, barely perceptible process of becoming a ruin takes on a form that can be observed and heard. As the new structure bears these forces, it wears away and becomes part of the same process. Through these movements and lingering sounds, I hope people will look beyond the scene destruction has left behind, following the changes still taking place into the ruin itself, toward the lives it once held, its present predicament, and the material life that remains. I call this practice, which begins after architecture and explores the disappearance of our creations through the act of building, “ruinwork.” Folly is its first concrete form.

“Ruinwright” is a role I have gradually found for myself through years of exploring, surveying, and building in ruins. The name corresponds to no established professional qualification, nor can any one discipline adequately describe it. It comes closer to the role I have assumed as an independent researcher working across the boundaries of art, architecture and engineering. The wild ideas that found no place in conventional building could finally be put to the test in these surviving spaces. My investigations and constructions could finally take root, and it was only here that I dared to call myself a wright, continuing to explore a craft still growing wild among the ruins.

I have chosen to write these observations in the form of a free, exploratory essay—an ensaio. This Manifesto of the Ruinwright does not seek to follow the conventions of an academic paper or formal research report. Field notes, research across disciplines and personal impressions intersect as these thoughts unfold, so that the writing itself becomes an ongoing intellectual adventure. For now, I want to put these thoughts down, as one might erect an unfinished structure in a ruin, bringing scattered experiences into relation while leaving enough room for future revision. The questions that remain unanswered will give me another reason to return.

# Tenets of the Ruinwright

When I decided to write down principles for ruinwork, my thoughts turned first to architecture's ancient tradition of building. Over centuries, we have accumulated knowledge of how to build, how to make buildings sound and useful, and how to preserve their form and beauty. Yet we have never been able to foresee entirely how natural changes, shifts in human affections and changes in society will alter the fate of what we make. Our civilization continues to build, leaving behind one architectural carcass after another whose material life is far from over. When a thing loses its original use and care, how might the knowledge that brought it into being help us face the rest of its life? As an inheritor of this craft, I want to begin with the remains left behind and write a few of the laws that have yet to be established for architecture after failure and abandonment. These principles govern first of all how I enter, observe and alter a site; they grant the ruinwright no right to dispose of ruins at will or to tell other people's histories on their behalf.

To this end, I deliberately borrow the solemn form in which The Ten Books on Architecture organized knowledge and established standards for the craft of building, in writing the Tenets of the Ruinwright.[^11] It is a somewhat irreverent homage: I still use the knowledge and tools inherited from architecture and engineering, but take them toward the very failures and disintegration they were intended to resist, testing the measures by which buildings are judged and even overturning those that no longer apply to ruins. There is something absurd, too, about drawing up a code for an office nobody has conferred upon me. Ten is a limit I have deliberately drawn, borrowed from the familiar count of the fingers of both hands. With this simple, slightly ceremonial number, I gather what I have learned through years of practice into ten principles to be remembered, consulted and revised.

![Fig. 8](manifesto-440-assets/fig-08-a.png)

@caption Fig. 8 (a–j). “The Broken Frame”: ten successive emblems for the Tenets of the Ruinwright, arranged in order of the frame's progressive loss and fracture. Drawings by the author, 2025.

These laws have been distilled from repeated surveys and acts of building. Some have already taken shape in the particular methods of Folly; others await the test of further practice. The tenets begin with entering a site and recognizing its remains anew, before turning to borrowing surviving structures and forces, and writing the reverberations of disintegration into the work. As a construction finally approaches its own ruin, the question turns from materials and structure to the builder: knowing that what we make will eventually disintegrate, why do we continue to build? Every building has its time after architecture. The tenets that follow offer provisional guidelines for a craft still finding its way, to be questioned and revised in practice.

## First Tenet: Ruin Lexicology

As second nature expands beyond what any one person can perceive or understand, the everyday spaces organized by buildings and infrastructure become part of our lives long before we understand what lies within them. They even shape the spatial experiences we take for granted. We know these places well, but rarely understand the construction hidden behind their walls and shells, or the complex rules that keep them operating. In time, we mistake familiarity for understanding. Only when a building's apparent solidity begins to break apart do the boundaries that once defined its territories begin to lose their former spatial roles. The ruinwright gains another vantage point from which to recognize spaces once taken for granted.

When we enter an unfamiliar ruin, its former name and scattered historical accounts can still tell us what it once was, but they can tell us little about what is happening there now. If we place the building within a narrative of the past simply by adding the word “ruins” to its former name, the structures still bearing loads, sinking, and breaking apart before us can easily recede into a static image. The language of ruins draws our attention back to the present tense of their disintegration. It gives names to the conditions observed at a particular moment, so that we can return to them and see what has changed. I call this method of identifying, naming, and continually revising the conditions of ruins on site “ruin lexicology.” These names coexist with the buildings' original names and histories, like small archives of field experience.

To understand this ongoing disintegration, one must confront the sense of weightlessness that arises when the familiar spatial logic of a building begins to fail. When architecture betrays its promise of shelter, walls and slabs that once seemed solid can become latent threats. Familiar paths are cut off by rubble, and neither the floor beneath one's feet nor the structure overhead offers reassurance; we have to reconsider where we can go. Yet it is precisely in this wasteland, stripped of its former guarantees, that the ruinwright, through years of wandering and surveying, gradually develops the “eyes of the wasteland”: an ability to notice signs of instability concealed within the remains, in cracks, corrosion and traces of erosion, and to stay almost instinctively alert to changes that have not yet occurred. This perception, honed through repeated surveys, enables the ruinwright to look beyond a building's former appearance and use, toward the erosion its surviving structures are undergoing and the disintegration that may lie ahead.

Over the course of these journeys, things initially called simply “ruins” began to reveal their differences in tilt, weathering, water levels and cavities. When their differences became too numerous for a single noun to contain, new words grew out of the sites themselves. I gradually gathered these into an index of ruin lexicology, organizing morphemes according to the terrain of a ruin, its recognizable architectural parts, its present physical condition and the traces of change left by natural forces. They can be combined, but must also be revised in response to new erosion and fractures, lest a ruin still in motion be mistaken in writing for a specimen.

Over the years, ruin lexicology has connected structures scattered across the world. Remains that resist ordinary spatial understanding gradually disclose their affinities through an expanding vocabulary, coming together in an ever-growing atlas of ruins across the world—Ruin Atlas. Though they differ in form, origin and cause of destruction, the same predicament recurs: the relations that once sustained a space have loosened, while the construction continues to decay in place. When these distant remains are connected within a single atlas, something akin to the tremor of the overview effect begins to emerge: the human-made orders that seem almost self-evident in daily life can also slowly dissipate from their own creations.[^12] Such a shift in perspective is not enough to explain the particular history of any ruin, but it allows us to look back from these disordered constructions at the world we still inhabit.

## Second Tenet: An Archaeology of Things Close to Us

When a building falls out of use and is no longer maintained, it does not immediately revert to anonymous matter. In modern ruins this predicament is especially striking: decay and abandonment happen to things close at hand, and objects fall out of daily life before they have grown old. Function and sign, once closely connected, gradually separate in abandonment. Some components remain, yet it is no longer possible to infer their former use; elsewhere, names, signs and forms remain legible, while the activities once organized around them have dispersed.[^13] Yet they still bear the patina and wear of living, evoking a familiarity we readily recognize even when it is not part of our own lived memory. These things close to us hang awkwardly between everyday waste and cultural heritage, having lost the context that once made them self-explanatory. They are not far removed from us in time, but forgetting has already made a way of life that ended only recently accessible only through something approaching archaeology.

![Fig. 12](manifesto-440-assets/fig-12-a.jpg)

@caption Fig. 12. Things close to us: an old chair in an abandoned corridor, with ferns growing through the remaining cotton and foam of its cushion. Its outline still suggests its former use, while the plants are already altering its present material condition. As for who once sat here, the traces do not suffice to tell us. Photograph by the author, 2026; Ruin Atlas · Relic Archive.

This archaeological way of looking must begin by identifying the present condition of an object. Patina and wear record previous use, while cracks, rust and plant growth continue to alter those traces. The ruinwright follows the clues that remain recognizable into the past, trying to understand how things once intimately connected with daily life came to be abandoned. The things we can no longer identify are also part of the ruin before us. Looking at these incomplete things calls for a restrained respect: an acknowledgment that those vanished everyday lives really existed, and that our knowledge will never encompass them in their entirety.

The ruinwright may continue to build within a ruin, even reconnecting components long separated, but cannot thereby restore the lost relationship between objects and their former lives. If, with limited knowledge and good intentions, one fills in gaps in the story that cannot be verified, the weight of the ruin's own silence may be covered over by sentimentality. Ruinwork restrains the impulse to supply the missing lives, letting the work recede to the very fractures of narrative and matter. It continues along the materials and relations that survive, so that irrecoverable traces of life can be perceived again through forms still changing before us. The work leaves an “anchor in space and time” at which one may stop and look back from the fracture of the present toward those who once used, cherished and eventually left this place, and toward a past that is not ours to possess.

## Third Tenet: Borrowing from Ruins

In the gardens and landscaped spaces of many modern cities, boundaries, pruning and borrowed scenery make nature accessible and pleasing to look at on a human scale. Such ease of viewing often depends on shelter and continuous upkeep. Once the structures maintaining the boundaries begin to fracture, the natural world once framed by the window seeps indoors through the cracks. Rain erodes walls, roots pry masonry apart, and the nature once viewed as scenery begins to occupy and reshape the very space from which it was viewed.

“Borrowing from ruins” arises from this reversal. Following the paths by which nature returns to a space, the ruinwright looks anew at the remains and borrows the forces once tamed and excluded by the garden's order, directing them back toward architecture and the human will behind it. Through the broken skeleton, the histories and aspirations once built up layer upon layer begin to reveal their limits. The people who lifted all this matter high above the ground have gone, yet the surviving components still carry loads; their struggle against gravity has not ceased, as though a fall had been suspended in midair. It is this unresolved change itself that the ruinwright borrows.

This way of seeing eventually enters the actual work of building. To borrow means that a construction must find a footing in the site's existing structures, available remnants and environmental conditions. Just as guerrilla gardening recognizes conditions for growth on neglected land, this site-specific construction first seeks materials, supports and load-bearing relations that can still be borrowed. The work begins by borrowing things and ultimately finds its direction in borrowing forces: responding to different terrains and surviving forms, it lets the openings created by weathering shape the work. The material conditions of the site constrain and revise the original design; the ruinwright must, as far as possible, contain the failure of the new structure within the work itself, without inflicting unwarranted damage upon the original ruin. Folly comes into being through such borrowing: it grows along the fracture where the old order failed and draws on the continuing intrusion of nature to lead the garden toward its own eventual dissolution.[^14]

Borrowed things must eventually be returned. The first kind of return in Folly takes place when the builder no longer demands that the work indefinitely retain the form given to it: within the limits of the site and its environment, the new structure continues to weather and undergo changes in its materials. The other kind of return concerns the person who intervenes. The ruinwright cannot claim ownership of the ruin, nor leave the risks and consequences of the intervention to time. As this temporary borrowing comes to an end, materials that might impose a lasting burden on the environment or nearby lives must be dealt with responsibly, and necessary changes to the site documented in records that can be consulted later. The disappearance of a form does not require that every remnant be left in place. What the ruinwright relinquishes is the insistence on the work's future form, not responsibility for having altered the land.

## Fourth Tenet: Brutal Materiality

Modern ruins often appear as bare concrete frames. Exterior finishes that once concealed the construction within and softened its boundaries grow moldy and peel away as they weather, sometimes leaving only the skeleton that bears the loads and divides the space. This stripping resembles the peeling away of the skin of second nature, bringing the construction and material weight hidden beneath the building's intact appearance back into view. The aesthetics of ruination thus approach a kind of brutality, arising not from a predetermined formal expression, but from the physical reality that gradually emerges when maintenance ceases: a reality governed by gravity, corrosion and a finite lifespan.

In the ruinwright's eyes, these exposed structures also embody a powerful modernist impulse: through precise structural calculations and synthetic materials, we sought to extend our will high and far into space. Technological reason seems far removed from primitive wildness, yet these towering concrete skeletons also betray an ambition bordering on conquest—to contain the uncertainties of nature within limits of our own making.

Yet as these buildings that embody human ambition gradually fall into ruin, another kind of wildness reveals itself from within. The skeleton left by precise calculations still stands, while the connections that keep it stable slowly loosen through erosion. The legacy of highly developed technology and the most elementary physical forces meet violently within the same broken body, lending the ruin an untamable wildness. In this confrontation, the brutality of the modern ruin takes on a different meaning: the desire for conquest once embodied in the structure encounters the intractable weight and finite lifespan of matter itself. This material reality breaking free from within human-made order is what I call “brutal materiality”—the flesh laid bare as control recedes. Reason has not ceased to be valid in a ruin; it can simply no longer take precedence over material reality.

Ruinwork likewise shifts its attention from the appearance of brutality to the material confrontation still taking place. Rather than symbolizing strength through rough forms, the work bears weight, undergoes displacement, and wears down, continually exposing the traces of its formation, loading and eventual decay. Where construction and use permit, natural fracture surfaces, joints and marks of fabrication need not all be concealed beneath finishes. This rough, unrefined beauty comes from materials that still carry the traces of their formation, loading and decay, rather than disappearing entirely beneath the skin of a finished product. Where do the actual load-bearing limits of the modern materials in which we once took pride lie, and at what point will they resist or yield? The ruinwright builds along the lines of this question and continues to observe these changes as the work evolves.

## Fifth Tenet: Following the Flow of Forces

From the moment of its completion, a building has organized enormous material weight into a system of forces that support and restrain one another. Masonry is laid layer upon layer, and steel cables held in tension keep components in place. The forces that fix space do not vanish along with the building's use. Even in ruins already on the verge of collapse, loads may still be acting on the structure, while surviving supports and restraints barely keep them standing for a while. Traces of structural decay point back in time: following spreading cracks and loosening connections, we can trace how forces were once borne and where resistance gradually gave way. Weathering and material fatigue make the moment of fracture difficult to predict, while the conditions of instability often develop long before, through construction, use and abandonment. The destruction of a ruin thus discloses a chain of cause and effect that has yet to end. Forces that once held space together are still finding paths of release and redistribution through the remains.

![Fig. 15](manifesto-440-assets/fig-15-a.jpg)

@caption Fig. 15. Following the flow of forces: on the wall of an old factory on Diantai Road, a new antenna guy wire is connected to the existing wall, where traces of cracking are also visible. The photograph prompted me to investigate the relationship between the wire's direction, the anchor point and the cracks. Photograph by the author, 2024; Ruinwork Engineering Database.

Collapse within a ruin is often more than an isolated local event. The failure of one component may affect others through their connections; fragments that crash down may come to rest on new supports as they fall, piecing together a damaged system that still transmits forces. This is the basis of the ruinwright's approach to the flow of forces. A steel cable is no longer merely a straight line on a drawing, but a strand of metal capable of rusting, stretching, slackening and tangling. Once legible load paths rearrange themselves as materials change. Even when the remains temporarily regain equilibrium, their countless connections and the hidden currents of their cracks continue to inscribe a long process of destruction within the structure.

Yet the unresolved tensions in the remains also prompt us to look back at histories still unexplained. Why did the ideals and ambitions that once raised these enormous buildings lose the power to sustain them? Does a fracture stem from a defect introduced during construction, or from the accumulated effects of neglect and environmental erosion? Decisions, errors and accidents of the past have left real material costs, though the remaining evidence may not allow us to trace them fully. A ruin retains another kind of tension yet to be clarified: a crack can preserve evidence of loading, but cannot on its own tell us who bears responsibility for the consequences of decisions, abandonment and mistakes. The structures that have not yet fallen are tethered at one end to histories of construction and abandonment, and at the other hang over a collapse that has not yet occurred.

Ruinwork attempts to bring these two kinds of tension together. Its mechanical transmission devices connect to existing load paths in the ruins. Their construction, use and abandonment have helped shape those relations; the work enables us to trace how they continue to act today. A single vibration cannot tell us an entire history, but following it back through the wreckage that carries it, we can keep asking where this destruction began. The ruinwright pulls at the fragments like a puppeteer, yet remains within the same web of cause and effect; every intervention introduces a new force. The forces borrowed will eventually act upon the work itself, making the construction that seeks to trace destruction part of the very process it investigates.

## Sixth Tenet: The Destruction Specimen

Through calculation, maintenance and continual repairs, architecture provides a period of stability on which life can depend. We rarely have to confront the slow accumulation of fatigue within its components: minor damage is difficult to notice, while a sudden spread of instability may make a safe approach impossible. Between imperceptible change and collapse we cannot safely witness, the way a construction actually proceeds toward failure is often absent from everyday experience. From this absence arises the “destruction specimen”: it reorganizes certain conditions of failure that can be controlled and recorded, bringing prolonged deterioration to an observable scale in a limited, observable form.

Like crash and durability tests in the automotive industry, the destruction specimen incorporates damage and failure into a predetermined process of observation. Such engineering tests normally examine load limits and the consequences of failure under specified conditions to improve future designs. The ruinwright borrows this experimental approach, but redirects it toward a direct experience of destruction itself. Identifying materials, joints, masses and load-bearing relations relevant to failure in a ruin, the ruinwright constructs from expendable materials a body made expressly to undergo destruction. Displacement markers and devices that record deformation and vibration are like sensory prostheses attached to it, enabling us to approach, under safe conditions of observation, changes difficult for the human body to withstand. The specimen bears the fracture in its own body, so that processes abstracted as wear and obsolescence appear once more as actual weight, sound and material injury. What it presents is, first of all, the evolution of this particular construction under given conditions; it cannot replace verification of the failure mechanisms of the original building.

We need such a surrogate outside our own bodies precisely because, within second nature, we have grown accustomed to guarding against failure through calculation, maintenance and renewal, keeping the collapse of order outside everyday experience. The ruinwright therefore borrows the same rational methods once used to maintain stability and builds with his own hands a body capable of bearing destruction. Just as the figure of the android has enabled us to question the boundaries of what it means to be human, the ruinwright uses a body carrying the logic of architectural construction to test the limits of the order sustaining space. We make it stand through rational calculation, and through the same reason lead it toward the threshold of collapse. The forces originally organized to sustain the structure gradually participate in its dissolution as connections loosen and the form loses balance. When the very reason that created order exposes its facade of permanence, the specimen becomes a cruel mirror. What we contemplate in its shattered remains is the finitude that neither human creations nor their creators can escape.[^15]

Ruinwork brings the logic of the specimen into the work, building its eventual disintegration into the work from the outset. Components may loosen through connections deliberately arranged in advance, yet their actual displacement and fall need not follow the path imagined in the blueprint. New points of contact and support may form by chance, and over longer periods plants may grow out of abandoned crevices. These unchoreographed changes allow an experiment that incorporates destruction into its construction to produce unforeseen forms as it falls apart. An eerie and cruel beauty emerges, arising not from destruction itself but from the accidents and life that find room to occur as the old order dissolves. The specimen is carefully built through reason, yet in its disintegration the builder gradually loses complete command of its subsequent evolution. The vibrations and reverberations released from deep within it allow us to experience forces directly, and with them the actual time it takes for destruction to unfold.

## Seventh Tenet: Returning to Liminality

As the building’s former uses recede, the modern ruin resembles an empty shell whose soul has died before its body. An intricate spatial arrangement once meticulously organized by people may become a dark, unfamiliar labyrinth without directions, light or inhabitants. A door no longer marks an entrance; a corridor no longer promises arrival; broken partitions and stairways that end abruptly betray in silence the shelter and safety architecture once promised. Rather than reconstructing the vanished whole, these severed limbs delineate human absence with extraordinary clarity. The wanderer faces a residual structure that has lost its former use but still bears loads. The spatial order once established around human movement loosens, and gravity, erosion and the relations between surviving components begin to govern space more directly. From the perspective of this spatial experience, the ruin seems to display a “posthuman condition,” no longer centered on human beings.[^16]

A ruin evolving on its own exerts an oppressive kind of attraction. The measure of time once given to a building by human activity has yielded to the long transformations of nature, which unfold at a pace our senses can hardly grasp. The ruin catches our eye through traces of former inhabitants, yet bears down upon us with a long, unanswering silence, disturbing the established boundaries between person and space. Architecture no longer busies itself accommodating whoever arrives. The central position we once claimed for ourselves is, on the scale of a ruin, little more than a vacancy briefly left by time. Ornament remains on the walls, while cracks and plants continue to alter spatial boundaries, quietly absorbing all human activity into the site.[^17] It is difficult to reconstruct the once intact space from the remains, and its future form cannot be outlined in advance. The land retains a human-made outline, lingering between former life and present abandonment while nature continually rewrites it. The liminality of the ruin lies in this suspension, unable to return fully to the past and not yet having found a definite place in what comes after.[^18]

Ruinwork brings scattered fragments together in this ruin, drawing our attention back to a space that has lost its former place in life. These fragments still carry traces of the past and forces not yet dissipated, but can no longer fit back into the life they once belonged to. They are reassembled into a strange body, maintaining a grotesque and fragile equilibrium through new connections. The work can neither restore their former functions nor bring back the lives that have left this ruin. From its beginning it has made no promise of permanence; even the form its eventual destruction will take remains open. We do not know what shape it will take in the future, yet still want to build with forces not yet exhausted, attempting through this work destined to grow ever stranger to recover our perception of the ruin.

The ruinwright devotes his efforts to building in this space, then offers the form made with his own hands as a “sacrifice” to the ruin, exiling it into time. At first the strange body seems like a foreign object wedged awkwardly into the site. Yet, placed within the unfinished decay of the ruin, new cracks become entangled with its damaged textures, and the work gradually loses its distinct boundary with the site, becoming a “spatial monster.” It still consists of recognizable architectural elements, but their relations have quietly fallen out of alignment. The more familiar their former uses, the more difficult it is to place the strange form before us.[^19] The fragments retain the warmth of what preceded their destruction, while the body now standing again has begun to undergo changes whose outcome cannot be prescribed. Through observing and waiting, the ruinwright experiences the work gradually passing beyond his control, allowing a time beyond the scale of human life to envelop his senses and oblige him to recognize this familiar space anew.

As this alien body enters the time of the ruin through its own wear, the person still trying to recognize the site by its former coordinates becomes the most untimely intruder. Its form hovers between architecture and sculpture, retaining some architectural dimensions without being large enough to inhabit, or small enough to be an architectural model one could look down on. Stairs still look like stairs, though one's feet can no longer ascend them; walls still divide inside from outside, yet enclose no complete living space. As scale and purpose fall out of alignment, familiar architectural elements gradually no longer correspond to our bodily experience. The viewer can recognize their former functions, but struggles to understand the form before them through former spatial conventions, having to find their own position in relation to the work within this liminal realm of former memories.

## Eighth Tenet: Dissipation into Sound

Fragments are the most visible image of ruins, but our most immediate experience of them often comes through sound. While a building is inhabited and used, slight vibrations at its connections are often masked by the sounds of daily activity, and the structure itself is held in a silence as solid as the building itself. Then a dull crack comes from deep within a load-bearing structure that ought to remain firm, and components that once bore weight silently suddenly disclose their imbalance through an unexpected sound. It violates our expectation that architecture should remain silent. Though its source may be out of sight, the sound reaches our ears ahead of the collapse and is already shaking our confidence in shelter and safety. Through sound, we experience a disquiet I call “civilizational dissonance” reaches the senses: the psychological defenses long maintained by second nature collapse before the building itself does.

This disquiet comes from material changes still unfolding inside ruins. Components rub under compression, strike one another as connections come loose, and release some of their stored elastic energy as they fracture. Echoing drops of water and wind whistling through rooms also leave audible traces of erosion and spatial form. Sometimes a sound breaks after a long silence; sometimes it recurs from a corner beyond sight, making it difficult to tell whether matter has shifted slightly or a larger instability is approaching. The threat of collapse keeps people outside, yet the ruin draws their eyes and ears through sounds whose sources are difficult to identify. We still recognize wind, dripping water and the sound of components rubbing together, but hear them emanating from corridors and rooms that have long since ceased to answer to life. Their familiarity deepens the estrangement, and passersby, conscious of potential danger, still find themselves wondering where the sounds come from.

![Fig. 21](manifesto-440-assets/fig-21-a.jpg)

@caption Fig. 21. “Wind Throat”: an abandoned section of a large ventilation duct on waste ground, with a damper remaining inside. When the wind stirred the surrounding reeds and fleabane, I heard a low rumble from within the duct over the rustling of the plants. Photograph by the author, 2024.

A crack is gone almost as soon as it sounds, leaving a long silence. The listener follows its echo, trying to identify the loose connection from which it came. These intermittent tremors draw attention to changes not yet apparent in the form, and the fading of sound need not mean that changes within the structure have subsided.

Ruinwork builds this impulse to follow sound back to its source into the sounding structures of Folly. New connections transmit barely perceptible displacements and vibrations in the ruin to a ruin instrument, sometimes transforming them into recognizable sound through mechanical transmission or electronic conversion. The ruinwright can arrange how forces are carried and transmitted, but cannot prescribe the sound every instance of friction, impact or fracture will produce. Tension may build at a connection for a long time before a sudden release; other components may find an unexpected point of support as they fall, bringing another unscripted reverberation. The work thus turns the process of disintegration into a performance of its own. As the performance continues, structural wear accumulates, and after some sounds have faded the form can no longer return to its former position. The reverberations that once called to us from deep within the ruins occur again in new forms through this steadily deteriorating construction.

## Ninth Tenet: Blueprints Becoming Scores

From the very first lines of a drawing, ruinwork connects the movement of fragments with the sounds of structural fracture. Lines on drawings marking anchor points, load-bearing relations and suspension points originally enable a construction to stand according to a predetermined arrangement. Yet the work incorporates loosening and falling into its design from the outset, so that the forces holding its form together can later become the forces that break it. Anchor points, axes and dimensions once used to establish stability thereby also point toward future displacements and fractures. Even when there is not a single note on the drawing, the conditions for sound are already embedded in these lines filled with tension.

Conventional plans and elevations can establish the geometry of a building at a given stage, but a single finished drawing can hardly record the long period of displacement, wear and fracture that follows. For a construction destined to keep disintegrating, completion is only a brief state. To trace its changes in time, the drawing needs to accommodate new trajectories and new marks. As actual displacement and wear set in, the drawing sheds its prescriptive role: elevation marks can become references for subsidence, and load-bearing axes help trace movement after connections come loose. Lines initially drawn to fix the form gradually serve to investigate where friction, impact and fracture might produce sound. When cold geometric lines begin to point toward possible fractures and sources of sound, the blueprint finally moves beyond engineering drawing and becomes a graphic score of destruction's rhythms.[^20]

A blueprint can arrange the conditions of sound in advance, but cannot determine when a sound will actually occur. Fragments may not fall along their original trajectories; an unexpected obstruction may delay an impact indefinitely, and an anomalous sound may come from a connection we previously overlooked. The ruinwright has to redraw the site according to its actual displacements, fractures and sound sources. The original lines remain, but no longer account for the wreckage before him. The blueprint gradually changes from a guide for predetermined movement into a record continually revised in response to the site.

The elusive texture of breaking sounds, the shapes of spreading dust and corrosion, the irregular edges of water stains—these sounds and material traces that resist precise notation are transcribed as disorderly marks or layered over existing trajectories, distorting engineering symbols through repeated contamination and redrawing. The lines initially drawn for construction remain on the paper, gradually losing their original purpose beneath successive inscriptions. Chance and disorder once excluded from engineering drawings have also found a place on this sheet.

## Tenth Tenet: The Meaning of Futility

In my own practice, ruinwork draws on the methods of surveying, design, and construction used in architecture and engineering, yet has gradually become an almost solitary undertaking. Much of the time I am alone deep within a ruin, quietly working in response to changes in the materials and the site. The moment I step among the remains, the demands of efficiency that govern daily life begin to loosen. There is rarely a client making demands, or any clear measure of investment and return. My attention gradually narrows to the physical reality of the ruin itself. I build here to give the fleeting process of destruction a tangible form, leading the work toward a carefully choreographed collapse. That collapse may begin with a sudden loss of balance or emerge slowly through erosion and wear, but its seed was planted on the first day the ground was broken. The work gradually borrows the very forces that will bring its structure to failure, drawing it toward a point of disintegration from which it will be difficult to turn back.

When society measures labor by the durability and output of what it produces, pouring one's efforts into a work destined to be destroyed seems profoundly uneconomical, bordering on absurdity. Such a measure quietly favors things that are sturdy, intact, and usable, readily treating breakage, weathering, and disappearance as failures. Ruinwork lingers in the cracks of this system of value, attending to what materials reveal when they lose their appointed uses, and tracing the loads and tensions still held in structures nearing failure. Even labor undertaken without a practical aim has real material costs, and the ruinwright must still decide what is worth building. Yet the meaning of that labor need not depend entirely on the durability or output of the work. Things built with care pass beyond their maker's control after completion, carrying the labor invested in them into time. The physical effort and the years spent doing the work are things no judgment of value can repay.

Knowing that the work will be destroyed does nothing to lessen the effort needed to build it. A form that looks incomplete must still be made to stand; materials must be joined, secured, and made to bear loads. A slight discrepancy at a joint may mean taking apart a section I have already built. Calculations have to be revised as conditions on the site change. Conventional architecture calculates for stability. Ruinwork must also make a form stand, but only for a while, leaving room for its subsequent loosening and disintegration. Maintaining this delicate balance amid the unpredictability of nature, while allowing intended fractures and displacements to emerge through chance, is a particular difficulty of building in this way.

A futility that extends far beyond the building of any one ruin is the time I have spent traveling from one ruin to another. Survey after survey connects scattered remains into a world atlas of ruins, while measured drawings, photographs, and notes preserve records of these changing structures that I can return to later. The work seems to be accumulating a body of knowledge and methods, but what it yields is often only the starting point for the next inquiry. New ruins enter the atlas, and old records need revising as the sites change. I devote a great deal of time and physical effort to a pursuit with no definite endpoint, trying to find a way of understanding and building for things that have long ceased to hold value in everyday life. Within the usual measures of efficiency, achievement, and return, this labor finds little recognition.

Sometimes I feel as though I have invented an entire discipline for modern ruins, one that has no place in the world, and devoted myself to practicing it with the care of a craftsman—building for uninhabited ruins, drawing plans for fleeting remains, and spending, bit by bit, the years I could have given to other work or other ways of living on a craft with no clear end. Yet has the city beneath our feet not also been built by the hands of generations, sustained through endless repair, replacement, and rebuilding? If even the city of the future will eventually become a ruin, where are we to seek recognition for the labor and the lives spent building it?[^21] Our creations may still leave wreckage behind when they disintegrate. The years we have already lived will not return, even if the work survives.

Yet in this work, so often frustrated and seemingly unrewarded, I began to notice another change difficult to explain. A connection that worked on paper might behave quite differently on site as the materials resisted my plans. I would have to stop, look again at constructions I had long taken for granted, and revise my judgment through dismantling, adjusting and starting over. For a long time I sought in ruins a meaning I could scarcely articulate. Gradually I discovered that what I had been searching for might not reside in the remains at all, but had, over these years of contending with materials, begun to enter my own way of knowing things.[^22] Maker and creation thus do not remain entirely separate: in trying to understand the ruin, I have myself been changed by the labor.

The meaning of futility makes room for labor that cannot be accounted for in terms of returns. The experience gained through labor cannot give back the years already spent, nor make the disappearance of the work reversible. Knowing that what we make will disintegrate, why do we keep building? Looking back at the long days spent traveling between ruins, I find the question already entwined with my own life. Ruins have promised me no final answer, and I have already given part of my life to this craft still finding its way. Such labor need not prove its worth by lasting forever. Even if the things I built with my own hands eventually disappear, I still want to return to this ground without guarantees and continue the work before me.

## The Limits of the Ruinwright

Even if different ruins eventually come to look materially similar through weathering, the reasons for their destruction need not be the same. War, engineering accidents and misguided decisions leave particular wounds in buildings; changing uses and prolonged neglect may also cause them to be abandoned prematurely. Nature can slowly wear away the traces of destruction, but cannot turn acts that took place in history into natural phenomena, much less erase responsibility for them. What happens to those displaced by abandonment and renewal? Who will inherit the traces of those who lived and worked here, and who has the right to decide the future of these remains? Such questions do not disappear with new construction. The beauty, sublimity and sorrow the ruinwright feels before the remains are ultimately his own experience of this encounter in the present; those who actually lived through the destruction may feel quite differently. Faced with histories his experience cannot encompass, the ruinwright must retain a sense of reverence.

Ruinwork borrows the perceptions of art and the means by which architecture and engineering organize matter. It can thus establish new connections within a ruin, allowing surviving forces to participate in the formation of a work. Once the ruinwright intervenes in a site, he must recognize the limits of what can be borrowed in materials, space, and time, and consider ownership, the lives of people nearby and private memories that should not be made public at will in the name of creation. A new construction may bear its own failure, but not at the cost of damaging existing ruins or exposing others to danger. Reverence for nature must also find expression in each actual intervention. Plants growing among broken walls do not in themselves demonstrate that the land has recovered from earlier damage, and materials and disturbances introduced by the maker do not cease to exist when the work loses its form. Subject to lawful access, site safety and environmental conditions, the ruinwright should minimize unnecessary disturbance and take responsibility for the actual consequences of each alteration.

The tenets the ruinwright has established for his craft must also undergo repeated revision. Morphemes used to identify remains, calculations for tracing forces and blueprints recording disintegration once offered directions into the ruin. Yet as the site continues to change, these methods repeatedly encounter things they cannot accommodate. If provisional measures of observation are elevated into fixed laws explaining all ruins, ruinwork may establish another second nature upon the ruins, concealing a reality still taking shape beneath an order of its own making. Imagination arising from fragments may go beyond established narratives, but must remain distinct from knowledge drawn from traces and documents. Theory offers a route into the site; it grants no one the authority to look down on it. What remains beyond our understanding is also where this craft can continue to grow.

Here the tenets come provisionally to a close. The methods that once served to identify, build and record ruins must be put to the test the next time we enter a site.

# Ruinwright Statement

The tenets give ruinwork a provisional way to enter ruins, recognize their remains and borrow forces not yet exhausted within them. Yet the impulses that lead us to return to these remains again and again cannot be fully explained by methods of building. Why do ruins fascinate us? Knowing that their disappearance cannot be reversed, why do we still wish to linger, to look and to feel, even to keep building? This inquiry gradually turns the gaze from the ruin toward the person looking at it.

The perception of beauty in ruins may be our first step into this long exploration. The beauty that appears by chance in a broken form often reaches the senses before rational judgment. Yet when we are moved by that beauty, or even take a voyeuristic pleasure in it, might we too readily absorb someone else's loss into our own aesthetic experience? What makes us want to follow the fracture further, rather than be satisfied with a scene already captured by a camera?

The statement that follows begins with that first perception of beauty. It follows the ways ruins continually disturb our senses and understanding, tracing the desires behind our gaze and how these desires have taken shape in modern life. These thoughts arise from experience accumulated in wandering and building, and touch upon a condition of the mind that some of us living today may likewise recognize.

# The Broken Frame — From the Picturesque to the Sublime

As wind and rain wear them down over time, intact buildings lose their sheen; yet where rubble and moss mingle, something fleetingly romantic takes shape. Ruins often first enter our aesthetic consciousness in this gentle guise of the picturesque.

## The Picturesque: A Ruined Scene within the Frame

The picturesque seeks scenes worthy of a picture in the transformations of nature, favoring rough, incomplete things that resist regular form. Time and chance gradually draw these forms away from their original human arrangements, revealing a beauty no one designed. Yet when a form that appears by chance moves us, we long to keep it before our eyes. Painting, photography and garden design have each given such fleeting encounters a form that can be viewed again and again. In seeking and retaining a landscape, an invisible frame gradually comes into being: from a relatively secure distance, we can contemplate scenes full of incompleteness and sudden change, temporarily accommodating within a composition what would otherwise be difficult to grasp.[^23]

The classical ruins we are accustomed to admiring are already forms that have survived centuries of erosion and the decisions of later generations. The picturesque gaze then selects from these surviving remains a scene worth looking at. Destruction left outside the picture recedes from sight as the composition is made. Over time, our image of destruction comes to rest upon these forms selected and preserved from a long course of decay.

Some ornamental buildings in European gardens, known as follies, were built to resemble ruins, arranging broken walls, the aura of antiquity and the appearance of weathering into scenery to be admired on a walk. One need not wait centuries for an actual building to decay in order to have a ruin imbued with the aura of age in one's own garden. Once the image of ruin previously confined to a picture acquires real weight through construction, one seems able to leap across those long years and give a newly completed building the form of something nearing the end of its life. The garden thereby seeks to keep this fleeting picturesque beauty within the scene it has chosen for a long time.[^24]

Yet if we invite a ruin into the garden, the garden must also receive its collapse, its shadows, and its rotting leaves. Ruined walls still bear their own weight, and weathering will not stop to preserve their appearance of age. The forces that mottle walls and give masonry its patina continue to work along cracks in the material, and may eventually undo the very scene we came to admire. In trying to preserve a landscape to which time has given beauty, we sometimes find ourselves continually repairing it to prevent the changes that made it beautiful.

This frame did not disappear with the classical garden. It persists in modern photography and in the way we turn the world into scenery. A camera can condense a long collapse into a visual cross section we may return to again and again. Yet when we grow accustomed to understanding ruins through such still images, we can easily lose sight of the depth of the space, the weight the structure continues to bear, and the changes slowly taking place beyond the picture. Destruction seems to have found its place in the photograph, where we can contemplate it at our leisure, even as the ruin itself has already moved toward another form beyond our gaze.[^25]

Yet the ruin does not always stay within the frame. A storm or a loosening load-bearing joint may wrench it out of the form that once moved us. The remains that had become part of the scenery continue to disappear along their fractures. When the forces that once receded beyond the composition make themselves felt again, we begin to realize that the destruction before us was never truly contained by the picture. Collapse, with all its physical weight, comes crashing through the stillness of the image.

## Folly: Setting the Frame Free

If the beauty that moves us arises in the very process by which things depart from their original forms, why, in a garden built expressly for this transformation, should we restrain once more the forces that gave it beauty at the moment when it is most pleasing to look at? In Folly, wildness once kept beyond the frame has already returned to the interior of the building through cracks in its walls. The space built by the ruinwright for looking upon the landscape will itself be drawn into the landscape's evolution.

Folly begins with this reversal. It borrows its name from the ornamental structures of traditional gardens, but is no longer content to preserve an object’s carcass in a picturesque state of decay for our enjoyment. The ruinwright builds anew with surviving structures and residual forces, incorporating loosening, imbalance and eventual disintegration into the construction. Changes once repeatedly trimmed back in the garden now continue along the connections of the new work, gradually altering its form and even undoing the structures once used to organize those changes. The ruinwright now sets free more than the scenery within the frame. The frame he built with his own hands is itself given over to this unfinished disintegration.

To allow this evolution to continue, the ruinwright builds future failure into the garden. The order given to the work by the blueprint will also be revised through actual movement and wear, so that this carefully choreographed destruction continually produces forms and events the choreography could not have foreseen. The order the ruinwright has given to matter does not simply vanish. Alongside other forces, it continues to participate in the garden's formation and disappearance as the work evolves.

As the garden evolves beyond its drawings, unforeseen forms briefly appear, sometimes existing only between two losses of balance and vanishing before they can be noticed. It becomes increasingly difficult to choose a single moment in this evolution as the finished appearance of the work. The picturesque beauty that once made the ruinwright stop still appears by chance at various stages of the garden. As his gaze follows the forces that bring about these changes of form, however, he begins to encounter a beauty that cannot be accommodated by composition alone.

The tremor of the sublime does not wait for a building to be destroyed. Long before, while these constructions still fulfill their missions, it resides in the intricate workings of industrial machinery, the austere order of building structures and the material forces held in check within them. Cables and frameworks sustain enormous weights; precise transmission mechanisms carry forces through component after component. We can recognize their construction and use, yet the complex relations established by countless materials, calculations and acts of labor exceed what an individual can grasp through the senses. Faced with these carefully organized forces, we feel our own smallness while marveling at humanity's ability, through knowledge and craft, to make things on a scale so far beyond the individual.[^26]

Yet when something once meticulously built by people gradually passes beyond their control through changes of its own, the sublime that once inspired awe takes on an unsettling strangeness. Beams, cables, and mechanical components remain recognizable, but the purpose and care that once kept them standing and in motion have withdrawn. The surviving structures still bear loads; erosion and wear continue along their original connections. We recognize what these things once did, and may still see human ingenuity and intention in their construction, but familiarity no longer makes them behave as they once did. The forces that made us marvel at our ability to build are gradually breaking free of the structural restraints we imposed on them. The sublime changes direction: through our own constructions, we feel once more the limits of what we can do.

The Broken Frame (fig. 30) gives this change a visible form. As the frame itself becomes a ruin, it gradually loses its ability to hold a fleeting scene within a composition. When the scenery it once enclosed recedes, the damaged boundaries left behind become the very object of our gaze. Fractures spread along the frame, and the thing once entrusted with holding the landscape still exposes a body equally incapable of escaping destruction.

We once used this frame to contemplate scenes beyond our control from a relatively secure position, and to sense our distance from the world. Yet when the very boundaries that accommodated these scenes begin to break, we realize that the boundaries that once allowed us to look so calmly at the world also depend upon a finite human-made order. The disintegration of the frame gradually draws our gaze from the scenery within it to the frame itself, then, through its fractures, back toward the viewer who once stood outside it.

## Ruins of the Mind: The Lost Frame

Architecture once sheltered people’s lives with intact walls and familiar paths. Through daily use, second nature assigned things their purposes and places, confirming for us our own position in the world. It is this sense of security that allows us to contemplate ruins at our leisure, leaving destruction for a while within the frame, as though it were far removed from the life we continue to live.

Yet as our gaze moves deeper among the ruined walls, we find that the things that once housed other people's lives are so similar to those that house our own today. The space remains where it was. Things once used with care and cherished have not yet vanished, but have lost their former significance in the unanswering emptiness. We can still recognize what they were used for, yet can scarcely imagine what place they once held in someone's life. Faced with these familiar and forsaken things, we begin to notice that our own certainties about life also rest upon relations that will eventually change. A familiar building becomes a “lost space”: it retains the dimensions of the life once lived there, but can no longer retain the life that gave those dimensions meaning.

In daily life, our minds are constantly responding to the world outside, and time moves forward with the tasks awaiting completion. Many questions about ourselves are temporarily set aside in this recurring pattern. When the explanations attached to a space recede, the psychological shelter provided by functions, narratives and other people also loosens, and it becomes increasingly difficult to evade one's own presence within it. The loneliness of this encounter arises from a real, quiet absence: familiar life has departed, while the space remains. Even if we never lived here, the faintly recognizable traces of living can leave us with a feeling of loss akin to homesickness. We imagine histories we never took part in, and lives that might have happened but never did, yet can never find a complete response among these remnants.

The sublime turns inward when we can no longer keep our distance from this loss. Romantic melancholy for former grandeur may still allow us to place ourselves, for a while, outside the history we mourn. Yet when this lost space touches our own lives, we feel time acting simultaneously upon the ruined walls before us and upon our own bodies. The things that still have weight and warmth, that we cherish so carefully now, may one day recede into an overlooked layer of history; we ourselves are already part of this irreversible disappearance. The heaviness, helplessness and loneliness spread from the broken forms before us to the things we have not yet lost. Sometimes we instinctively clench a hand, as if to make sure something still remains in this moment.

With nothing to rely on, our awareness gradually sharpens. We cannot stop time or bring departed lives back. The voices that ordinarily explain the world to us and give us a place within it have sunk into silence. Yet we can still feel the weight of a fragment, grieve for things that have gone, and be moved by beauty in a form that has appeared by chance. In this almost absolute solitude, the space before us can no longer explain these feelings for us; we have to face their origins alone. And in this silence, it is still “I” who feel and respond.

## A Mirror of the Modern Mind: Phantoms of an Empty Frame

Things that feel strangely close to us after their destruction may hardly have caught our attention when they were intact. With clearly defined purposes and finished surfaces, they slipped into everyday life. Their walls and shells concealed their construction and kept the details of former lives apart from us. When their functions withdraw and their surfaces peel away, hidden components, scratches, and signs of wear begin to come into view. Along these fractures we recognize a belated familiarity. Even if we never took part in their past, we have lived within the same human-made order and are subject to the same matter and time. Their intact appearance once allowed us to pass them by. Broken, they come close to our bodies again, with all their physical weight.

Yet this familiarity brings little peace. We try to find our way through recognizable architectural elements according to the spatial logic we know, only to be stopped by severed paths and rooms that no longer connect as they once did. We keep walking, yet nothing we experience seems to amount to progress. Traces of everyday life occasionally evoke affection, only to awaken fear and loss in the emptiness that offers no response. The ruin remains suspended between intimacy and estrangement. The rhythms that once gave shape to time here have lost their force, while the time of the ruin continues slowly through changes in the material itself. As our movements lose their former bearings, we find ourselves wandering without knowing where we are going.

This wandering has a counterpart in modern life. When a building falls out of the course of development, the uses and relationships that gave it a place in society have been severed, yet its physical body remains, still bearing loads. In much the same way, losing a social identity or a sense of belonging does not free us from the habits we have already internalized. The coordinates by which we once located ourselves in society gradually fail, yet the expectation that life must keep moving forward remains. Even with nowhere to go, we keep looking for a new place for ourselves.

The liminality of modern ruins becomes particularly apparent in this shared experience of loss. To those who have experienced something similar on the margins of modern life, the desolation of a ruin may feel strangely familiar. The old order can no longer accommodate us, and the life ahead has yet to take shape. It is precisely this unsettling suspension that some people return to in search of an aesthetic experience.

Looking back at the rapid development of modern building technology, we were once excited by our growing power over space and poured all manner of desires for the future into building. Yet the things left without a place in the wake of overly rapid construction still hold expectations once invested in space that never found a response in the lives that followed. Born into a landscape already covered by human-made order, we inherited the spaces fashioned by our predecessors and have in turn been fashioned by them through years of living in them. The fascination with liminality and ruin aesthetics may suggest another direction for aesthetic desire: a fascination with the forms familiar spaces take when they depart from their original arrangements, even a wish to dismantle in imagination the order that once gave them meaning. When space no longer tells us where to go, the course of life it once prescribed also seems to loosen. Amid the unease of disorientation, we sense a faint freedom not to follow the old path, even if for now it exists only in imagination.[^27]

The broken beauty of the frame thus becomes a mirror of the modern mind. As we grow fascinated by the deconstruction of these spaces, our gaze gradually turns toward the systems of order that once shaped us through space. We have relied on our position in the world to understand ourselves, and become accustomed to understanding everything around us through its established relationships. Only as these relations loosen do we begin to recognize that much of the self that once seemed self-evident was gradually formed through a long history of playing roles and responding to the world.

Yet even in such nearly absolute solitude, the “I” that responds to itself may not be as pure as we imagine. Beyond the appearances we have gradually adopted so the world can recognize us, what still belongs to us? The fractures of a ruin seem to expose gaps where familiar social identities no longer fit the reality before us, but do not thereby give us back a purer self. The scenery within the frame has receded, while the boundaries that once enclosed it still linger in consciousness. Even when we recognize the apparent self-evidence of order and its limits, we cannot thereby escape the way it has shaped us.

We still live within second nature and even need to draw on its familiar measures to understand the world that is losing its certainty. Desires, fears and impulses we have yet to recognize, once concealed by roles and daily routines, begin to appear along the fractures of disintegration. And the beauty that first drew us to ruins becomes less and less self-explanatory amid this disorientation.

# Images of Ruins — Where Desire Goes

Born into modern society, we have inherited a way of measuring things by order, efficiency and use, internalizing these judgments until they shape the way we look at things almost instinctively. Modern ruins are precisely the fracture left by this logic: once a construction loses its former function and utility, the utilitarian gaze finds nothing to grasp among remains from which it can draw no benefit. It can explain why something was once built and how it was used, but struggles to account for why it is still worth looking at after it has fallen out of daily life. Yet precisely where judgment loses its footing, a moment of beauty may strike us unexpectedly from an exposed fragment or a ragged fracture, making us stop. Following this attraction that has yet to find an explanation, we approach the ruin again and continue along its exposed surfaces, asking where this beauty comes from, and what enables remains long deprived of their former use to stir desires within us that we have yet to recognize.

## The Broken Prism: Looking into the Fracture

Few images evoke ruins more immediately than the fragments that have fallen away from an intact building, and the fracture surfaces left where they parted. These surfaces fascinate us because they expose structural relationships that were once hidden within the whole. Imagine second nature as a prism continually refracting reality: the eye follows the refracted spectrum, while the glass itself remains hidden behind the bright illusion. Institutions and everyday experience perform a similar refraction, translating the complexity of reality into things that can be recognized and managed. In time, we may even mistake this organized image for the world itself. When the prism breaks, the light shifts and scatters across the new surfaces, and the thickness and fragility of the glass itself become visible again. The fractures in ruins likewise interrupt our habitual understanding of architecture, making the conditions that once helped constitute the meaning of things, but passed unnoticed in everyday life, subjects of inquiry in their own right.

This fracture also allows us to question relations previously obscured by a coherent narrative of the city. A building once acquired a distinct identity in response to a particular social need, yet the circumstances of its rise and decline often lay far beyond the influence of those within it. As its former shape and use disintegrate, we begin to examine things we once took for granted. Why was it built, and why was it given this particular form? How did the social needs and ideas that brought it into being change over time, bringing it to its present condition? The broken prism does not give us the power to see everything. It simply brings into our understanding the very medium we once relied on to explain the world, now no longer able to do so.

The fragments thus acquire a beauty that is difficult to place. They can no longer be restored to the appearance of the intact whole, yet they carry the material conditions that once made the whole possible. The absence they make visible allows us to notice how the former appearance came into being and why it could no longer be sustained. The beauty given to us by the broken prism arises from the experience of suddenly being able to contemplate the former boundaries of our understanding.[^28]

## Ruin Romanticism: The Tenderness of Fragments

Looking inward along the cracks, we begin to recognize the tragic character of modern life. The ideals in which builders once placed great hopes may never have been realized before the buildings that held them ceased to be worth maintaining. Other lives did take place there, but no longer find an echo after their inhabitants have gone. We hope that the meaning and beauty invested in our creations will endure, yet some ideals give rise to new tragedies even as they are realized. When losses once hidden beneath an intact appearance finally lie exposed before us, we still feel tenderness for what has broken, and cannot deny the beauty that sometimes emerges along its fractures.

To recognize suffering and still be moved by beauty lends the contemplation of ruins a distinctly modern tragic contradiction.[^29] The losses of the past have not been made good simply because the wreckage has taken on new forms, yet those forms may evoke fear and consolation at once. Perhaps the consolation comes from a loss we once found difficult to face finally acquiring a form we can look upon. Grief long set aside in daily life, sometimes dulled into numbness, finds something to hold on to among the wreckage. What moves us is that we can still sense something of life and beauty in what remains. We know the real weight of this destruction, yet cannot help being moved by what we see, as though the contradictions in our own lives that have been so difficult to put into words were laid bare alongside this broken body.

Perhaps the tenderness evoked by fragments has two destinations. Some of it is for lives that really took place, now only faintly recognizable through material traces; some is for futures that never came to pass, whose possibility vanished with the abandonment of the building. Even if we never lived in these spaces, a surviving ornament or an unfinished room may convey something of the affection and expectation once invested in it, and we may regret a life we never had. Two equally unreachable times remain with us in the present through these fragments. Perhaps this is a curious form of mourning particular to modern ruins: we can even feel the loss of something that never existed.[^30]

In modern life, which continually presses forward, we have learned to accept that things belong to the past before we have had time to grieve their disappearance. What reason classifies as obsolescence, wear, or renewal has not necessarily found a place in our feelings. A ruin, in its state of not quite having ended, gives the mourning we had no time for somewhere to linger. When we discover that we are still willing to stop for things no longer needed, this tenderness gradually folds back upon ourselves. What we cherish is both the world that has gone and our own remaining capacity to be moved by its beauty.

This desire that draws us toward destruction and makes it so difficult to resist approaching ruins might be called a “ruin complex.”[^31] We are fascinated by the merciless way time carves matter, and drawn to the strange forms in which decay and new life become intertwined. Disappearance itself takes on a melancholy, almost irresistible beauty. Mourning gradually seeps into the way we look. The lingering warmth of the lives once lived there, and the grandeur and ideals once invested in the buildings, make us willing to stop for things that have lost their use. Such tenderness cannot reverse disappearance, yet it still seeks to hold on to something of the light not entirely extinguished in the fragments, bringing things forgotten by their own age into our lives again.

Perhaps this is a romantic spirit belonging to modern ruins. It knows that fragments cannot restore a vanished age and no longer expects complete redemption, yet it still seeks beauty in the desolation. Wishes never realized in the past become intertwined with forms that have grown by chance out of the fractures we see today, as though the ruin held both a past once hoped for but never lived and a future we ourselves may never reach. The fragments before us are like scattered lines of verse, still carrying their former resonance, yet no longer bound together by their original poem. As we try to find new connections between these lines, the tenderness that once made us reluctant to touch the fragments begins to turn into a desire to create. With these surviving lines, we want to write the poem that was never written.

## The Fool's Stance: A Utopia of Fragments

When the desire for reconstruction recedes, the ruin remains in an interval of time that has yet to run its course: its walls have neither been drawn back into life nor found their end in collapse. In the continuing tension between grandeur not quite dissipated, wishes left unfulfilled and destruction long delayed, the ruin awakens from a long, neglected suspension and silence, beginning to develop a story and a character of its own. The ruinwright enters in the guise of the Fool. He feels tenderness for the fragments, yet is fascinated by the possibilities they disclose after departing from their former shapes. The folly the ruinwright seeks is an innocence not yet exhausted by the act of seeing clearly.

In approaching a ruin, tenderness, curiosity and voyeuristic pleasure often become entangled. Driven by some unspoken expectation, we try to find what has been concealed among the jumbled fragments. Sometimes we cannot even say what we are looking for. We stop at the sight of a fracture, or cannot help imagining how a strangely shaped fragment might look joined to another piece of wreckage. In this aimless exploration, the images continually arising from the ruin stir our desires; associations we might overlook in daily life often give us the urge to bring these things together before reason has even recognized them.

A desire to create, akin to that of Surrealism, begins to grow here. As established rational order loses its hold over the uses of things, imaginings once suppressed by habit find opportunities to connect seemingly unrelated objects. Sometimes we are moved first by an image that has not yet taken form and only afterward begin to wonder how it might exist in reality. Following this impulse that has long lain dormant among the fragments, the ruinwright tries out connection after connection, borrowing the forces still remaining in the wreckage to give physical weight to forms that previously existed only in imagination.[^32]

Creation here gradually leaves behind the aims of repair and utility. The Fool need not make these fragments fulfill their former duties, nor seek a justification for destruction that has already taken place. He begins to wonder: if destruction is ultimately a fate our creations cannot escape, could the forces that draw them toward disintegration also become a medium of creation? Through the exchange of matter and energy with its surroundings, life can maintain its intricate and delicate form for a while;[^33] within this irreversible evolution the Fool likewise seeks a material body for his wild imaginings. Yet he has no intention of keeping that body forever in the order he has given it. He is willing to compose its subsequent wear and disintegration into the work, allowing the process of disappearance to become part of its poetic expression. An impulse to break established forms thus gradually turns into a desire to create. Since the destruction that has already occurred, and the forces it has set in motion, can already become part of creation, the Fool need not resort to more thorough destruction to satisfy that impulse. He borrows the forces of destruction to build, and through the evolution of the form itself gives poetic expression to finite life.

We cannot, after all, return to a primordial world untouched by civilization. Even when the rules of daily life temporarily lose their force, the memories, experiences, and habits that shape our desires remain part of us. This does not make the impulse to create any less real. Among abandoned buildings we may still, from time to time, sense a beauty to which no meaning has yet been assigned, and create out of that feeling. Perhaps a return to authenticity begins with the willingness to face feelings long set aside, even after we have acknowledged that we can no longer stand apart from this world. We may use the very knowledge and skills that once disciplined us to give these feelings a form with our own hands.

Folly is the physical embodiment of this role.[^34] The Fool sets aside, for a while, the calculation of results and returns. With an almost innocent obstinacy, he begins to build again from the fragments he can borrow. He knows that what he makes cannot withstand time, yet insists on letting something that has never existed stand for a little while, giving it a dignified presence.

With fragments scattered across different times, the Fool may even construct a past that never existed, or a future he himself may never reach. Such a past belongs to his imagination and cannot replace the histories these fragments actually bear; yet it need not follow the original plot of history. Wishes once entrusted to buildings can become intertwined with the ruin's present form in this fictional act of construction, giving rise to a garden its former builders might never have imagined. This is the utopia of fragments the Fool builds among the remains. From the moment it is completed, it bears the traces of destruction and cannot escape the fate of the ruins still disintegrating around it.

The Fool walks toward the precipice, unwilling to let established experience and judgment exhaust the possibilities ahead of him.[^35] His step remains light among the ruins. He knows there is no guarantee beneath his feet, yet he also knows that each piece of rubble may once have borne the weight of a life he can never fully understand. The ruinwright continues to play this role on ground that offers no assurance, earnestly choreographing his wild imaginings on a fleeting stage.

# The Last Sound — A Performance after the Curtain Falls

The Fool who gathered fragments among the ruins has finally given his homeless imaginings a form that stands, if only for a while. Yet when a work has accepted its eventual disintegration from the first day of construction, where does this act of creation leave its maker? The rules that once governed how architecture should be used and kept in existence no longer find a response here. Still the ruinwright measures and builds with care, even arranging an irreversible finale for his creation. This almost mad obstinacy becomes part of the work's performance.

Second nature resembles a theater that has been running for years, where we accept our respective roles and gradually take the actions arranged for us to be life itself. A ruin resembles the backstage area of a deserted theater. The former play is over; the walls and props that once sustained the performance remain, yet there are no longer characters for them to answer to. Here the ruinwright's performance reveals a kind of structural madness. Even after the old roles have lost their resonance and the enactment of order has failed, he chooses to play, in earnest, the part of a builder unconcerned with gains and losses. For a while he resists the impulse of second nature to absorb the unknown into its order and smooth away its strangeness. He defers rational judgment, allowing the fear, the loss of control, and the sense of the sublime provoked by the unknown to linger. In these moments of departure from the old order we draw close to a chaos that has yet to take form. Among the ruins, we discover that the order we once took for granted can itself appear absurd when the reality in which it was enacted falls away.[^36]

## Postdramatic Space: Behind the Stage

Modern ruins often linger in the aftermath of a cycle of construction and renewal. The new city moves forward, while buildings that could not be carried into its next life stay where they were, as though the scenery had been prematurely withdrawn from the stage of history. Past upheavals reside in their fractured surfaces. The ideals and ambitions that once drove construction embodied an age's imagination of future life; yet as times changed, the expectations that had sustained some of those possible lives faded before those lives ever began.[^37]

Unrealized futures, together with histories that have yet to find a response, linger in this theater where the former performance has ended. Viewed in time, the ruin is a theater after the plot: spaces once designed for a particular future have already become relics of an earlier age. Their material lives are not exhausted, and they continue to bear the unsettled consequences of history. A postdramatic view of the time and space of modern ruins allows material events left behind by the narrative of progress to reach our senses again, as if in a belated flicker of life before death.[^38] Mechanisms left by the old order sometimes display a strange vitality once deprived of their former coordination, only to move toward deeper decay as each wears down. They remain entangled in the same theater, yet no longer enact a single story written in advance.

When we look upon a ruin as the form a building takes after its original purpose has ended, we may follow the surviving components and rediscover material relations no longer contained by an established use. Architecture was built through the human arrangement of materials and the meanings assigned to them. In disintegration it gradually loses the images and signs that once established its identity, lingering in the very heart of civilization as a carcass difficult to accommodate within its former order, openly tearing the seemingly intact surface of civilized order. The ruin now resembles the backstage area of a theater opened to the audience after the performance. As the former plot recedes, viewers gradually depart from the roles the space once assigned them. Things that the audience never needed to notice begin to occupy the stage, and architecture's own unfinished material evolution steps out of the background of human activity to become the subject of this theater.

## Disintegration into an Instrument: A Performance through Deconstruction

If silence itself is understood as a directive imposed by order, then architecture as we inhabit it in daily life resembles a character required to remain silent. We are accustomed to the solidity of the slabs beneath our feet and the columns and beams beside us, as though these enormous material bodies need only bear their loads in silence. Yet when a dull grinding sound or a crack from deep within a support suddenly reaches us, architecture steps out of its role as scenery. The anomalous sounds of the ruin gradually undo this convention of silence, abruptly shaking our confidence in shelter and safety.

The earlier silence of inhabited architecture owes much to the way its order has long restrained the natural vibrations of matter. The ruin enters disintegration through one anomalous sound after another; ruinwork attempts to reorganize this departure from order, so that the new construction gradually takes the form of a disintegrating instrument as it loses balance and falls apart. An instrument, too, organizes vibration through relations between materials, allowing certain sounds to be recognized repeatedly through tuning and performance. Extending this relation to architecture, the order sustained by function and construction resembles a theory of music by which matter is organized. As that order gradually fails, the conditions of sound continually change with the wear of materials. By some accident, architecture seems to acquire the qualities of an instrument backstage, beginning a performance that was never composed for it.

At the site of a ruin, the ruinwright tries to bring this intermittent, barely perceptible performance of anomalous sounds into his own work, so that the mechanical structure originally used to transmit and organize vibrations also alters the way it produces sound as wear and imbalance accumulate. The performance of a ruin instrument thus presents both the condition of a structure at a particular moment and the gradual failure of its original relationships of tuning through continued wear, enabling us to hear the disintegration of the order that shapes its sound. Out of this precisely designed mechanical structure come increasingly harsh and disordered sounds, giving the absurdity revealed in the breakdown of order an audible form we can experience directly. The performance resembles an auditory dissection of the death of a building: the very relations that once made its order possible are now taking part in its dissolution.

## From Detuning to Return: The Spent Note

Looking back through this fracture, the very term “musical instrument” implies two kinds of discipline. The “musical” part organizes the vibrations of matter into recognizable timbres and rhythms through habits of listening; the “instrument” keeps these vibrations within relations that can be repeated over time. As the order maintaining these relations slackens and fails, pitches and timbres may depart from what is expected. An instrument that has lost its original tuning can still sound, but may no longer answer to the familiar expectations of the performer.

So it is with ruins. If we can imagine a building as an instrument kept continuously silent, the anomalous sounds it gradually makes in ruin resemble a performance that is constantly going out of tune. Hearing a scrape as a sign of failure, or classifying a vibration that fails to meet our expectations as disturbing noise, still owes something to our memory of intact architecture. These are neither the sounds of former everyday life nor the familiar quiet of home. Though the ruin gradually returns to nature, it still carries the material order left by second nature. The old construction continues to shape how natural forces are transmitted and sounds formed. Thus, although these sounds have departed from the score we gave architecture, we still find it difficult to listen as we might to wind in the woods or running water, relinquishing the question of meaning and simply attending to the grace of natural sound.

A sound does not, in the instant it departs from our listening habits, enter a realm of absolute authenticity untouched by language and experience. We still instinctively try to locate its source and associate the sound of a malfunction with danger precisely because our former habits of listening linger in our minds. As ruination continues, sounds gradually depart from the delicate boundaries maintained by listening habits and systems of tuning. Through continual wear, architecture sheds the form imposed upon it by people, making us realize that what we once considered fixed forms are still subject to the continuing transformations of matter itself.[^39] Yet it is this unexpected departure that briefly draws us out of our established experience of listening. In a predictable performance, the mind often draws on past experience to anticipate the next note. An anomalous sound interrupts this rehearsal of the future, abruptly drawing our senses back to the vibration taking place. This is what I call the “absolute present”: reality momentarily breaks away from the form experience has prescribed for it, and we have to recognize anew the material event that is occurring.

The performance of a ruin as a disintegrating instrument is always full of suspense. A connection expected to loosen may not happen for a long time, while another anomalous sound suddenly breaks out after a long silence. As we try to follow the sounds to understand the changes in the structure, our predictions of the next sound repeatedly fail. Listening is thereby drawn into the time of the ruin itself. Between sound and silence, we await the next vibration that has yet to occur, and may encounter the moment when the structure finally tears apart, cutting through the present. Yet whenever sound gives way to silence again, we cannot tell whether the sound we have just heard was already the last note of the performance.

## Collapse at the Threshold: A Self-Destructive Finale

As the performance draws to a close, the remaining supports approach the threshold of failure. The long suspense the listener has experienced between sound and silence is finally interrupted by a last fall. The form that could previously still be sustained in the imagination has become an irretrievable past. The destruction of the work brings the fissures of failing order and fractured narrative to a physical scale we can directly feel, turning an otherwise abstract finitude into an event we witness ourselves. The body now losing its own form casts light upon the ultimate limits we ourselves cannot escape.

When the last precarious support fails, the disintegrating instrument built by the ruinwright's own hands sounds its final dramatic note as its structure comes apart—the “self-destructive last sound.” The instant the note sounds is also the instant the structure that enables it to sound gives way. At the moment of its birth, the note loses the possibility of ever being sounded again by this body, becoming an unrepeatable material event. It is also a sudden exposure of the instrument, performer and conventions of the stage: precisely as music approaches its end, it reveals the material body on which it has always depended.[^41] The weight of this note comes from an almost mad act of surrender—the instrument stakes the remainder of its material life on this final sound. The ruinwright once gave his shapeless imaginings a material body and a finite life; in the last performance, that body finally completes its expression through its own disappearance.

The ruinwright knows that former roles no longer correspond to their settings, yet with the seriousness of a builder he composes a new performance among uninhabited remains, in a backstage area without an audience. By conventional measures of value, this decision to stake the work itself has an almost mad resolve, recalling the extreme theatrical image of lovers bound to an irrevocable ending: in the performance composed by the Fool's own hands, an affection that can find no eternal guarantee in the outside world takes on a tangible, irreversible cost.[^42] This cost cannot establish the truth of a belief, but lends actual weight to feelings otherwise difficult to measure. When the final expression of something built with such care coincides with the disappearance of its form, preservation, endurance and return—values long taken for granted—reveal the limits of their explanatory power. What I call the “beauty of fatal devotion” lies between this lucidity and madness: the Fool knows that he cannot promise eternity to the work he cherishes, yet still wishes to entrust it with such genuine feeling and a portion of his finite time.

Yet when the collapse he has so carefully choreographed finally reaches its end, the ruinwright gradually realizes that this destruction is not enough to put him outside the stage. He once sought through the disintegration of his work to expose order's claim to permanence, but in the finale arranged by his own hands he also sees his own limits. He can create the conditions for a fracture, yet cannot determine the ultimate course of materials; and this act of building that sought to break away from established order itself depends on the knowledge and skills acquired within that very order. Even his imagination of freedom bears the traces of that world. For a time, the ruinwright used destruction to resist the wearing away of belief by reality. Only when the work actually loses its form does he realize that destruction cannot, after all, guarantee any belief forever.

When the last sound fades and the stage and instrument fall silent together, the land remains, along with the wreckage of what he made with his own hands. The old roles have long since left the stage. The role the ruinwright chose for himself loses its footing with the disintegration of the work, yet he is still standing there. The time spent on the work cannot be recovered, and the remains before him cannot answer the questions with which he began. Yet in this situation that never offered any guarantees, he did freely choose to build; he did experience, so vividly, the birth and departure of something he cherished. How, then, is the freedom briefly found among ruins to be brought back into the world that carries on? When we no longer have such a theater in which to play our parts, how are we to face ourselves, equally finite and equally imperfect?

# Epilogue — After Ruinwork

The curtain has fallen. The work remains among the wreckage, but its maker must return to the world that carries on as usual. The freedom and joy found through building and disintegration did not vanish with the last fracture, yet they cannot fill the emptiness that washes over us after the performance. We sought through destruction to expose order's claim to permanence, and saw the same absurdity and finitude in our own work. Yet when the forms with which we once resisted the world finally disintegrate, the world has not changed with them. We remain products of this world, carrying what we have inherited from it as we continue to live within its order.

Should the ruinwright insist on remaining among the ruins in the face of this emptiness, his fascination with decay may gradually turn toward nihilism. The solitude that once allowed us to face ourselves may become a condition in which we refuse to reconnect with reality. When the familiar world can no longer convince us, destruction can easily be mistaken for the most honest response to falsehood. Once only the breaking of established forms can make us feel something real again, the next collapse always seems to need to be more thorough than the last. Over time, the demand for destruction may harden into a habit we can no longer break. If we continually demand that things break in order to escape order, what we ultimately obey may be another prison reinforced by our own hands.[^43]

Beyond this cycle of destruction, ruins still retain the beauty that first drew us to them. Looking back at components I once joined with my own hands, now scattered among the rubble, I may better understand why I first felt tenderness toward other people's fragments. Knowing in advance that something will disappear does not mean we need feel no sorrow when it goes. The wishes once invested in it do not lose their weight simply because we have come to recognize its limits. We can still grieve for departed lives and stop before beauty that emerges by chance among fragments. This feeling does not require another, more thorough act of destruction to sustain it.

Perhaps we can return to a kind of unadorned authenticity only after recognizing this. We once sought to strip things in ruins of the appearances given to them by human-made order, searching for a purer authenticity of matter, only to discover that even the gaze with which we observe the world has long been shaped by second nature. We cannot entirely shed these experiences and memories, much less return to a primordial world untouched by civilization. Yet as we come to recognize the complexity of the world and acknowledge that we cannot exempt ourselves from its contradictions, we can still open ourselves to what is before us, as we did at first. That innocence has not grown more distant as our understanding has deepened. It now carries the hesitations, tenderness, and self-knowledge we have since acquired. Even knowing that a thing will eventually lose its form, we can approach it and feel the fleeting but real changes it undergoes.

Our bodies, our lives, and our capacities have limits, and we need not evade them. Precisely because our time and existence are finite, we can choose for ourselves where to spend them. Looking back over the years I have spent wandering and building among ruins, I cannot retrieve that time, and the works may leave behind little by which to measure it. I still want to understand ruinwork as a craft of “giving beauty to imperfect destruction”: faced with damage already done and impossible to reverse, I borrow forces not yet exhausted to make something that has never existed stand for a while, then return the form I gave it to time, knowing it will eventually depart. This beauty changes the way I encounter destruction, but it cannot repay the losses others have suffered. The wild imaginings I once entrusted to fragments have, through these fleeting forms, also become part of the reality we could experience for ourselves. Even if this act of creation could not make the world perfect, and I still cannot transcend reality, the materials I touched with my own hands, the years I spent on site, and the beauty I felt among broken things have all been real in my life.

After leaving the ruins, the world is still complex, and I am still small and imperfect. The unanswered questions will continue to unfold with the next journey and the next act of building. And I will still want to stop before the things that awaken my curiosity and my sense of beauty.
