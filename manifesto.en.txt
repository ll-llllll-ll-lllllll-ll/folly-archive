# Preface — After Architecture

Architecture shapes our lives almost imperceptibly. Long before we come to understand space, doors, windows and walls have already become common sense to us, and we have learnt to inhabit the spaces between them. Since industrialisation, buildings, technological infrastructure and institutional rules have become increasingly intertwined, forming an environment that is kept in continuous operation — what I call “second nature”.[^2] We are born into it, work and build within it, and seek shelter in it. In time, we come to mistake a world that has been designed for the way the world has always been.

As second nature expands, we use rational calculation and technology to bring more of the uncertainties and dangers of the natural world within the reach of prediction and intervention. There are good reasons for this pursuit of certainty: time and again, our ability to control our surroundings and anticipate the future has enabled us to survive. Yet as the means of maintaining stability gradually become the measure by which life itself is arranged, reason begins to prescribe how the world ought to appear, having once helped us understand it. In these increasingly elaborate arrangements, there may be less and less room for the accidents of trial and error, or for things to grow of their own accord.

As we entrust so much of our lives to second nature, keeping the system stable acquires a legitimacy that scarcely needs defending. The separation we established between ourselves and nature for our protection can gradually become a boundary that confines us in turn. Rational systems continually repair their own damage and failures, concealing their inherent fragility beneath an appearance of constant upkeep, until the order we have made seems indestructible. Caught in a web of our own weaving, we grow accustomed to understanding and arranging life according to its rules, and find it increasingly difficult even to imagine what might lie off the prescribed course.

And yet, ruins appear.

Ruins emerge along the cracks that have long been accumulating in the shadows of civilisation. They take shape wherever an order can no longer sustain itself. As weathering strips away the surfaces of a building, the damage and vulnerability concealed beneath its once solid appearance come into view; the decisions behind its construction and abandonment may also leave their costs inscribed upon what remains. The very yardstick by which life was organised, value assessed and resources distributed is now applied to the spaces we ourselves have made, deciding which buildings deserve further care and which are to be exiled into time to disintegrate on their own. In ruins, the costs that once found little place in the narrative of progress acquire a form that remains on the ground.

Looking back from a ruin, our gaze, once carried forward by civilisation's narrative of progress, can linger for a moment on the wreckage left behind. If we turn our backs to the future and face everything that has already happened, we see discarded objects and stillborn ideals behind all those resounding achievements. Yet the wind we call “progress” continues to drive us forward, piling debris at our feet and behind us.[^3] When such costs are readily accepted as necessary losses in the course of development, the road of modernity inevitably becomes strewn with ruins.

## Modern Ruins: The Strata of Progress

The road of modernity stretches ahead, while the land along it accumulates the traces left in its wake. Housing, industrial facilities and roads overlap to form a landscape utterly familiar to modern life. The New Topographics photography that emerged in the latter half of the twentieth century turned its attention from romanticised natural scenery to these ordinary landscapes, recording with a cool, restrained eye the marks that human construction had left on the land. These photographs reveal precisely what we have grown accustomed to in second nature: human-made order has already profoundly altered the landscape, and the alterations themselves often pass unnoticed in the familiar background of everyday life.[^4]

Yet there are no walls on either side of the gate to separate the city from the wilderness. Buildings loom faintly through the distant fog, and this solitary gate already seems capable of dividing space for us and assigning different destinations to the road. Photograph by the author, 2024.

The forms and load-bearing relations of enormous ruined structures remain on the ground. They once housed the workings of intricate technologies and social missions, yet those who look upon them may find it difficult to imagine the lives that unfolded inside. When the camera turns from facilities still in use to modern ruins, the losses passed over so readily in accounts of construction and renewal begin to acquire a recognisable outline. We are accustomed to treating the withdrawal of a building from use as the cost of development, and seldom ask why it was abandoned before its material life was exhausted, or who bears the consequences. Once a thing is pronounced obsolete, the labour and life that once gathered around it can disappear from discussions centred on utility and returns. In time, this becomes a habitual failure to see.

Meanwhile, modernity continues down its road, and the things left behind accumulate as construction and renewal accelerate. Modern technology has continually increased our capacity to transform the environment; expectations of efficient land use and functional renewal have risen accordingly, though the resources invested do not necessarily correspond to actual needs. Some places still lack essential housing and public facilities, while costly buildings of questionable usefulness continue to appear elsewhere. A great deal of material, labour and aspiration may be invested in something that loses its reason to be used and properly cared for before it has even had a chance to participate fully in life.[^5] In this imbalance, the social life of a building sometimes ends before its material life, leaving the landscape of modern civilisation studded with structures whose bodies survive long after they have been withdrawn from the context of living.

There is something glaringly untimely about these modern ruins that have yet to reach the end of their material lives. Their outlines, components and traces of use are still clear, but the lives that once took place around them have withdrawn. They linger beside the prosperity in which we live, unable to find their way easily back into the complex relations of society, or to find a place in new experiences and collective memories. They often die too young: before they have had time to become ancient, they already face demolition and erasure once more.[^6] This premature death makes it difficult to accommodate them as a melancholy or magnificent spectacle of history. The lingering traces of life and the material changes still taking place leave a long aftershock upon their surviving forms.

The historical ruins we can recognise today may represent only a fraction of all that has ever been destroyed. Some survive in recognisable architectural form, like the Parthenon after centuries of alterations, damage and repairs. Others, like Yinxu, long ago lost the appearance of their original buildings, yet have re-entered history through sites, foundations and excavated objects. Still others, such as the Old Summer Palace in Beijing, became important places through which later generations reckon with history and memory after their destruction in war. Their survival owes as much to subsequent repair, reuse, rediscovery and historical judgement as it does to materials and environment.[^7] Countless ordinary things that once existed alongside them may already have vanished through erosion, demolition and successive cycles of building, leaving us no means of recovering even their ruined appearance or the lives they once held. Those ruins that did not survive, together with their unfinished histories, have receded into a past beyond our reach.

Looking back at the ruins that have survived, we begin to realise that many modern ruins beside us are at a moment when their fate remains undecided. With ancient sites, we rarely have the opportunity to witness how an ordinary building became a ruin, or how it subsequently lost the last of its form. Modern ruins bring this passage towards disappearance within the span of our own lives. The lives and wishes they once held may vanish with them before they have even entered the memory of future generations.

The dust of history has not yet settled on modern ruins. They were abandoned not so long ago, and many remain embedded in familiar neighbourhoods and surroundings. Their proximity is temporal and spatial, but it also touches our understanding of our own lives: those things for which there is now no place were once part of the same second nature we inhabit today.

Surviving ruins overlap with buildings still in use, infrastructure and traces of living, gradually forming a stratum made by our own acts of construction, use and abandonment — the “human stratum”.[^8] This landscape preserves the material forms left by human building, while continually revealing which creations are allowed to endure and which recede into oblivion as new arrangements of life emerge. To me, in this landscape of overlapping layers, a ruin resembles the form that absent time has grown upon the ground: a history I never took part in, a hometown that no longer coincides with memory, yet which still remains in the world through its surviving body. The fragments and broken narratives cannot bring the past back, but they allow me, at least, no longer to be absent from its disappearance.

## Ruinwork and the Ruinwright

I have always felt a tenderness for prematurely abandoned modern structures that I have never quite known where to put. They once embodied people's labour, lives and aspirations, and the histories and memories attached to them may find no resting place when the buildings disappear. This tenderness makes me reluctant to leave ruins solely in photographs and acts of remembrance. I return to them again and again, following fractures that have yet to heal, asking how the remains before me came to be as they are, and how we might still respond to them.

![Figure 4a](manifesto-435-assets/fig-04-a.jpg)

![Figure 4b](manifesto-435-assets/fig-04-b.jpg)

@caption Fig. 4 (a–b). “Broken-Wing Ground”: the former Meigs Field airport, now Northerly Island Park, Chicago. During my years as a student, I often came here alone to spend time. It gradually became the place in the city to which my heart was most attached, a kind of anchorage. After the airport closed, the runway was broken up and the land once used for take-offs and landings gradually returned to a grassland landscape. This accidental posture did not last. As the fragments loosened, the tree too fell among the ruins. Photographs by the author, 2023.

Tenderness for these remains, however, cannot in itself tell us what should be done with them. Demolition and removal entail further material expenditure. Repair and reuse may prolong the life of the materials and labour already invested, but need not find a place for the lives and memories dispersed by abandonment; preserving every ruin as it stands may, in turn, dress real suffering up as scenery for others to admire. These contradictions gradually led me to realise that the questions left by ruins extend well beyond architectural form.

I once tried to follow the conventional paths of architecture and engineering, looking for workable solutions to problems of use, maintenance and the environment. I gradually realised that architecture can help improve actual conditions through repair, design and technology, but cannot on its own determine how resources are distributed or whose lives deserve continued care. My former wish to arrive at a satisfactory answer through a building proposal repeatedly found nothing to grasp in the face of these relations. I began to ask questions that design had not previously required me to answer directly: why do we keep building, renewing and abandoning spaces, and how do the things we make in turn shape our lives, perceptions and memories?

When I returned to my hometown after nearly ten years away, the familiar city had changed through continuous demolition and reconstruction. Between old buildings not yet entirely demolished and new ones never completed, I recognised scenes familiar from my childhood, when I watched the city expand. What disoriented me was that I was only in my twenties, yet the spaces that had formed the memories of my life already had to be rediscovered as though they belonged to a distant history. The streets and buildings were still clear in my memory, but it was difficult to find the spaces on which those lives had once depended. In just ten years, even the years I had lived through seemed to have become a past in need of investigation. This feeling brought to mind solastalgia, a kind of homesickness at home: even after returning to the land of one's hometown, the things that once made it feel like home have been disappearing in place.[^9] As a child, I had taken these recurring scenes of building, stagnation and disappearance for granted. Looking again after my architectural training, I began to recognise their structural and spatial relations, and became more concerned with how these constructions had housed people's lives and why, in so few years, they had lost their reason to be needed. This gradually became the reason I kept returning to ruins.

![Figure 5](manifesto-435-assets/fig-05-a.jpg)

@caption Fig. 5. Returning to the ruins of my hometown: a low building stands on vacant ground between fields and roads near an old cement factory. Housing and a communications tower remain visible in the distance. Former industrial land and ongoing everyday life meet within the same view. Photograph by the author, 2025.

Walking again into these decaying structures, I began to pay attention both to the supports and tensions that had not yet dissipated among the rubble and to the traces of life buried beneath it. Surveying allowed me to trace, from the material fractures, how these constructions had come to their present state; their predicament made it impossible for me to remain satisfied with my former wish to answer everything through a building proposal. I tried instead to begin with surviving traces and material relations still at work, practising the craft of building I had learnt after the architecture itself had withdrawn from life, in order to approach once more the histories and unfulfilled wishes concealed beneath the rubble.

Where ownership and site safety permit, new constructions borrow existing supports and tensions, so that changes brought about by weathering and forces still at work within a ruin pass through newly established connections into the work. The slow and scarcely perceptible process of becoming a ruin thus acquires a form that can be observed and heard; in bearing these forces the new construction itself wears away and becomes part of that process. Through these actual movements and lingering sounds, I hope that people will look beyond the scene destruction has left behind, following the changes still taking place into the ruin itself, towards the lives it once held, its present predicament and the material life it has yet to exhaust. I call this practice, which takes place after architecture and explores the disappearance of our creations through the act of building, “ruinwork”. Folly is the first concrete form it has taken.

“Ruinwright” is a role I have gradually found for myself through years of entering, surveying and building in ruins. The name corresponds to no established professional qualification, nor is it adequately described by any one discipline. It comes closer to the role I have assumed as an independent researcher working across the boundaries of art, architecture and engineering. The wild ideas that found no place in conventional building could finally be put to the test in these surviving spaces. My investigations and constructions found a place to take root, and it was only here that I dared to call myself a wright, continuing to explore a craft still growing wild among the ruins.

I have chosen to write these observations in the form of a free, exploratory essay — an ensaio. This Manifesto of the Ruinwright does not seek to follow the conventions of an academic paper or formal research report. Field notes, research across disciplines and personal impressions intersect as the thinking unfolds, so that the writing itself becomes an ongoing intellectual adventure. I am willing to put these thoughts down for now, as one might erect an unfinished structure in a ruin, bringing scattered experiences into relation while leaving enough room for future revision. The questions that remain unanswered will give me another reason to return.

# Tenets of the Ruinwright

When I decided to write down principles for ruinwork, my thoughts turned first to architecture's ancient tradition of building. Over centuries, we have accumulated knowledge of how to build, how to make buildings sound and useful, and how to preserve their form and beauty. Yet we have never been able to foresee entirely how natural changes, shifts in human affections and changes in society will alter the fate of what we make. The civilisation of building continues to advance, leaving behind one architectural carcass after another whose material life is not yet exhausted. When a thing loses its original use and care, how should the very knowledge that once brought it into being face the rest of its life? As an inheritor of this craft, I want to begin with the remains left behind and write a few of the laws that have yet to be established for architecture after failure and abandonment. These principles govern first of all how I enter, observe and alter a site; they grant the ruinwright no right to dispose of ruins at will or to tell other people's histories on their behalf.

To this end I deliberately borrow the solemn form in which The Ten Books on Architecture organised knowledge and established standards for the craft of building, to write the Tenets of the Ruinwright.[^10] It is a somewhat irreverent homage: I still use the knowledge and tools inherited from architecture and engineering, but take them towards the very failures and disintegration they were intended to resist, testing the measures by which buildings are judged and even overturning those that no longer apply to ruins. There is something absurd, too, about drawing up a code for an office nobody has conferred upon me. Ten is a limit I have deliberately drawn, borrowed from the familiar count of the fingers of both hands. With this simple, slightly ceremonial number, I gather what I have learnt through years of practice into ten principles to be remembered, consulted and revised.

![Figure 7](manifesto-435-assets/fig-07-a.png)

@caption Fig. 7 (a–j). “The Broken Frame”: ten successive emblems for the Tenets of the Ruinwright, arranged in order of the frame's progressive loss and fracture. Drawings by the author, 2025.

These laws have been distilled from repeated surveys and acts of building. Some have already taken shape in the particular methods of Folly; others await the test of further practice. The tenets begin with entering a site and recognising its remains anew, before turning to borrowing surviving structures and forces, and scoring the reverberations of disintegration into ruinwork. As a construction finally approaches its own ruin, the question turns from materials and structure to the builder: knowing that what we make will eventually disintegrate, why do we continue to build? Every building has its time after architecture. The tenets that follow offer a provisional measure for a craft still finding its way, to be questioned and revised in practice.

## First Tenet: Ruin Lexicology

As second nature expands beyond what any individual can perceive and understand, the everyday spaces organised by buildings and infrastructure quietly become part of life long before we understand what lies inside them, even prescribing the spatial experiences we take for granted. We know these spaces well, but rarely understand the construction hidden behind their walls and shells, or the complex rules that keep them operating. In time, we mistake familiarity for understanding. Only when a building's appearance of solidity breaks apart do the boundaries that once demarcated its “territories” begin to lose their original spatial roles. The ruinwright gains another vantage from which to recognise spaces once taken for granted.

On entering an unfamiliar ruin, its former name and scattered historical accounts can still identify what it once was, but scarcely explain what is happening there now. If we enter the structure into a narrative of the past simply by appending “ruins” to its former name, the structures still bearing loads, sinking and breaking apart before us easily recede into a static image. The language of ruins draws the gaze back to their present continuous tense, naming the conditions observed at a particular moment so that they can be revisited. I call this method of identifying, naming and continually revising the conditions of ruins on site “ruin lexicology”. These names coexist with the structures' original names and histories, like miniature archives of field experience.

To situate this ongoing disintegration, one must confront the sense of weightlessness that arises when a building's former spatial common sense begins to fail. When architecture betrays its promise of shelter, walls and slabs that once seemed solid can become latent threats. Familiar paths are cut off by rubble, and neither the floor beneath one's feet nor the structure overhead offers reassurance; one must reassess one's route. Yet it is precisely in this wasteland, stripped of its former guarantees, that the ruinwright, through years of wandering and surveying, gradually develops the “eyes of the wasteland”: an ability to notice signs of instability concealed within the remains, in cracks, corrosion and traces of erosion, and to stay almost instinctively alert to changes that have not yet occurred. This perception, honed through repeated surveys, enables the ruinwright to look beyond a building's former appearance and use, towards the erosion its surviving structures are undergoing and the disintegration that may lie ahead.

With the accumulation of journeys and observations, things initially called simply “ruins” began to distinguish themselves by their different inclinations, weathering, water levels and cavities. When their differences became too numerous for one general noun to hold, new words grew out of the sites themselves. I gradually gathered these into an index of ruin lexicology, organising morphemes according to the terrain of a ruin, its recognisable architectural parts, its present physical condition and the traces of change left by natural forces. They can be combined, but must also be revised in response to new erosion and fractures, lest a ruin still in motion be mistaken in writing for a specimen.

Over the years, ruin lexicology has brought structures scattered across the world into relation with one another. Remains that resist ordinary spatial understanding gradually disclose their affinities through an expanding vocabulary, coming together in an ever-growing atlas of ruins across the world — Ruin Atlas. Though they differ in form, origin and cause of destruction, a similar predicament appears repeatedly: the relations that once sustained a space have loosened, while the construction continues to decay in place. When these distant remains are connected within a single atlas, something akin to the tremor of the overview effect begins to emerge: the human-made orders that seem almost self-evident in daily life can also slowly dissipate from their own creations.[^11] Such a shift in perspective is not enough to explain the particular history of any ruin, but it allows us to look back from these disordered constructions at the world we still inhabit.

## Second Tenet: An Archaeology of Things Close to Us

After a building loses its function and maintenance, it does not immediately revert to anonymous matter. In modern ruins this predicament is especially striking: decay and abandonment happen to things close at hand, and objects fall out of daily life before they have grown old. Function and sign, once interrelated, gradually separate in abandonment. Some components remain, yet it is no longer possible to infer their former use; elsewhere, names, signs and forms remain legible, while the relations of use once organised around them have dispersed.[^12] Yet they still bear the patina and wear of living, evoking a familiarity we readily recognise even when it is not part of our own lived memory. These things close to us hang awkwardly between everyday waste and cultural heritage, having lost the context that once made them self-explanatory. They are not far removed from us in time, but forgetting has already made the life that has barely passed accessible only through something approaching archaeology.

![Figure 11](manifesto-435-assets/fig-11-a.jpg)

@caption Fig. 11. Things close to us: an old chair in an abandoned corridor, with ferns growing through the remaining cotton and foam of its cushion. Its outline still suggests its former use, while the plants are already altering its present material condition. As for who once sat here, the traces do not suffice to tell us. Photograph by the author, 2026; Ruin Atlas · Relic Archive.

This nearly archaeological gaze must begin by identifying the present condition of an object. Patina and wear record previous use, while cracks, rust and plant growth continue to alter those traces. The ruinwright follows the clues that remain recognisable into the past, trying to understand how things once intimately connected with daily life came to be abandoned. And those aspects that can no longer be identified are also part of the ruin before us. Looking upon these incomplete things thus entails a restrained respect: an acknowledgement that those vanished everyday lives really existed, and that our knowledge will never encompass them in their entirety.

The ruinwright may continue to build within a ruin, even reconnecting components long separated, but cannot thereby repair the lost relationship between objects and their former lives. If, with limited knowledge and good intentions, one fills in gaps in the story that cannot be verified, the weight of the ruin's own silence may be covered over by cheap sentiment. Ruinwork restrains the impulse to supply the missing lives, letting the work recede to the very fractures of narrative and matter. It continues along the materials and relations that survive, so that irrecoverable traces of life can enter perception again through forms still changing before us. The work leaves an “anchor in space and time” at which one may stop and look back from the fracture of the present towards those who once used, cherished and eventually left this place, and towards a past that cannot be one's own to possess.

## Third Tenet: Borrowing from Ruins

In the gardens and landscaped spaces of many modern cities, boundaries, pruning and borrowed scenery make nature accessible and pleasing to look at on a human scale. Such untroubled viewing often depends on shelter and continuous upkeep. Once the structures maintaining the boundaries begin to fracture, the nature once framed beyond the window seeps indoors through the cracks. Rain erodes walls, roots prise masonry apart, and the nature once viewed as scenery begins to occupy and reshape the very space from which it was viewed.

“Borrowing from ruins” arises from this reversal. Following the paths by which nature returns to a space, the ruinwright identifies the remains anew and borrows the forces once tamed and excluded by the garden's order, turning them upon architecture and the human will behind it. Through the broken skeleton, the histories and aspirations once built up layer upon layer begin to reveal their limits. The people who lifted all this matter high above the ground have gone, yet the surviving components still carry loads; their struggle against gravity has not ceased, as though a fall had been suspended halfway through the air. It is this unresolved change itself that the ruinwright borrows.

This way of seeing eventually enters the actual work of building. To borrow means that a construction must find a footing in the site's existing structures, available remnants and environmental relations. Just as guerrilla gardening recognises conditions for growth on neglected land, this site-specific construction first seeks materials, supports and load-bearing relations that can still be borrowed. The work begins by borrowing things and ultimately finds its direction in borrowing forces: responding to different terrains and surviving forms, it allows the openings made by weathering to participate in its formation. The material conditions of the site constrain and revise the initial conception; the ruinwright must, as far as possible, contain the failure of the new structure within the work itself, without inflicting unwarranted damage upon the original ruin. Folly comes into being through such borrowing: it grows along the fracture where the old order failed and draws on the continuing intrusion of nature to lead the garden towards its own eventual dissolution.[^13]

Borrowed things must eventually be returned. The first kind of return in Folly takes place when the builder no longer demands that the work indefinitely retain the form given to it: within the limits of the site and its environment, the new structure continues to bear the weather and changes in its materials. The other return concerns the person who intervenes. The ruinwright cannot claim ownership of the ruin, nor leave the risks and consequences of the intervention to time. As this temporary borrowing comes to an end, materials that might impose a lasting burden on the environment or nearby lives must be dealt with responsibly, and necessary changes to the site documented in traceable records. The disappearance of a form does not require that every remnant be left in place. What the ruinwright relinquishes is the insistence on the work's future form, not responsibility for having altered the land.

## Fourth Tenet: Brutal Materiality

Modern ruins often appear as bare concrete frames. Exterior finishes that once concealed the construction within and softened its boundaries grow mouldy and peel away with weathering, sometimes leaving only the skeleton that bears the loads and divides the space. This stripping resembles the peeling away of the skin of second nature, bringing the construction and material weight hidden beneath the building's intact appearance back into view. The aesthetics of ruination thus approach a kind of brutality, arising not from a predetermined formal expression, but from the physical reality a building gradually reveals when maintenance ceases: a reality governed by gravity, corrosion and a finite lifespan.

In the ruinwright's eyes, these exposed structures also embody a powerful modernist impulse: through precise structural calculations and synthetic materials, we sought to extend our will high and far into space. Technological reason seems far removed from primitive wildness, yet these towering concrete skeletons also betray an ambition bordering on conquest — to contain the uncertainties of nature within limits of our own making.

Yet as these buildings embodying human ambition gradually fall into ruin, another kind of wildness reveals itself from within. The skeleton left by precise calculations still stands, while the connections that keep it stable slowly loosen through erosion. Highly developed technological legacies and the most elementary physical forces meet violently within the same broken body, lending the ruin an untameable wildness. The brutality of the modern ruin changes direction in this confrontation: the desire for conquest once embodied in the structure encounters the intractable weight and finite lifespan of matter itself. This material reality breaking free from within human-made order is what I call “brutal materiality” — the flesh laid bare as control recedes. Reason has not ceased to be valid in a ruin; it can simply no longer take precedence over material reality.

Ruinwork likewise shifts its attention from the appearance of brutality to the material confrontation still taking place. Rather than symbolising strength through rough forms, the work bears actual weight, displacement and wear in its own structure, continually exposing the traces of its formation, loading and eventual decay. Where construction and use permit, natural fracture surfaces, joints and marks of fabrication need not all be concealed beneath finishes. This rough, unrefined beauty comes from materials that still carry the traces of their formation, loading and decay, without being wholly contained by the skin of a finished product. Where do the actual load-bearing limits of the modern materials in which we once took pride lie, and at what point will they resist or yield? The ruinwright builds along the lines of this question and continues to observe these changes as the work evolves.

## Fifth Tenet: Following the Flow of Forces

From the moment of its completion, a building has organised enormous material weight into a set of forces that support and restrain one another. Masonry is laid layer upon layer, and steel cables held in tension keep components in place. The forces that fix space do not vanish along with the building's use. Even in ruins already on the verge of collapse, loads may remain active, while surviving supports and restraints barely keep them standing for a while. Traces of structural decay are like pointers leading backwards: following spreading cracks and loosening connections, we can trace how forces were once borne and where resistance gradually gave way. Weathering and material fatigue make the moment of fracture difficult to predict, while the conditions of instability often develop long before, through construction, use and abandonment. The destruction of a ruin thus discloses an unfinished chain of causation. Forces that once held space together are still finding paths of release and redistribution through the remains.

![Figure 14](manifesto-435-assets/fig-14-a.jpg)

@caption Fig. 14. Following the flow of forces: on the wall of an old factory on Diantai Road, a new antenna guy wire is connected to the existing wall, where traces of cracking are also visible. The photograph prompted me to investigate the relationship between the wire's direction, the anchor point and the cracks. Photograph by the author, 2024; Ruinwork Engineering Database.

Collapse within a ruin is often more than an isolated local event. The failure of one component may affect others through their connections; fragments that crash down may find new points of bearing as they fall, piecing together a damaged but still functioning system of force transmission. This is the basis of the ruinwright's approach to the flow of forces. A steel cable is no longer merely a straight line on a drawing, but a strand of metal capable of rusting, stretching, slackening and tangling. Once legible load paths rearrange themselves as materials change. Even when the remains temporarily regain equilibrium, their countless connections and the hidden currents of their cracks continue to inscribe a long process of destruction within the structure.

Yet the unresolved tensions in the remains also prompt us to look back at histories still unexplained. Why did the ideals and ambitions that once raised these enormous buildings lose the power to sustain them? Does a fracture stem from a defect introduced during construction, or from the accumulated effects of neglect and environmental erosion? Decisions, errors and accidents of the past have left real material costs, though the remaining evidence may not allow us to attribute them fully. A ruin retains another kind of tension yet to be clarified: a crack can preserve evidence of loading, but cannot on its own tell us who bears responsibility for the consequences of decisions, abandonment and mistakes. The structures that have not yet fallen are tethered at one end to histories of construction and abandonment, and at the other hang towards a collapse that has not yet occurred.

Ruinwork attempts to bring these two kinds of tension together. Its mechanical transmission devices connect to actual load-bearing relations in the ruins. Their construction, use and abandonment have helped shape those relations; the work enables us to trace how they continue to act today. A single vibration cannot tell us an entire history, but following it back through the wreckage that carries it, we can keep asking where this destruction began. The ruinwright pulls at the fragments like a puppeteer, yet remains within the same web of cause and effect; every intervention introduces a new force. The forces borrowed will eventually act back upon the work itself, making the construction that seeks to trace destruction part of the very process it investigates.

## Sixth Tenet: The Destruction Specimen

Through calculation, maintenance and continual repairs, architecture provides a period of stability on which life can depend. We rarely have to confront the slow accumulation of fatigue within its components: small damage is difficult to notice, while a sudden spread of instability may make a safe approach impossible. Between imperceptible change and upheaval we cannot bear, the way a construction actually proceeds towards failure is often absent from everyday experience. From this absence arises the “destruction specimen”: it reorganises certain conditions of failure that can be controlled and recorded, bringing prolonged deterioration to an observable scale in a finite medium.

Like crash and durability tests in the automotive industry, the destruction specimen incorporates damage and failure into a predetermined process of observation. Such engineering tests normally examine load limits and the results of destruction under specified conditions to improve subsequent manufacture. The ruinwright borrows this experimental approach, but turns its purpose towards an immediate experience of destruction itself. Identifying materials, joints, masses and load-bearing relations relevant to failure in a ruin, the ruinwright constructs from expendable materials a body made expressly to undergo destruction. Displacement markers and devices recording deformation and vibration are like sensory prostheses attached to it, enabling us to approach, under safe conditions of observation, changes difficult for the human body to withstand. The specimen bears the fracture in its own body, so that processes abstracted as wear and obsolescence appear once more as actual weight, sound and material injury. What it presents is, first of all, the evolution of this particular construction under given conditions; it cannot replace verification of the failure mechanisms of the original building.

We need such a surrogate outside our own bodies precisely because, within second nature, we have grown accustomed to guarding against failure through calculation, maintenance and renewal, keeping the collapse of order outside everyday experience. The ruinwright therefore borrows the very reason once used to maintain stability and builds with his own hands a body capable of bearing destruction. Just as the figure of the android has enabled us to question the boundaries of what it means to be human, the ruinwright uses a body carrying the logic of architectural construction to test how far the order sustaining space can reach. We make it stand through rational calculation, and through the same reason lead it towards the threshold of collapse. The forces originally organised to sustain the structure gradually participate in its dissolution as connections loosen and the form loses balance. When the reason that created order exposes with its own hands the facade of permanence, the specimen becomes a cruel mirror. What we contemplate in its shattered remains is the finitude that neither human creations nor their creators can escape.[^14]

Ruinwork brings the logic of the specimen into the work, incorporating its passage towards disintegration into the construction from the outset. Components may loosen through connections deliberately arranged in advance, yet their actual displacement and fall need not follow the path imagined in the blueprint. New bearings and supports may form by chance, and over longer periods plants may grow out of abandoned crevices. These unchoreographed changes allow an experiment that incorporates destruction into its construction to produce unforeseen forms as it falls apart. An eerie and cruel beauty emerges, arising not from destruction itself but from the accidents and life that find room to occur as the old order dissolves. The specimen is carefully built through reason, yet in its disintegration the builder gradually loses complete command of its subsequent evolution. The vibrations and reverberations released from deep within it allow us to experience forces directly, and with them an actual interval of time through which destruction unfolds.

## Seventh Tenet: Returning to Liminality

As the original relations of use recede, the modern ruin resembles an empty shell whose soul has died before its body. An intricate spatial arrangement once meticulously organised by people may become a dark, unfamiliar labyrinth without directions, light or inhabitants. A door no longer points to an entrance, a corridor no longer assures arrival; broken partitions and stairways that end abruptly betray in silence the shelter and safety architecture once promised. Rather than reconstructing the vanished whole, these isolated severed limbs delineate human absence with extraordinary clarity. The wanderer faces a residual structure that has lost its former use but still bears loads. The spatial order once established around human movement loosens, and gravity, erosion and the relations between surviving components begin to govern space more directly. From the perspective of this spatial experience, the ruin seems to display a “posthuman condition”, no longer centred on human beings.[^15]

A ruin evolving on its own exerts an oppressive kind of attraction. The measure of time once given to a building by human activity has yielded to the long transformations of nature, which no longer reveal themselves at a pace our lives and senses can grasp. It seizes our gaze through traces of former inhabitants, yet bears down upon us with a long, unanswering silence, disturbing the established boundaries between person and space. Architecture no longer busies itself accommodating whoever arrives. The central position we once claimed for ourselves is, on the scale of a ruin, little more than a vacancy briefly left by time. Ornament remains on the walls, while cracks and plants continue to alter spatial boundaries, quietly absorbing all human activity into the site.[^16] The formerly intact space is difficult to reconstruct from the remains, and its future form cannot be outlined in advance. The land retains a human-made outline, lingering between former life and present abandonment while nature continually rewrites it. The liminality of the ruin lies in this suspension, unable to return fully to the past and not yet having found a definite place in what comes after.[^17]

Ruinwork brings scattered fragments together in this ruin, returning the gaze to a space that has lost its former place in life. These fragments still carry traces of the past and forces not yet dissipated, but can no longer fit back into a living context. They are reassembled into a strange body, maintaining a grotesque and fragile equilibrium through new connections. The work can neither restore their former functions nor bring back the lives that have left this ruin. From its beginning it has made no promise of permanence; even its eventual destruction has not been arranged into a definite form. We do not know what shape it will take in the future, yet still want to build with forces not yet exhausted, attempting through this work destined to grow ever stranger to recover our perception of the ruin.

The ruinwright expends his creative efforts in this space, then offers the form made with his own hands as a “sacrifice” to the ruin, exiling it into time. At first the strange body seems like a foreign object wedged awkwardly into the site. Yet, placed within the unfinished decay of the ruin, new cracks become entangled with its damaged textures, and the work gradually loses the distinct boundary between its form and the site, becoming a “spatial monster”. It still consists of recognisable architectural elements, but their relations have quietly fallen out of alignment. The more familiar their former uses, the more difficult it is to place the strange form before us.[^18] The fragments retain the warmth of what preceded their destruction, while the body now standing again has begun to undergo changes whose outcome cannot be prescribed. Through observing and waiting, the ruinwright experiences the work gradually passing beyond his control, allowing a time that cannot be measured by human life to close around his senses and oblige him to recognise this familiar space anew.

As this alien body enters the time of the ruin through its own wear, the person still trying to recognise the site by its former coordinates becomes the most untimely intruder. This estrangement also occurs between the viewer's body and the work. Its form hovers between architecture and sculpture, retaining some architectural dimensions without being large enough for actual habitation, or small enough to be an architectural model one could look down upon. Stairs still look like stairs, though one's feet can no longer ascend them; walls still divide inside from outside, yet enclose no complete living space. As scale and purpose fall out of alignment, familiar architectural elements gradually lose their correspondence with bodily experience. The viewer can recognise their former functions, but struggles to understand the form before them through former spatial conventions, having to find their own position in relation to the work within this liminal realm of former memories.

## Eighth Tenet: Dissipation into Sound

Fragments are the most visible image of ruins, but our most immediate experience of them often comes through sound. While a building is inhabited and used, slight vibrations at its connections are often masked by the sounds of daily activity, and the structure itself is held in a silence as solid as the building itself. Then a dull crack sounds from deep within a load-bearing structure that ought to remain firm, and components that once bore weight silently suddenly disclose their imbalance through an anomalous sound. It violates our expectation that architecture should remain silent. Though its source may be out of sight, the sound reaches our ears ahead of the collapse and is already shaking our confidence in shelter and safety. Through sound, a disquiet I call “civilisational dissonance” reaches the senses: the psychological defences long maintained by second nature collapse before the building itself does.

This disquiet comes from material changes still unfolding inside ruins. Components rub under compression, strike one another as connections come loose, and release some of their stored elastic energy as they fracture. Echoing drops of water and wind whistling through rooms also leave audible traces of erosion and spatial form. Sometimes a sound breaks out after a long silence; sometimes it recurs from a corner beyond sight, making it difficult to tell whether matter has shifted slightly or a larger instability is approaching. The threat of collapse keeps people outside, yet the ruin draws their eyes and ears through sounds whose sources are difficult to identify. We still recognise wind, dripping water and the friction of components, but hear them emanating from corridors and rooms that have long since ceased to answer to life. Their familiarity deepens the estrangement, and passers-by, conscious of potential danger, still find themselves wondering where the sounds come from.

![Figure 20](manifesto-435-assets/fig-20-a.jpg)

@caption Fig. 20. “Wind Throat”: an abandoned section of a large ventilation duct on waste ground, with a damper remaining inside. When the wind stirred the surrounding reeds and fleabane, I heard a low rumble from within the duct over the rustling of the plants. Photograph by the author, 2024.

A crack is gone almost as soon as it sounds, leaving a long silence. The listener follows its echo, trying to identify the loose connection from which it came. These occasional tremors draw attention to changes not yet apparent in the form, and the fading of sound need not mean that changes within the structure have subsided.

Ruinwork incorporates this impulse to follow sound back to its source into the sounding structures of Folly. New connections transmit barely perceptible displacements and vibrations in the ruin to a ruin instrument, sometimes transforming them into recognisable sound through mechanical transmission or electronic conversion. The ruinwright can arrange how the forces are borne and transmitted, but cannot prescribe the sound every instance of friction, impact or fracture will produce. Tension may build at a connection for a long time before a sudden release; other components may find an unexpected bearing as they fall, bringing another unscripted reverberation. The work thus gives the process of disintegration over to its own performance. As the performance continues, structural wear accumulates, and after some sounds have faded the form can no longer return to its former position. The reverberations that once called to us from deep within the ruins occur again in new forms through this steadily deteriorating construction.

## Ninth Tenet: Blueprints Becoming Scores

From the first lines drawn, ruinwork connects the movement of fragments with the sounds of structural fracture. Lines on drawings marking anchor points, load-bearing relations and suspension positions originally enable a construction to stand according to predetermined relations. Yet the work incorporates loosening and falling into its design from the outset, so that the forces holding its form together can later become the forces that break it. Anchor points, axes and dimensions once used to establish stability thereby also point towards future displacements and fractures. Even when there is not a single note on the drawing, the conditions of sound have already been buried in these lines filled with tension.

Conventional plans and elevations can establish the geometry of a building at a given stage, but a single finished drawing can hardly record the long period of displacement, wear and fracture that follows. For a construction destined to keep disintegrating, completion is only a brief state. To trace its changes in time, the drawing needs to accommodate continually added trajectories and new marks. As actual displacement and wear set in, the drawing sheds its prescriptive role: level marks can become references for subsidence, and load-bearing axes help trace movement after connections come loose. Lines initially drawn to fix the form gradually serve to investigate where friction, impact and fracture might produce sound. When cold geometric lines begin to point towards possible fractures and sources of sound, the blueprint finally crosses the boundary of engineering drawing and becomes a graphic score of destruction's rhythms.[^19]

A blueprint can arrange the conditions of sound in advance, but cannot determine when a sound will actually occur. Fragments may not fall along their original trajectories; an unexpected obstruction may delay an impact indefinitely, and an anomalous sound may come from a connection previously overlooked. The ruinwright has to redraw the site according to its actual displacements, fractures and sound sources. The original lines remain, but no longer suffice to account for the wreckage before him. The blueprint gradually changes from a guide for predetermined movement into a record continually revised in response to the site.

Faced with an increasingly tangled drawing, the ruinwright begins to bring into the notation the noise and disorder that cannot be contained in existing symbols. The elusive texture of breaking sounds, the shapes of spreading dust and corrosion, the irregular edges of water stains — these sounds and material traces that resist precise notation are transcribed as disorderly marks or written in layers over existing trajectories, distorting engineering symbols through repeated contamination and redrawing. The lines initially drawn for construction remain on the paper, gradually losing their original purpose beneath successive inscriptions. Chance and disorder once excluded from engineering drawings have also found a place on this sheet.

## Tenth Tenet: The Meaning of Futility

In my actual practice, ruinwork adopts the methods of surveying, design and construction used in architecture and engineering, yet has gradually withdrawn into an almost solitary labour. Much of the time I am alone deep within the ruins, quietly proceeding in response to the changes in materials and site. The moment I step among the remains, the chains of efficiency that drive daily life loosen: there are few demands from a client, and no clear measure of investment and return. My concerns gradually narrow to the physical reality of the ruin itself. Building here aims neither to provide a use nor to obtain a return, but to give the fleeting process of destruction a tangible form, leading the work towards a carefully choreographed collapse. The collapse may begin in a sudden loss of balance, or emerge gradually through erosion and wear, but its seed was planted on the first day the ground was broken. Everything cultivated in ruinwork amounts to borrowing the forces that will eventually bring the structure to failure, leading the work towards an edge of disintegration from which it is difficult to turn back.

When society habitually measures labour by endurance and output, pouring one's efforts into a work destined to be destroyed seems profoundly uneconomical, bordering on absurd futility. This utilitarian yardstick quietly favours things that are sturdy, intact and usable, readily consigning breakage, weathering and disappearance to failure. Ruinwork lingers precisely in the fissures of this system of values: listening to the materiality exposed when materials lose their appointed uses, and tracing the loads and tensions not yet exhausted in structures nearing failure. Labour undertaken without a utilitarian aim still has actual material costs, and the ruinwright must make choices about what to build. Yet its meaning need not be established entirely through the endurance and output of the work. Things built with care pass step by step beyond their maker's control after completion, carrying the labour invested in them into time; the bodily effort and years actually spent in that labour lie beyond what any judgement of value can repay.

The fact that the work is destined for destruction does not lessen the effort needed to build it. Forms that look incomplete must still be made to stand through the joining, fixing and loading of materials. A slight discrepancy at a connection may require dismantling parts already completed; calculations must be repeatedly revised as conditions on site change. Conventional architecture calculates for stability, whereas ruinwork must allow a form to stand temporarily while leaving room for later loosening and disintegration. How to sustain a delicate equilibrium amid nature's unpredictability, while allowing designed fractures and displacements to occur through chance, becomes a particular difficulty of this kind of building.

A futility more prolonged than building a ruin is the years I have spent travelling repeatedly between ruins in different places. Survey after survey gradually connects scattered remains into a world atlas of ruins, while measured drawings, photographs and notes leave records of these still-changing structures that can be checked again. The work appears to accumulate a comprehensive body of knowledge and methods, but what it yields is often only the starting point for the next inquiry: new ruins continually enter the atlas, and older records need revising as sites change. I spend a great deal of time and bodily effort on this work without a definite endpoint, trying to establish a way of understanding and building for things that have long fallen outside the everyday system of value. The labour itself scarcely finds a commensurate response within the usual measures of efficiency, achievement and return.

It is as though I had invented a discipline for modern ruins that does not exist in the world, and undertaken to practise it with all the care of a craftsman — building for uninhabited ruins, drawing plans for fleeting remains, and spending, bit by bit, the years that could have gone towards other work or other ways of living on a craft with no clear end. Yet has the city beneath our feet not likewise been built up by the hands of generations, continuing to the present through endless repair, replacement and rebuilding? If even the city of the future will eventually become a ruin, where are we to seek recognition for the labour and lives spent upon it?[^20] The disintegration of our creations can still leave wreckage behind; the years already lived will not return with the preservation of the work.

Yet in this work repeatedly obstructed and seemingly unrewarded, I began to notice another change difficult to explain. A connection that worked on paper might deviate from what I expected on site through the resistance of materials. I would have to stop, look again at constructions I had long taken for granted, and revise my judgement through dismantling, adjusting and starting over. For a long time I sought in ruins a meaning I could scarcely articulate. Gradually I discovered that what I had been searching for might not reside in the remains at all, but had, over these years of contending with materials, begun to enter my own way of knowing things.[^21] Person and creation thus do not remain entirely separate: in trying to understand the ruin, I have myself been changed by the labour.

The meaning of futility makes room for labour that cannot be settled in terms of returns. The experience gained through labour cannot give back the years already spent, nor make the disappearance of the work reversible. Knowing that what we make will disintegrate, why do we keep building? Looking back at the long days spent travelling between ruins, I find the question already entwined with my own life. Ruins have promised me no final answer, and I have already given part of my life to this craft still finding its way. Such labour need not prove itself through eternity. Even if the things I built with my own hands eventually disappear, I still want to return to this ground without guarantees and continue the work before me.

## The Limits of the Ruinwright

Even if different ruins eventually acquire similar material appearances through weathering, the reasons for their destruction need not be the same. War, engineering accidents and misguided decisions leave particular wounds in buildings; changing uses and prolonged neglect may also cause them to be abandoned prematurely. Nature can slowly wear away the traces of destruction, but cannot turn acts that took place in history into natural phenomena, much less erase responsibility for them. How should those displaced by abandonment and renewal be accommodated? Who will inherit the traces of those who lived and worked here, and who has the right to decide the future of these remains? Such questions do not disappear with new construction. The beauty, sublimity and sorrow the ruinwright feels before the remains are ultimately his own experience of this encounter in the present; those who actually lived through the destruction may feel quite differently. Faced with histories his experience cannot encompass, the ruinwright must retain a sense of reverence.

Ruinwork borrows the perceptions of art and the means by which architecture and engineering organise matter. It can thus establish new connections within a ruin, allowing surviving forces to participate in the formation of a work. Once the ruinwright intervenes in a site, he must acknowledge limits to the materials, space and time that can be borrowed, and consider ownership, lives continuing nearby and private memories that should not be made public at will in the name of creation. A new construction may bear its own failure, but not at the cost of damaging existing ruins or exposing others to danger. Reverence for nature must also find expression in each actual intervention. Plants growing among broken walls do not in themselves demonstrate that the land has recovered from earlier damage, and materials and disturbances introduced by the maker do not cease to exist when the work loses its form. Subject to lawful access, site safety and environmental conditions, the ruinwright should minimise unnecessary disturbance and take responsibility for the actual consequences of each alteration.

The tenets the ruinwright has established for his craft must also undergo repeated revision. Morphemes used to identify remains, calculations for tracing forces and blueprints recording disintegration once offered directions into the ruin. Yet as the site continues to change, these methods repeatedly encounter things they cannot accommodate. If provisional measures of observation are elevated into fixed laws explaining all ruins, ruinwork may establish another second nature upon the ruins, concealing the still unformed reality before us beneath an order of its own making. Imagination arising from fragments may go beyond established narratives, but must remain distinct from knowledge drawn from traces and documents. Theory offers a route into the site; it grants no one the authority to look down upon it from above. What remains beyond our understanding is also where this craft can continue to grow.

Here the tenets come provisionally to a close. The methods that once served to identify, build and record ruins must be put to the test the next time we enter a site.

# Ruinwright Statement

The tenets have provisionally given ruinwork a way to enter ruins, recognise their remains and borrow forces not yet exhausted within them. Yet the impulses that lead us to linger among these remains time and again cannot be fully explained by methods of building. Why do ruins fascinate us? Knowing that their disappearance cannot be reversed, why do we still wish to linger, to look and to feel, even to keep building? This inquiry gradually turns the gaze from the ruin towards the person looking at it.

The perception of beauty in ruins may be our first way into this long exploration. The beauty that appears by chance in a broken form often reaches the senses before rational judgement. Yet when we are moved by that beauty, or even take a voyeuristic pleasure in it, might we too readily absorb someone else's loss into our own aesthetic experience? What makes us want to follow the fracture further, rather than be satisfied with a scene already captured by a camera?

The statement that follows begins with that first perception of beauty. It follows the ways ruins continually disturb our senses and understanding, tracing the desires behind our gaze and how these desires have taken shape in modern life. These thoughts arise from experience accumulated in wandering and building, and touch upon a condition of the mind that some of us living today may likewise recognise.

# The Broken Frame — From the Picturesque to the Sublime

With the passage of time and the erosion of wind and rain, intact buildings lose their sheen; yet in the mottled intertwining of rubble and moss, something fleetingly romantic takes shape. Ruins often first enter our aesthetic consciousness in this gentle guise of the picturesque.

## The Picturesque: A Ruined Scene within the Frame

The picturesque seeks scenes suited to a picture in nature's transformations, favouring rough, incomplete things that resist regular form. Time and chance gradually draw these forms away from their original human arrangements, revealing undesigned beauty through long transformations. Yet when a posture that appears by chance moves us, we long to keep it before our eyes. Painting, photography and garden design have each given such fleeting encounters a form that can be viewed again and again. In seeking and retaining a landscape, an invisible frame gradually comes into being: from a relatively secure distance, we can contemplate scenes full of incompleteness and sudden change, temporarily accommodating within a composition what would otherwise be difficult to grasp.[^22]

The classical ruins we are accustomed to admiring are already forms preserved through centuries of erosion and the choices of later generations. The picturesque gaze then selects from these surviving remains a scene worth looking at. Destruction that has not entered the picture recedes from sight as the composition is made. Over time, our image of destruction comes to rest upon these postures selected and preserved from a long course of decay.

Some ornamental buildings in European gardens, known as follies, were built in the guise of ruins, arranging broken walls, the aura of antiquity and the appearance of weathering into scenery to be admired on a walk. One need not wait centuries for an actual building to decay in order to have a ruin imbued with the aura of age in one's own garden. Once the image of ruin previously confined to a picture acquires real weight through construction, one seems able to leap across those long years and give a newly completed building the posture of something approaching the end of its life. The garden thereby seeks to keep this fleeting picturesque beauty within its appointed scenery for a long time.[^23]

Yet if we invite a ruin into the garden, the garden must also receive its collapse, its shadows and its rotting leaves. Ruined walls still bear their own weight, and weathering will not stop for the sake of preserving an antique posture. The forces that mottle walls and give masonry the appearance of age continue to act along the damage within the materials, and may eventually deprive the scene before us of its form. To retain a landscape to which time has given beauty, we sometimes find ourselves having to prevent, through continual repairs and upkeep, the very changes that made it beautiful.

This frame did not vanish with the retreat of the classical garden. It persists in modern photography and in the gaze that turns the world into scenery. A camera can condense a long collapse into a visual cross-section to be looked at repeatedly. Yet when we grow accustomed to understanding ruins through such arrested images, the depth of space, the weight a structure has yet to relinquish and the changes slowly taking place outside the picture can easily escape us. Destruction seems to have found a place in the photograph, allowing us to contemplate its brokenness at leisure, while the actual ruin has already moved towards another form beyond our gaze.[^24]

Yet the ruin does not always obey this frame. A storm, or the loosening of a load-bearing joint, can wrench it out of the posture that once moved us. Remains that once belonged to the scenery continue towards disappearance along their fractures; when the material forces that had receded beyond the composition appear again, we begin to realise that the destruction before us was never truly accommodated within the picture. Collapse, bearing its actual material weight, comes crashing through the tranquillity of the image.

## Folly: Setting the Frame Free

If the beauty that moves us arises in the very process by which things depart from their original forms, why, in a garden built expressly for this transformation, should we restrain once more the forces that gave it beauty at the moment when it is most pleasing to look at? In Folly, wildness once kept beyond the frame has already returned to the interior of the building through its cracks. The space built by the ruinwright for looking upon the landscape will itself be drawn into the landscape's evolution.

Folly begins with this reversal. It borrows its name from the ornamental structures of traditional gardens, but is no longer content to maintain the carcass of an object in a picturesque state of antiquity for our enjoyment. The ruinwright builds anew with surviving structures and residual forces, incorporating loosening, imbalance and eventual disintegration into the construction. Changes once repeatedly trimmed back in the garden now continue along the connections of the new work, gradually altering its form and even undoing the structures once used to organise those changes. What the ruinwright sets free is no longer only the scenery within the frame. The frame he built with his own hands is itself given over to this unfinished disintegration.

To allow this evolution to continue, the ruinwright writes future failure into the construction of the garden as he builds it. The order given to the work by the blueprint will also be revised through actual movement and wear, so that this carefully choreographed destruction continually produces things beyond the choreography itself. The order the ruinwright has given to matter does not simply vanish. Alongside other forces, it continues to participate in the garden's formation and disappearance as the work evolves.

As the garden gradually departs from its drawings through its own evolution, unforeseen forms briefly appear, sometimes existing only between two losses of balance and vanishing before they can be noticed. It becomes increasingly difficult to choose a single moment in this evolution as the final appearance of the completed work. The picturesque beauty that once made the ruinwright stop still appears by chance at various stages of the garden. As his gaze follows the forces that bring about these changes of form, however, he begins to encounter a beauty that cannot be accommodated by composition alone.

The tremor of the sublime does not wait until a building is destroyed. Long before, while these constructions still fulfil their missions, it resides in the intricate composition of industrial machinery, the austere order of building structures and the material forces held in check within them. Cables and frameworks sustain enormous weights; precise transmission mechanisms carry forces through component after component. We can recognise their construction and use, yet the complex relations established by countless materials, calculations and acts of labour exceed what an individual can grasp through the senses. Faced with these carefully organised forces, we feel our own smallness while marvelling at humanity's ability, through knowledge and craft, to make things on a scale so far beyond the individual.[^25]

Yet when something once meticulously built by people gradually passes beyond their control through its own evolution, the sublime that once inspired awe begins to reveal another, unsettling strangeness. Beams, cables and mechanical components remain recognisable, but the mission and care that once enabled them to stand and operate have withdrawn. The surviving structures still bear loads, and erosion and wear continue along their original connections. We recognise their former purposes and may still discern human ingenuity and intention in their construction, yet that familiarity can no longer make them submit to their former arrangement. Material forces that once made us marvel at humanity's ability to build are gradually breaking free from their structural constraints, revealing an aspect no longer wholly subject to human will. The sublime changes direction: through our own constructions, we feel once more the limits we cannot transcend.

The Broken Frame (fig. 27) gives this change a visible form. As the frame itself becomes a ruin, it gradually loses the ability to fix a fleeting scene within a composition. When the scenery it once enclosed recedes, the damaged boundaries left behind become the very object of our gaze. Fractures spread along the frame itself, and the thing once entrusted with holding the landscape still now exposes a body equally incapable of escaping destruction.

We once used this frame to contemplate scenes beyond our control from a relatively secure position, and to recognise our distance from the world. Yet when the very boundaries that accommodated these scenes begin to break, we realise that the measures that once allowed us to look so calmly upon the world also depend upon a finite human-made order. The disintegration of the frame gradually draws our gaze from the scenery within it to the frame itself, then, through its fractures, back towards the viewer who once stood outside it.

## Ruins of the Mind: The Lost Frame

Architecture once accommodated people's lives with intact walls and familiar paths. Through daily use, second nature assigned things their purposes and places, confirming for us our own position in the world. It is this sense of security that allows us to contemplate ruins at our leisure, leaving destruction for a while within the frame, as though it were far removed from the life we continue to live.

Yet as our gaze moves deeper among the ruined walls, we find that the things that once housed other people's lives are so similar to those that house our own today. The space remains where it was. Things once used with care and cherished have not yet vanished, but have lost their former weight in the unanswering emptiness. We can still recognise what they were used for, yet can scarcely imagine what place they once held in someone's life. Faced with these familiar and forsaken things, we begin to notice that our own certainties about life also rest upon relations that will eventually change. A familiar building becomes a “lost space”: it retains the dimensions of former life, but can no longer retain the life that gave those dimensions meaning.

In daily life, our consciousness is forever responding to the world outside, and time moves forward with the tasks awaiting completion. Many questions about ourselves are temporarily set aside in this recurring pattern. When the explanations attached to a space recede, the psychological shelter provided by functions, narratives and other people also loosens, and it becomes increasingly difficult to evade one's own presence within it. The loneliness of this encounter arises from a real, quiet absence: familiar life has departed, while the space remains. Even if we never lived here, the faintly recognisable traces of living can leave us with a loss resembling homesickness. We imagine histories we never took part in, and lives that might have happened but never did, yet can never find a complete response among these remnants.

An inward sublime takes place in this loss from which we can no longer maintain a distance. Romantic melancholy for former grandeur may still allow us to place ourselves, for a while, outside the history we mourn. Yet when this lost space touches our own lives, we feel time acting simultaneously upon the ruined walls before us and upon our own bodies. The things that still have weight and warmth, that we cherish so carefully now, may one day recede into an unnoticed cross-section of history; we ourselves are already part of this irreversible disappearance. The heaviness, helplessness and loneliness spread from the broken forms before us to the things we have not yet lost. Sometimes we instinctively clench a hand, as if to make sure something still remains in this moment.

Where there is nothing to rely on, consciousness gradually grows clearer. We cannot stop time or bring departed lives back. The voices that ordinarily explain the world to us and give us a place within it have sunk into silence. Yet we can still feel the weight of a fragment, grieve for things that have gone, and be moved by beauty in a form that has appeared by chance. In this almost absolute solitude, the space before us can no longer explain these feelings for us; we have to face their origins alone. And in this silence, it is still “I” who feel and respond.

## A Mirror of the Modern Mind: Phantoms of an Empty Frame

Things that feel strangely close to us after their destruction may not have felt so when intact. With clearly defined purposes and finished appearances, they entered everyday life; their walls and shells concealed their construction and kept the details of former lives apart from us. When function withdraws and surfaces peel away, hidden components, scratches and wear come gradually into view, and along these fractures we recognise a belated familiarity. Even if we never participated in their past, we have lived within the same human-made order and are equally subject to matter and time. Their former intact appearance allowed us to pass them by. Broken, they come close to our bodies again with actual material weight.

Yet this familiarity never brings genuine peace. We try to find a way through the recognisable architectural elements by following familiar spatial logic, only to be repeatedly thwarted by severed routes and spaces that have lost their former connections. Our steps seem to carry us ever forward, yet nothing we experience seems to add up to any definite progress. Traces of everyday life occasionally evoke affection, only to awaken fear and loss in the unanswering emptiness, leaving the ruin suspended between intimacy and estrangement. The rhythm that daily life once gave to time is no longer effective, while the time of the ruin continues slowly through transformations of matter itself. When our actions lose their former temporal bearings, it becomes difficult to find a footing in this chaotic space, and we fall into an aimless wandering.

This wandering without a place to arrive at has a hidden counterpart in the mental life of modern people. When a building falls out of the course of social development, the relations of use that enabled it to participate in society have been severed, while its material body lingers in place bearing loads not yet exhausted. Likewise, even after losing a former social identity and sense of belonging, we may not escape the habits of living already internalised. The social coordinates by which we once identified ourselves gradually fail, yet the measure demanding that life continually move forward remains in the minds of those who have lost their place. Even with nowhere to go, we keep trying to find a new position for ourselves.

The liminality of modern ruins becomes especially pronounced in this parallel experience of loss. To those who have experienced something similar on the margins of modern life, the desolation of a ruin may feel strangely familiar. It is as though we were once more suspended in a gap in our lives: the old order can no longer accommodate us, and the life ahead has yet to take definite form. This unsettling liminality has itself become something some people actively seek in their aesthetic experience.

Looking back at the rapid development of modern building technology, we were once excited by our growing power over space and poured all manner of desires for the future into building. Yet the things left without a place in the aftermath of some overly rapid construction still hold expectations once invested in space that never found a response in the lives that followed. Born into a landscape already covered by human-made order, we inherited the spaces fashioned by our predecessors and have in turn been fashioned by them through years of inhabitation. The rise of cultural phenomena such as liminal spaces and ruin aesthetics may now suggest another direction for aesthetic desire: a fascination with the forms familiar spaces take when they depart from their original arrangements, even a wish to dismantle in imagination the order that once gave them meaning. When space no longer tells us where to go, the course of life it once prescribed also seems to loosen. Amid the unease of disorientation, we sense a faint freedom not to follow the old path, even if for now it exists only in imagination.[^26]

The broken beauty of the frame thus becomes a mirror of the modern mind. As we grow fascinated by the deconstruction of these spaces, our gaze gradually turns towards the orders that once shaped us through space. We have relied on our position in the world to understand ourselves, and become accustomed to understanding everything around us through its arranged relations. Only as these relations loosen do we begin to recognise that much of the self that once seemed self-evident was gradually formed through a long history of playing roles and responding to the world.

Yet even in such nearly absolute solitude, the “I” that responds to itself may not be as pure as we imagine. Beyond the appearances gradually formed so that the world could recognise us, what still belongs to us? The fractures of a ruin seem to expose gaps where familiar social identities no longer fit the reality before us, but do not thereby give us back a purer self. The scenery within the frame has receded, while the boundaries that once enclosed it still linger in consciousness. Even when we recognise the seemingly self-evident appearance of order and its limits, we cannot thereby escape the way it has shaped us.

We still live within second nature and even need to borrow its measures to understand the world that is losing its certainty. Desires, fears and impulses we have yet to recognise, once concealed by roles and daily routines, begin to appear along the fractures of disintegration. And the beauty that first drew us to ruins becomes less and less self-explanatory amid this disorientation.

# Images of Ruins — Where Desire Goes

Born into modern society, we have long inherited a way of measuring things by order, efficiency and use, internalising these judgements into an almost instinctive gaze. Modern ruins are precisely the fracture left by this logic: once a construction loses its former function and utility, the utilitarian gaze finds nothing to grasp among these remains from which it can extract no profit. It can explain why something was once built and how it was used, but struggles to account for why it is still worth looking at after withdrawing from life. Yet precisely where judgement loses its footing, a moment of beauty may strike us unexpectedly from an exposed fragment or a ragged fracture, making us stop. Following this attraction that has yet to find an explanation, we approach the ruin again and continue along its exposed surfaces, asking where this beauty comes from, and what enables remains long deprived of their former use to stir desires within us that we have yet to recognise.

## The Broken Prism: Looking into the Fracture

Few images are more immediately characteristic of ruins than the fragments that have fallen away from an intact building, and the fracture surfaces left where they parted. These surfaces fascinate us because they expose relations of construction that were once hidden within the whole. Imagine second nature as a prism continually refracting reality: the eye follows the refracted spectrum, while the glass itself remains hidden behind the bright illusion. Institutions and everyday experience perform a similar refraction, translating the complexity of reality into things that can be recognised and managed. In time, we may even mistake this organised image for the world itself. When the prism breaks, the light shifts and scatters across the new surfaces, and the thickness and fragility of the glass itself become visible again. The fractures in ruins likewise interrupt our habitual understanding of architecture, making the conditions that once helped constitute the meaning of things, but passed unnoticed in everyday life, objects of inquiry in their own right.

This fracture also allows us to question relations previously obscured by a coherent narrative of the city. A building once acquired a distinct identity in response to a particular social need, yet its rise and decline often lay far beyond the influence of those within it. As its former shape and use disintegrate, we begin to examine things we once took for granted. Why was it built, and why was it given this particular form? How did the social needs and ideas that brought it into being change over time, bringing it to its present condition? The broken prism does not give us the power to see everything. It simply brings into our understanding the very medium once entrusted with explaining the world, now no longer able to do so.

The fragments thus acquire a beauty that is difficult to place. Their appearance can no longer be restored to that of the intact whole, yet they carry the material conditions that once made the whole possible. What they retain of absence allows us to notice how the former appearance came into being and why it could no longer be sustained. The beauty given to us by the broken prism arises from the experience of suddenly being able to contemplate the former boundaries of our understanding.[^27]

## Ruin Romanticism: The Tenderness of Fragments

Looking inward along the cracks, we gradually discern the tragic character of modern life. The ideals in which builders once placed great hopes may never have been realised before the buildings that carried them lost their reason to be maintained; some lives that did come to pass have lost their echo with the departure of their inhabitants. We always hope that the meaning and beauty invested in our creations will endure, yet some ideals give rise to new tragedies even in the course of their realisation. When losses once concealed beneath an intact appearance finally lie exposed before us, we still feel tenderness for their brokenness, while unable to deny a certain beauty emerging from these fractures.

To recognise suffering and at the same time be moved by beauty lends the contemplation of ruins a distinctly modern tragic contradiction.[^28] The losses of the past have not been made good simply because the wreckage has acquired new forms, yet those forms may evoke both fear and consolation. Perhaps the consolation comes from a loss once difficult to face finally acquiring a form we can look upon. Grief long set aside in everyday life, even dulled into numbness, finds something to hold on to in the wreckage; what moves us is that we can still sense life and beauty not entirely extinguished in what remains. We are fully aware of the weight of this destruction in the actual world, yet cannot help being moved by what we see, as though the contradictions in our own lives that have been so difficult to articulate were laid bare alongside this broken body.

Perhaps the tenderness evoked by fragments has two different destinations. Some is reserved for lives that really took place and are now only faintly recognisable through material traces; some for futures that never happened, whose possibility of realisation vanished with the abandonment of the building. Even if we never lived in these spaces, a surviving ornament or an unfinished room may convey something of the affection and expectation once invested in it, and we may regret a life we never had. Two equally unreachable times remain with us in the present through these fragments. Perhaps this is a curious form of mourning particular to modern ruins: we can even feel the loss of something that never existed.[^29]

In modern life, which continually presses forward, we have gradually learnt to accept that things belong to the past before we have had time to grieve their disappearance. What reason classifies as obsolescence, wear or renewal has not necessarily found a place in our feelings. In its posture of not quite having ended, a ruin gives mourning that had no time to occur in daily life somewhere to linger. When we discover that we are still willing to stop for things no longer needed, this tenderness gradually folds back upon ourselves. What we cherish is both the world that has gone and our own remaining capacity to be moved by its beauty.

This desire that draws us towards destruction and makes us unable to resist approaching ruins might be called a “ruin complex”.[^30] It is fascinated by the merciless carving of matter by time, and drawn to the peculiar postures in which decay and new life intertwine within the same form, lending disappearance a melancholy, almost irresistible attraction. Mourning gradually seeps into the way we look. The warmth of former days that has not yet dissipated, and the grandeur and ideals once invested in buildings, make us willing to stop for things that have lost their use. Such tenderness has no power to reverse disappearance, yet still seeks to preserve in the fragments something of the light not yet extinguished, bringing things forgotten by their age into the present of our existence again.

Perhaps this is a romantic spirit belonging to modern ruins. It knows that fragments cannot restore a vanished age and no longer expects a complete redemption, yet still wants to discern beauty in this desolation. Wishes never realised in the past intertwine with forms that have grown by chance out of fractures today, as though the ruin held both a past once hoped for but never lived and a future we ourselves may never reach. The fragments before us are like scattered lines of verse, still carrying their former resonance, yet no longer bound to one another by their original poem. As we try to find new connections between these lines, the tenderness that once made us reluctant to touch the fragments begins to give rise to a desire to create: with these surviving lines, we want to write the poem that was never written.

## The Fool's Stance: A Utopia of Fragments

When the desire for reconstruction recedes, the ruin remains in an interval of time not yet exhausted: its walls have neither been drawn back into life nor found their end in collapse. In the continuing tension between grandeur not quite dissipated, wishes left unfulfilled and destruction long delayed, the ruin awakens from a long, neglected suspension and silence, beginning to develop a narrative and a bearing of its own. The ruinwright enters in the guise of the Fool. He feels tenderness for the fragments, yet is fascinated by the possibilities they disclose after departing from their former shapes. The folly the ruinwright seeks is an innocence not yet exhausted by the act of seeing clearly.

In approaching a ruin, tenderness, curiosity and voyeuristic pleasure often become entangled. With some hidden expectation, we try to find what has been concealed among the jumbled fragments. Sometimes we cannot even say what we are looking for. We stop before a fracture that happens to come into view, or cannot help imagining how a strangely shaped fragment might look joined to another piece of wreckage. In this aimless exploration, the images continually arising from the ruin stir our desires; associations difficult to notice in daily life often give us the urge to bring these things together before reason has even recognised them.

A desire to create, akin to that of Surrealism, begins to grow here. As established rational order loses its hold over the uses of things, imaginings once suppressed by habit find opportunities to connect seemingly unrelated objects. Sometimes we are moved first by an image that has not yet taken form and only afterwards begin to wonder how it might exist in reality. Following this force long sedimented among the fragments, the ruinwright tries out connection after connection, borrowing the forces still remaining in the wreckage to give actual weight to forms that previously existed only in imagination.[^31]

Creation here gradually leaves behind the aims of repair and utility. The Fool need not make these fragments fulfil their former duties, nor seek a justification for destruction that has already taken place. He begins to wonder: if destruction is ultimately a fate our creations cannot escape, could the forces that draw them towards disintegration also become a medium of creation? Through the exchange of matter and energy with its surroundings, life can maintain its intricate and delicate form for a while;[^32] within this irreversible evolution the Fool likewise seeks a material body for his wild imaginings. Yet he has no intention of keeping that body forever in the order he has given it. He is willing to compose its subsequent wear and disintegration into the work, allowing the process of disappearance to become part of its poetic expression. An impulse to break established forms thus gradually turns towards a desire to create. Since the destruction that has already occurred, and its forces still at work, are enough to participate in creation, the Fool need not resort to more thorough destruction to satisfy that impulse. He borrows the forces of destruction to build, and through the evolution of the form itself makes a poetic expression of finite life.

We cannot, after all, return to a primordial world untouched by civilisation. Even when the rules of daily life temporarily lose their force, the memories, experiences and habits that guide our desires have long been part of our lives. This does not make the impulse to create any less real. Among abandoned constructions, we may still occasionally sense beauty to which no meaning has yet been assigned, and create out of that feeling. Perhaps a return to the authentic is simply the willingness, once we have acknowledged that we can no longer stand apart from this world, to face feelings long set aside, and even to use the very knowledge and skills that once disciplined us to give them a form with our own hands.

Folly is the material form taken by this playing of a role.[^33] The Fool sets aside for a while the reckoning of results and returns, and with an almost innocent obstinacy starts building again from fragments that can be borrowed. Knowing full well that what he makes cannot withstand time, he insists on making something that has not existed stand for a little while, giving it a dignified presence.

With fragments scattered from different times, the Fool may even construct a past that never existed, or a future he himself may never reach. Such a past belongs to his imagination and cannot replace the histories the fragments actually went through; yet it need not follow history's original plot. Wishes once entrusted to buildings can intertwine with the ruin's present form in this fictional act of construction, giving rise to a garden its former builders might never have imagined. This is the utopia of fragments the Fool builds among the remains. From the moment of its completion it bears the traces of destruction, and cannot escape the fate of the ruins that are still disintegrating.

The Fool walks towards the precipice, so that possibility is not exhausted in advance by established experience and conclusions.[^34] His step remains light among the ruins. He knows quite clearly that there is no guarantee beneath his feet, yet also knows that each piece of rubble may once have borne the weight of a life he can never fully understand. The ruinwright continues to play this role on ground with nothing to rely on, earnestly choreographing his wild imaginings on a fleeting stage.

# The Last Sound — A Performance after the Curtain Falls

The Fool who gathered fragments among the ruins has finally given his homeless imaginings a form that stands, if only for a while. Yet when a work has accepted its eventual disintegration from the very beginning of construction, where does this act of creation, which found freedom among ruins, leave its creator? The rules that once directed how architecture should be used and kept in existence have long ceased to find a response. Still the ruinwright measures and builds there with care, even arranging an irreversible finale for his creation. This almost mad obstinacy enters the work's ensuing performance and finale.

Second nature resembles a theatre that has long been in operation, where we accept our respective roles and gradually take the actions arranged for us to be life itself. A ruin resembles the backstage area left behind after a theatre has been vacated. The former play is over; the walls and props that once sustained the performance remain in place, yet there are no longer characters for them to answer to. Here the ruinwright's performance reveals a kind of structural madness: even after the old roles have lost their resonance and the enactment of order has failed, he chooses to play in earnest the part of a builder unconcerned with gains and losses, resisting second nature's impulse to incorporate the unknown into its order and smooth away its strangeness. Rational judgement is deliberately deferred, allowing the loss of control, fear and sense of the sublime provoked by the unknown to linger briefly. In moments of departure from the old order we draw close to a chaos yet to take form; in our dealings with ruins, we discover that the order once taken for granted can itself appear absurd when deprived of the reality in which it was enacted.[^35]

## Postdramatic Space: Behind the Stage

Modern ruins often linger in the aftermath of a cycle of construction and renewal. The new city moves forward, while buildings that could not be carried into its next life stay where they were, as though the scenery had been prematurely withdrawn from the stage of history. Past upheavals reside in their fractured surfaces. The ideals and ambitions that once drove construction embodied an age's imagination of future life; yet as times changed, the social expectations sustaining some of those possible lives had already lost their force before the lives themselves arrived.[^36]

Futures never realised, together with histories that have yet to find a response, linger in this theatre where the former performance has ended. Seen in terms of time, the ruin is a theatre after the plot: spaces once designed for a particular future have already become relics of an earlier age. Their material lives are not exhausted, and they continue to bear the unsettled consequences of history. A postdramatic view of the time and space of modern ruins allows material events left behind by the narrative of progress to reach our senses again, as if in a belated flicker of life before death.[^37] Mechanisms left by the old order sometimes display a strange vitality once deprived of their former coordination, only to move towards deeper decay through their respective wear. They remain entangled in the same theatre, yet no longer enact a single story written in advance.

When we look upon a ruin as the form a building takes after its original mission has ended, we may follow the surviving components and rediscover material relations no longer contained by an established use. Architecture was built through human organisation of materials and the conferral of meaning. In disintegration it gradually loses the images and signs that once established its identity, lingering in the very heart of civilisation as a carcass difficult to accommodate within its former order, openly tearing the seemingly intact surface of civilised order. The ruin now resembles the backstage area of a theatre opened to the audience after the performance. As the former plot recedes, viewers gradually depart from the roles once assigned to them by the space. Things that the audience never needed to notice begin to occupy the stage, and architecture's own unfinished material evolution steps out of the background of human activity to become the subject of this theatre.

## Disintegration into an Instrument: A Performance through Deconstruction

If silence itself is understood as an instruction issued by order, then architecture in its everyday habitation resembles a character required to remain silent. We are accustomed to the solidity of the slabs beneath our feet and the columns and beams beside us, as though these enormous material bodies need only bear their loads in silence. Yet when a dull grinding sound or a crack from deep within a support suddenly reaches us, architecture steps out of its role as scenery. The anomalous sounds of the ruin gradually undo this convention of silence, abruptly shaking our confidence in shelter and safety.

The earlier silence of inhabited architecture owes something to the long containment, by order, of natural vibrations within matter. The ruin enters disintegration through one anomalous sound after another; ruinwork attempts to reorganise this departure from order, so that the new construction gradually takes the form of a disintegrating instrument as it loses balance and falls apart. An instrument, too, organises vibration through relations between materials, allowing certain sounds to be recognised repeatedly through tuning and performance. Extending this relation to architecture, the order sustained by function and construction resembles a music theory by which matter is organised. As that order gradually fails, the conditions of sound continually change with the wear of materials. By some accident, architecture seems to acquire the qualities of an instrument backstage, beginning a performance that was never composed for it.

At the site of a ruin, the ruinwright tries to bring this anomalous sounding and intermittent, barely perceptible performance into his own work, so that the mechanical structure originally used to transmit and organise vibrations also alters the way it produces sound as wear and imbalance accumulate. The performance of a ruin instrument thus presents both the condition of a structure at a particular moment and the gradual failure of its original tuning relations through continued wear, enabling us to hear the disintegration of a sounding order itself. Out of this precisely designed mechanical structure come increasingly harsh and disordered sounds, giving the absurdity revealed in the breakdown of order an audible form we can experience directly. The performance resembles an auditory dissection of the death of a building: the very relations that once made its order possible are now taking part in its dissolution.

## From Detuning to Return: The Spent Note

Looking back through this fracture, the very term “musical instrument” implies a double act of discipline and arrangement. The “musical” part organises the vibrations of matter into recognisable timbres and rhythms through habits of listening; the “instrument” keeps these vibrations within relations that can be repeated over time. As the order maintaining these relations slackens and fails, pitches and timbres may depart from what is expected. An instrument that has lost its original tuning can still sound, but may no longer answer to the familiar expectations of the performer.

So it is with ruins. If we can imagine a building as an instrument kept continuously silent, the anomalous sounds it gradually makes in ruin resemble a performance that is constantly going out of tune. Our hearing a scrape as a fault in the order, or classifying a vibration that fails to meet our expectations as disturbing noise, still owes something to our memory of intact architecture. These are neither the sounds of former everyday life nor the familiar peace of home. Though the ruin gradually returns to nature, it still carries the material order left by second nature. The old construction continues to shape how natural forces are transmitted and sounds formed. Thus, although these sounds have departed from the score we gave architecture, we still find it difficult to listen as we might to wind in the woods or running water, relinquishing the question of meaning and simply attending to the grace of natural sound.

A sound does not, in the instant it departs from our listening habits, enter a realm of absolute authenticity untouched by language and experience. We still instinctively try to locate its source and associate the sound of a malfunction with danger precisely because the former order of listening lingers in our consciousness. As ruination continues, sounds gradually depart from the delicate boundaries maintained by listening habits and systems of tuning. Through continual wear, architecture sheds the form imposed upon it by people, making us realise that what we once considered fixed forms are still subject to the continuing transformations of matter itself.[^38] Yet it is this unexpected departure that briefly draws us out of our established experience of listening. In a predictable performance, consciousness often draws upon past experience to arrive ahead of time at the note not yet played. An anomalous sound interrupts this rehearsal of the future, abruptly drawing our senses back to the vibration taking place. This is what I call the “absolute present”: reality momentarily breaks away from the form experience has prescribed for it, and we have to recognise anew the material event that is occurring.

The performance of a ruin as a disintegrating instrument is always full of suspense. An expected loosening may not happen for a long time, while another anomalous sound suddenly breaks out after a long silence. As we try to follow the sounds to understand the changes in the structure, our predictions of the next sound repeatedly come to nothing. Listening is thereby drawn into the time of the ruin itself. Between sound and silence, we await the next vibration that has yet to occur, and may encounter the moment when the structure finally tears apart, cutting through the present. Yet whenever sound gives way to silence again, we cannot tell whether the sound we have just heard was already the last note of the performance.

## Collapse at the Threshold: A Self-Destructive Finale

As the performance draws to a close, the remaining supports approach the threshold of failure. The long suspense the listener has experienced between sound and silence is finally interrupted by a last fall. The form that could previously still be sustained in the imagination has become an irretrievable past. The destruction of the work enlarges the fissures of failing order and fractured narrative to an immediately felt material scale, turning an otherwise abstract finitude into an event we witness ourselves. The body now losing its own form casts light upon the ultimate limits we ourselves cannot escape.

When the last precarious support fails, the disintegrating instrument built by the ruinwright's own hands sounds its final dramatic note as its structure comes apart — the “self-destructive last sound”. The instant the note sounds is also the instant the structure that enables it to sound gives way. At its birth it bids farewell to the possibility of ever being sounded again by this very body, becoming an unrepeatable material event. It is also a sudden exposure of the instrument, performer and conventions of the stage: precisely as music approaches its end, it reveals the material body on which it has always depended.[^40] The weight of this note comes from an almost mad act of surrender — the instrument stakes the remainder of its material life on this final sound. The ruinwright once gave his shapeless imaginings a material body and a finite life; in the last performance, that body finally completes its expression through its own disappearance.

@caption Fig. 31. Mauricio Kagel, Konzertstück für Pauken und Orchester (Concerto for Timpani and Orchestra, 1990–1992): the closing performance gesture and score materials. The performer breaks a specially made drumhead, bringing the final sound and the destruction of part of its sounding apparatus together on stage.

The ruinwright knows that former roles and scenes can no longer answer to one another, yet with the seriousness of a builder he composes a new performance among uninhabited remains, in a backstage area without an audience. By worldly measures of value, this decision to stake the work itself has an almost mad resolve, recalling the extreme theatrical image of lovers bound to an irrevocable ending: in the performance composed by the Fool's own hands, an affection that can find no eternal guarantee in the outside world is given such a tangible, irreversible cost.[^41] This cost cannot establish the truth of a belief, but lends actual weight to feelings otherwise difficult to measure. When the final expression of something built with such care coincides with the disappearance of its form, preservation, endurance and return — values long taken for granted — reveal the limits of their explanatory power. What I call the “beauty of fatal devotion” lies between this lucidity and madness: the Fool knows that he cannot promise eternity to the work he cherishes, yet still wishes to entrust it with such genuine feeling and a portion of his finite time.

Yet when the collapse he has so carefully choreographed finally reaches its end, the ruinwright gradually realises that this destruction is not enough to put him outside the stage. He once sought through the disintegration of his work to expose order's claim to permanence, but in the finale arranged by his own hands he also sees his own limits. He can create the conditions for a fracture, yet cannot decide the ultimate course of materials; and this act of building that sought to break away from established order itself depends on the knowledge and skills acquired within that very order. Even his imagination of freedom bears the traces of that world. For a time, the ruinwright used destruction to resist the wearing away of belief by reality. Only when the work actually loses its form does he realise that destruction cannot, after all, give any belief an eternal guarantee.

When the last sound fades and the stage and instrument fall silent together, there remains the land that was there before, and a set of remains made by his own hands. The old roles have long since left the stage. The role the ruinwright chose for himself loses its footing with the disintegration of the work, yet he is still standing there. The time spent on the work cannot be recovered, and the remains before him cannot answer the questions with which he began. Yet in this situation that never offered any guarantees, he did freely choose to build; he did experience, so vividly, the birth and departure of something he cherished. How, then, is the freedom briefly found among ruins to be brought back into the world that continues to operate? When we no longer have such a theatre in which to play our parts, how are we to face ourselves, equally finite and equally imperfect?

# Epilogue — After Ruinwork

The curtain has fallen. The work remains among the wreckage, but its maker must return to the world that carries on as usual. The freedom and joy found through building and disintegration did not vanish with the last fracture, yet cannot fill the detachment and emptiness that wash over us after the performance. We sought through destruction to expose order's claim to permanence, and saw the same absurdity and finitude in our own work. Yet when the forms with which we once resisted the world finally disintegrate, reality has not thereby changed. We remain products of this world, carrying all that we have inherited from it as we continue to live within its order.

Should the ruinwright insist on remaining among the ruins in the face of this emptiness, his fascination with decay may gradually turn towards nihilism. The solitude that once allowed us to face ourselves may become a condition in which we refuse to reconnect with reality. When the familiar world can no longer convince us, destruction can easily be mistaken for the most honest response to falsehood. Once only the breaking of established forms can make us feel something real again, the next collapse seems always to need to be more thorough than the last. Over time, the demand for destruction may harden into a habit that cannot be stopped. If we continually demand that things break in order to escape order, what we ultimately obey may be another prison reinforced by our own hands.[^42]

Beyond this cycle of destruction, however, ruins still retain the beauty that first drew us to them. Looking back at components I once joined with my own hands, now scattered among the rubble, I may better understand why I first felt tenderness towards other people's fragments. Foreseeing the eventual disappearance of a thing does not mean we need feel no sorrow when it goes; the wishes once invested in it do not lose their weight simply because we have recognised its limits. We can still grieve for departed lives and stop before beauty that emerges by chance among fragments. This feeling does not require another, more thorough destruction to sustain it.

Perhaps a return to an unadorned authenticity can only take place after such a realisation. We once sought to strip from things in ruins the appearance given to them by human-made order, searching for a purer authenticity of matter, only to discover that even the gaze with which we observe the world has long been shaped by second nature. We cannot completely strip these experiences and memories from ourselves, much less return to a primordial world untouched by civilisation. Yet after gradually discerning the world's complexity and acknowledging that we cannot exempt ourselves from its contradictions, we can still open ourselves to what is before us, as we did at first. That purity has not grown more distant as understanding has deepened. It now carries the hesitations, tenderness and self-knowledge we have since acquired, allowing us, even knowing that a thing will eventually lose its form, to approach it and feel the fleeting but real changes it undergoes.

Our bodies, lives and capacities all have limits, and we need not evade them. Precisely because our time and existence are finite, where we choose to spend them becomes a genuinely autonomous choice. Looking back over the years of wandering and building among ruins, I cannot retrieve the years spent, and the works may not leave behind results sufficient to measure them. I still want to understand ruinwork as a craft of “giving beauty to imperfect destruction”: faced with damage already done and impossible to reverse, borrowing forces not yet exhausted to make something that never existed stand for a while, then returning the form I gave it to time, knowing it will eventually depart. This beauty changes the way I encounter destruction, but cannot repay the losses others have suffered. The wild imaginings once entrusted to fragments also became, through this fleeting form, a part of reality we could experience ourselves. Even if this creation could not ultimately make the world perfect, and I still cannot transcend reality, the materials I touched with my own hands, the years I spent on site, and the beauty I felt among broken things have all really occurred in my life.

After leaving the ruins, the world is still complex, and I am still small and imperfect. The questions that remain unanswered will continue to unfold with the next journey and act of building; and I will still want to stop before the things that awaken my curiosity and my sense of beauty.
