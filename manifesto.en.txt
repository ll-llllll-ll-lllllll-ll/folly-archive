# Preface — After Architecture

Architecture shapes our lives almost imperceptibly. Long before we understand space, we have already absorbed doors, windows, walls, and the basic elements of buildings into common sense, and learned to dwell among them with ease. Since industrialization, buildings, technical infrastructures, and institutional rules have become ever more closely interwoven, gradually forming a continuously operating environment of life—what I call “second nature.” We are born, work, build, and seek shelter within it, until we come to mistake this extensively designed and maintained system for the world as it was given to us.[^2]

As second nature expands beyond us, rational calculation and technical means bring more of nature’s unknowns and hazards within the reach of prediction and intervention. Control over our surroundings and foresight about what lies ahead have repeatedly helped human beings resist danger and sustain life; it is precisely through those successes that the pursuit of certainty became embedded in the logic of civilization’s construction, eventually serving as a basic measure of whether life could be considered secure. Yet as that measure moved beyond the demands of survival and entered the organization of everyday life, the room for chance, deviation, and trial and error—the conditions in which life might grow of its own accord—steadily narrowed. Maintenance and care, once undertaken to protect life, gradually acquired the character of discipline under the demand that the system continue to run without interruption.

When we entrust everything bound up with our lives to second nature, maintaining the system’s stability gains an almost unassailable legitimacy. The distance established between humanity and nature to fend off danger has long since hardened into boundaries of order that now constrain human beings themselves. Rationalized operations continually repair damage and failure within the system, concealing the inherent fragility of human-made order beneath the appearance of perpetual maintenance, until it seems indestructible. We become entangled in a net of our own making, increasingly accustomed to understanding and arranging life by prescribed rules, and less and less able to imagine what might lie beyond the assigned course.

And yet, the ruin appears.

Ruins emerge along cracks long gathering in civilization’s hidden recesses, taking form wherever order can no longer sustain itself. As natural erosion strips away the surfaces of buildings, damage and fragility once concealed by their solid appearance are exposed anew. The costs of construction, use, and abandonment come to rest upon the surviving material forms. The same measures that once organized life, assigned value, and distributed resources now fall back upon civilization’s own creations, deciding which buildings deserve further care and which must be abandoned to dissolve in time. The ruin exposes the choices this order makes among its own works, and the costs those choices leave behind.

For a moment, ruins lend us the gaze of the “angel of history,” compelling us to look back upon the narrative that calls itself civilization and progress. Facing what has already happened, with our backs turned toward the future, we glimpse discarded remains and ideals cut short behind the spectacle of achievement. But the wind called “progress” continues to drive us forward, while wreckage piles up beneath and behind our feet. We have learned to account for these losses as the necessary expenditure of development; the road of modernity thus becomes inescapably strewn with ruins.[^3]

## Modern Ruins: The Strata of Progress

The road of modernity stretches onward, while the ground beside it accumulates the marks of what has been left behind: housing, industrial facilities, and roads interweave into a landscape so ordinary that it scarcely attracts notice. In the latter half of the twentieth century, New Topographics photography turned its gaze away from romanticized wilderness toward such everyday terrains, recording the traces of human construction with a cool, restrained eye. Those austere images reveal the second nature we have come to take for granted: human order has profoundly changed the face of the land, while daily familiarity has slowly dulled our sensitivity to those changes.[^4]

When this detached gaze reaches modern ruins, the losses long concealed by narratives of progress come into view. Yet confronted with these remains, so close to everyday life, we have become accustomed to treating their disappearance as the inevitable price of development. Why a building was erected, why it was abandoned, and what was lost as it passed away—questions that ought to provoke reflection—are displaced by ready explanations of efficiency, renewal, and progress. Over time, these explanations form a layer of structural ignorance, allowing us to witness the death of our own creations without asking seriously about its causes or costs, and to keep destruction at a reassuring distance.

Meanwhile, the road of modernity goes on, and abandonment accumulates as construction and renewal accelerate. Modern technology has greatly enlarged humanity’s capacity to alter its surroundings, while demands for spatial efficiency and changing functions have grown increasingly exacting. Buildings are erected and replaced in ever shorter cycles to meet needs that expand and shift with equal speed; amid repeated development and changes of use, many spaces lose the grounds for continued care long before their material lives are exhausted. Records of buildings proliferate, yet the experiences of life that are recognized and allowed to settle into collective memory are not necessarily preserved alongside them. The landscape of civilization is thus studded with remains that retain their forms even after the contexts of life have been withdrawn from them.

These creations, still short of the end of their material lives yet already deprived of social purpose, lie dormant beside the prosperity we inhabit. They give modern ruins their piercing untimeliness. Often they die too soon: before time can confer the halo of age, they face the prospect of being erased again.[^5] Such premature deaths resist being settled into the image of a melancholy or magnificent spectacle. Traces of lives not yet dispersed and material changes still underway leave a prolonged “aftershock” across their cold surfaces.

The ancient ruins familiar to us may be only a small fraction of countless structures that happened to survive. Whether a building endures depends on its materials and surroundings, but also on whether later generations protect it, repair it, and recognize its historical worth. More ordinary remains have vanished through erosion, demolition, and repeated building, leaving scarcely any way to recover even the appearance they had as they disappeared. Modern ruins, by contrast, linger for a time before that selection has run its course. Reinforced concrete still retains sharply defined human-made contours, though its functions and maintenance have departed; the structure gradually loosens under erosion and may be erased altogether by the next round of construction. We can thus draw close to an experience that has largely escaped the historical record: how structures never granted the halo of age, unattended through the years, disappear together with the very forms of their ruin.

The dust of history has therefore not yet settled over modern ruins. Their abandonment belongs to a relatively recent past, and their remains still linger near daily life. Their unfinished stories have not fully retreated into history, while surviving structures continue to change through erosion and collapse. Modern ruins remain so *close* to us: when we witness a familiar creation lose the order that sustained it, the confidence that the world around us might remain secure for a long time begins to loosen along the same cracks. The memories and costs that have not yet faded, together with this unfinished material transformation, remain entangled with our present. We cannot declare these works devoid of all value or meaning simply because their social purpose has ended.

Along the road of modernity, new construction presses ahead, while abandoned creations remain where they were left. Overlapping with buildings still in use, infrastructures, and traces of daily life, they gradually form a stratum of humanity’s own making, use, and abandonment—what I call the “Anthroposcene.”[^6] This stratum bears the recognized achievements of construction alongside residues not yet wholly forgotten, yet seldom admitted into collective memory. For me, the ruin is the form that absent time grows upon the earth: a history in which I took no part, a homeland that no longer coincides with memory, yet remains in the world through what survives of its body. Its fragments and broken narratives cannot bring the past back; but as it passes toward disappearance, they allow me, at least, not to be absent.

## Ruinwork and the Ruinwright

Facing the steadily decaying remains of buildings left by modern life, I have chosen to take hold of a portion of their unfinished disintegration through making. I call this practice, undertaken after architecture, “ruinwork.” It builds anew upon the structures and residual forces already present, incorporating the very process of becoming a ruin into the work’s construction, so that building continues through time after its form has been completed. The *Folly* series gives ruinwork a tangible body: the day of completion merely fixes its form for an instant in a posture already tending toward collapse; its further transformations are surrendered to time.

“Ruinwright” is the role I gradually established for myself through years of entering ruins, observing, and building. It has little to do with conventional professional qualifications, and more with the position I have found as an independent researcher through sustained practice. As a building’s original functions and rules of use begin to fail, fantasies for which ordinary construction offers no place can finally be tested in its remaining spaces. My investigations and creations have found a concrete place to stand. It is only here that I dare claim the word *wright* for myself, and continue to learn a craft still growing wild among ruins.

# Tenets of the Ruinwright

The “Tenets of the Ruinwright” arose through repeated building on site, and through the testing and gradual revision of initial ideas as materials and spaces underwent actual change. They follow the experience of entering a site and building within it: from first looking upon the remains and stepping inside, through the mechanical relations exposed by fractured materials, to the resonance of collapsing structures and the recording of time. These accumulated encounters eventually lead ruinwork to a question it cannot evade: knowing that every creation will disintegrate, why do we continue to build? Some tenets have been tempered through specific working methods; others remain judgments awaiting further trials. I set them down as tenets to leave a measure that future interventions can consult repeatedly, and revise whenever necessary.

## First Tenet: Ruin Lexicology

As second nature expands beyond the limits of individual perception and understanding, the everyday spaces organized by buildings and facilities become part of our lives before we truly understand their interiors. They even prescribe the spatial experiences we have come to regard as self-evident. We know what these spaces look like and what they are used for, but rarely understand the structures behind their walls and shells, or the complex rules that keep them operating. In time, we mistake familiarity for understanding. Only when the apparent solidity of a building fractures in ruin do the limits of the order that has long governed inside and outside begin to appear beneath its once-intact exterior. Through the exposed openings, we can enter and, among remaining structures and traces of operation, recognize anew a space we had taken entirely for granted.

Upon entering unfamiliar remains, their former names and scattered historical accounts can still identify what they once were, but they cannot explain what is happening there now. If we take a partial view and the recovery of past events for a complete understanding of the ruin, we turn it into an object: a historical relic imprisoned in memory’s “past tense,” flattened into a safe, static image that conceals the material weight still being borne, sinking, and breaking apart. Ruin lexicology attempts to return our gaze to the ruin’s “present progressive,” confronting the bare physical reality of the building: whether it continues to bear a load, has begun to tilt, or has already been pierced by roots. When the formula “former name + ruin” can no longer account for these continually changing differences, new names formed from the site itself become a starting point for rebuilding our understanding.[^7]

To locate this disintegration while it is happening, one must first confront the weightlessness felt when ordinary assumptions about architectural space fail altogether. When a building betrays its promise of shelter, walls and floors that once seemed firm may conceal danger. Familiar routes are cut off by rubble; neither the load beneath one’s feet nor the supports overhead inspire confidence. To stand among them is to be cast into wilderness in the dark. Yet it is in this perilous wasteland, through long wandering and close inspection, that the Ruinwright gradually tempers a pair of “wasteland eyes”: an ability to discern signs of instability hidden within the remains from cracks, rust, and erosion, and to remain almost instinctively alert to changes still to come. Sensibility repeatedly honed in dangerous places allows the Ruinwright to look beyond a building’s former appearance and purpose, to perceive the forces its surviving structure is bearing now, and the disintegration drawing near.[^8]

Through repeated observation and recording, the subtle differences in loading produced by collapse become clearer. New words are drawn from among the rubble and gathered into a “ruin lexicon”—an index of lexical elements recording the material conditions of remains and their evolution. Four kinds of elements overlap within it: terrain, architecture, physical state, and natural transformation. A site can thus receive a new name through its present spatial relations and material changes. The lexicon gradually assumes something of the rigor of natural-history classification, seeking to record with measured precision the ever-changing and irreproducible scenes of disintegration. Yet naming cannot fix a ruin in place; the vocabulary, too, must be revised as the site evolves. Ruin lexicology reconnects remains scattered across the world, allowing traces that resist ordinary spatial categories to disclose their relations through a growing vocabulary, until they form an expanding atlas of the world’s ruins.

## Second Tenet: An Archaeology of Things Close at Hand

When a building loses its function and maintenance, it does not immediately become nameless matter. In modern ruins this predicament is especially stark: destruction reaches things close to our own lives, and objects not yet old fall out of everyday use. The functions and signs that once belonged together begin to separate. Some objects retain a functional structure, although their original use can no longer be identified; others preserve only a name or recognizable form, while the practices to which they belonged have disappeared.[^9] Yet the patina and wear left by daily life remain, calling forth a familiarity we can readily recognize without necessarily possessing the memories from which it arose. These things close at hand hang awkwardly between “everyday refuse” and “cultural heritage.” Before they have had time to become ancient, they have lost the context of life that once made them self-explanatory. They belong to a past not far removed from ours, but forgetting has already made it necessary to approach that recent life almost as an archaeologist would.[^10]

The Ruinwright examines these remains with the cool gaze of “archaeology,” asking how things once intimately bound to daily life came to be abandoned. Lives that seemed close at hand must now be traced through surviving scratches and wear. Even when an object’s appearance is familiar, we may no longer recognize its use, much less recover the full experience of those who lived with it from scattered evidence. Forgetting has made objects that are scarcely old appear strange, while the severed ties between function and sign have turned what once went without saying into a history that must be excavated. The respect inherent in this gaze comes from bearing clear-eyed witness to incompleteness: acknowledging that lives now withdrawn truly took place, and that some part of them remains beyond our reach—deserving, before it disappears from memory altogether, to be regarded with gravity.

The Ruinwright may continue to build within surviving structures, even reconnect components long broken apart, but cannot thereby restore the lost connections between objects and the lives once lived with them. If, with limited knowledge and even the best intentions, we presume to fill gaps in the story that the evidence cannot resolve, the dense silence of the remains may be covered by facile sentiment. Ruinwork therefore restrains the impulse to reconstruct former lives, returning the work to the very breaks in narrative and matter, where surviving forms and scars may surface again. Deep within the ruin it suspends only a “spatiotemporal anchor,” a point at which one might pause and, through the remaining body, attend once more to those who built, used, cherished, and eventually departed from these things.

## Third Tenet: Borrowing the Ruin

When a building is intact and strong, human beings habitually domesticate nature’s inconstancy through boundaries and technology, bringing it down to the scale of a garden in which to dwell and take pleasure. Even mountains and rivers far beyond the grounds can be drawn through the window by means of “borrowed scenery,” becoming views within a human-made order. In such leisurely contemplation, people also admire their own power to define space and hold nature at bay, as though landscapes once encountered with awe had finally been brought within human measure. Yet this composure depends upon architectural shelter and continual maintenance. As the structures maintaining the boundary begin to break, what was framed beyond the window enters along the cracks. Rain wears away the walls, roots pry apart the masonry, and nature—once regarded as scenery—begins to occupy and rewrite the garden from within.

“Borrowing the ruin” arises from this reversal. The Ruinwright reads the remains by following nature’s return into space, borrowing the very force that garden order once sought to tame and exclude, and turning it upon architecture and the human will behind it. Through the fractured frame, the limitations of layered histories and ambitions become visible. Those who raised everything aloft have gone, but the surviving members still carry loads; their resistance to gravity has not ceased, as though a fall were suspended halfway to the ground. Here the Ruinwright seems, for a moment, to borrow nature’s inconstant gaze, looking along erosion’s openings at the finite nature of works that once proclaimed themselves imperishable.

This way of looking eventually enters the act of building. To “borrow” means that ruinwork must arise from structures, remaining materials, and ecological relations already present at the site. Just as guerrilla gardening finds usable ground and nutrients in neglected plots, the Ruinwright searches discarded bricks, cracked concrete, and remaining supports for forces not yet exhausted. Making begins by “borrowing matter” and arrives at “borrowing force”: responding to each ruin’s particular terrain and surviving form, making use of the existing load-bearing relations among fragments, and allowing openings made by natural erosion to participate in the formation of the work. Material conditions continually constrain and revise the initial conception, so that the new creation attaches itself to the ruin’s unfinished transformation. *Folly* takes form through such borrowing: it grows along the fissures of an order that has ceased to work, then borrows the force of nature’s continued incursion to carry the garden itself toward its final deconstruction.[^11]

To borrow is also, inevitably, to return. The first return in *Folly* takes place in its own deconstruction: spaces once bounded and domesticated by human beings gradually pass back to rain, plants, and time as the garden’s structures loosen and fall apart. The second return belongs to the Ruinwright. We borrow the remains’ materials and residual forces to build within them, but this gives us neither possession of the ruin nor the right to supply, from our limited knowledge, the missing histories of those who are absent. As this period of borrowing draws to a close, the Ruinwright must answer for the consequences of intervention and then make a dignified departure, neither covering the remains’ scars with a new order nor deciding their fate in the name of art. The work’s further transformations are yielded to nature; the space bearing its unfinished histories is returned, at last, to the ruin itself.

## Fourth Tenet: Brutal Materiality

Modern ruins often appear as exposed concrete frames. Exterior finishes that once concealed interiors and softened boundaries are the first to mold, peel, and fall away under weathering, until only the bones used for bearing load and dividing space remain. This irreversible stripping is like tearing away the false skin of “second nature,” exposing the most direct posture of structural force. Ruination inadvertently produces the aesthetic appearance of “Brutalism”; in fact, it simply returns architecture to a physical fact governed by gravity, corrosion, and lifespan.[^11]

Within these exposed, brutal structures there often condenses a powerful modernist impulse: once people mastered precise structural calculation and synthetic materials, they began to believe they possessed the power to command space. That precision displays the confidence of technological reason, but also reveals its desire to bring the environment completely under human order. Yet when the same frame becomes a ruin, this “brutality” develops an opposite tension—matter begins to struggle free of the will imposed upon it.

The “brutality” of the modern ruin is born from this fracture of contradiction. The frame left by precise calculation still stands, yet the materials that compose it have once again become subject to gravity, corrosion, and microscopic fracture. A highly developed technological inheritance and the most primitive, blind physical forces collide within the same damaged body, giving the remnant an untamable wildness. As the frame that once embodied order begins to fail under erosion, the “brutality” revealed by the fallen skin deepens into what I call “brutal materiality”—the naked body exposed after control withdraws. When ruined walls lean and force falls out of balance, rational calculation must face the weight of matter itself, the error inherent in materials, and a finite lifespan. Reason has not failed in the ruin; it can simply no longer stand above material reality.

Ruinwork therefore strips away added symbols and formal embellishment, allowing the work to carry, nakedly, the physical traces of how it was formed, how it bears force, and how it will eventually decay. The “brutality” here is not an aesthetic accessory attached to scenes of dilapidation. It arises from uncertainties deep within matter that geometry can never fully contain. Where are the true load-bearing limits of the modern materials in which humanity once took pride? Where will they resist, and where will they yield? The answer does not exist in drawings or calculations. It can only emerge from the materials’ actual properties through the prolonged struggle and confrontation of collapse.

## Fifth Tenet: The Force-Flow View

The displacement and disintegration of ruins under force are not accidental failures of a system. Traces of structural decay do more than signal the impossibility of restoration; the cracks that spread through them act like pointers tracing back toward the origin of destruction. Under continuous natural erosion, weak nodes and material fatigue make the precise moment of fracture unpredictable, yet the points at which forces accumulate and stress will ultimately concentrate are often unmistakable. Destruction, then, carries from the moment of construction a causal chain that can be traced backward through the structural remains.

Collapse inside a ruin is rarely a local, isolated event; it moves through chain reactions and implicates the whole. Fragments that fall violently often form new overlaps, braces, and suspended contacts by chance, assembling a damaged but still operative system of force transmission. From this, the Ruinwright establishes the “force-flow view”: broken walls, hanging beams, and leaning columns are no longer seen as static dead matter, but as a dynamic field of relations through which residual energy continues to pass and dissipate. Even in debris that appears still and balanced, countless connections and cracks alive with hidden currents have already inscribed the long process of destruction into the structure itself.

When ruinwork connects its mechanical transmission devices to these active or taut relations, otherwise invisible internal stresses are translated into observable displacement, vibration, and fracture. The apparatus takes on the task of tracing causality: it gives form to latent force flows and makes the resistance buried in matter legible along their paths. Yet this connection is never a manipulation from above. Once ruinwork takes up the ruin’s residual forces, it is irreversibly drawn into the same fate of disappearance. The Ruinwright may appear to arrange fragments, but in fact is caught within the same causal web of events: a seemingly trivial change can reshape a local field of tension, and even the smallest error can rewrite the outcome of the entire structure.[^12]

## Sixth Tenet: The Destructive Test Body

Beneath the illusion of stability—even permanence—woven by “second nature,” destruction is often abstracted into a kind of inevitable metabolism. We have not learned to resonate with the disappearance of our own creations; instead, we become numb to the process by which they decay. This numbness comes from a mismatch of perception: microscopic fatigue and damage in materials unfold too slowly for the human eye to register, while catastrophic collapse at the other scale is so violent that the physical energy it releases can crush the human body with ease. Between “imperceptible micro-change” and “unbearable rupture,” we lose any real bodily sense of a building’s lifespan. The “destructive test body” is born from this gap. It forcibly compresses a long, hidden process of decay into a medium-body capable of real-time feedback, returning destruction to a scale the eye and nervous system can directly perceive.

The “destructive test body” extracts key relations of material, joints, mass, and gravity from a ruin and reconstructs them as a body made for destruction. Displacement markers, load sensors, strain gauges, and vibration pickups serve as “sensory prostheses” that take risks in place of the human body. They translate hidden structural injuries invisible to the eye, and lethal tensions the body cannot safely approach, into visible and audible wounds, making the disintegration of a physical structure into an unfolding fact that can be witnessed at bodily range.[^13]

Just as human beings have used the figure of the android to ask where the boundary of “what makes us human” lies, the destructive test body probes the ultimate limit of second nature. It carries the structural logic on which second nature depends, yet precise calculation drives that very logic toward the threshold of collapse, allowing the test body to approach—and step across—the boundary of death that human-made things are not meant to touch. When the reason that creates order exposes, with its own hands, that order is not permanent, the test body becomes a brutal mirror: in its wreckage we see the finitude that neither human-made things nor the human subject can overcome.

Ruinwork turns this testing of finitude into a central creative method. In the Folly Series, the movement toward disintegration is placed at the endpoint from the very beginning of design. Once the planned loosening finally breaks open, environmental intervention and uncontrollable error rapidly take over, dragging the collapsing remains beyond what can be calculated or foreseen. Unscripted contingency flowers through the tearing, and a brutal beauty arrives with it. This beauty does not stand opposed to reason; it erupts precisely in the ruin’s “final resonance,” where reason has spent itself and can no longer answer on behalf of life.

## Seventh Tenet: Return to the Suspended Realm

Modern ruins often resemble shells whose souls have died before their bodies. Complex spatial frameworks once pushed to the limits of human use lose guidance, light, and occupation, and become dark, unfamiliar labyrinths. Doors no longer indicate entrances; corridors no longer guarantee arrival. Broken partitions and stairs that end in midair quietly betray the promise of shelter and strip the building of the safety it was meant to provide. These remains cannot reassemble the whole that has disappeared; instead, isolated limbs make absence newly visible. The wanderer moves through them facing structures that have lost meaning yet still carry load. With people gone, these “residual structures” turn from background into agents and establish a posthuman spatial order.

The ruin, evolving on its own, gives off an attraction charged with pressure. It seizes the gaze, yet presses back upon the viewer through an unresponsive silence, unsettling the assumed boundary between person and space. The building no longer receives its visitor hospitably; whoever enters must move carefully under the threats of leaning and falling. The “central position” human beings once imagined themselves to occupy proves, at the scale of the ruin, to be only a temporary vacancy left by time. Ornament, cracks, and plants begin to reorganize space according to their own rules, while human activity is in turn absorbed without mercy by the site. Suspended between the human-made and the natural, between former shelter and present abandonment, one is left without ground. From this weightlessness, the ruin’s “liminality” emerges.[^14][^15]

Ruinwork brings detached fragments back into contact and returns the gaze to a space that has already lost its human center. Scattered remains are joined again, but although the fragments carry memories of the past, they can no longer fit into a living context. They are assembled into an unfamiliar body that holds a grotesque and fragile balance under the continuous pull of gravity and corrosion. At first it appears as a hard foreign object wedged into the site; under the environment’s continued consumption, however, it becomes tightly entangled with the texture of the ruin, eventually mutating into a “spatial monster” that can no longer be peeled away from the site.

Yet the most conspicuous intruder on site is not this constructed body. Once the human position as spatial subject has been stripped away, the person who still insists on searching for coordinates and anchoring a sense of familiarity becomes the truly strange and untimely presence. Person and space are left together in a state that cannot land. It is at this moment, when both subject and object have lost their footing, that the “suspended realm” appears.

Faced with this suspension of the subject, the Ruinwright refuses to rebuild any false sense of command. Once placed on site, the work is already prepared to be exiled, estranged, and carried away from its original intention. Its fragments anchor memory toward the time before destruction, while its grotesque body pushes the gaze toward a sealed future—yet neither end can ever be reached. I build the Folly Series precisely because the shape of the other side cannot be foreseen. The work does not prepare an answer for the unknown; it merely lets familiar things, once stripped of their old relations, condense briefly into a nameless body before surrendering it without reserve to time.

## Eighth Tenet: Dissipation into Sound

When a dull cracking sound bursts from deep within a load-bearing structure presumed to be permanent, it instantly breaches a psychological line of defense—a condition that might be called “civilizational dissonance.” As a precursor to collapse, the anomalous sound is the first path by which that dissonance reaches the senses: the building has not yet physically fallen, but the shelter and safety it held in the mind have already collapsed.

This bodily unease comes from irreversible material imbalance within the ruin. Residual tension and gravity are released through friction and fracture and converted into vibrations in the air; the echo of dripping water and the howl of cross-drafts interweave into sonic traces of erosion. These sounds mark the structure’s continuing death after it has left human order behind, and at times seem to send an uncanny summons beyond the ruin itself. The space keeps people out through the threat of collapse, yet seizes the mind through hearing and pulls passersby back into a force field not yet spent.

Ruinwork inscribes this paradoxical listening experience back into the structure of the work. The Folly Series is set within an irreversible process of collapse, attempting to guide residual aftershocks in the remnant into another sounding construction already charged with tension. Precisely because these sounds violate the expectation that architecture should remain “silent,” passersby stop and listen, trying to locate where the fracture came from and when the next break will arrive. Listening to the ruin ceases to be passive reception and becomes a direct, bodily tracing of destruction, driven jointly by fear and curiosity.

## Ninth Tenet: The Blueprint Becomes a Score

From its first line, ruinwork binds the movement of fragments to the sounds produced by structural fracture, so that the work carries the rhythm of a ruin’s collapse from the design stage onward. When the Folly Series finally generates a real sound field through warped form and torn material, the engineering drawing that once organized its mechanical structure also becomes a latent acoustic score.

Plans and elevations are good at fixing a building that does not yet exist into measurable form, but they cannot record how space is actually experienced through time. Conventional architectural drawings usually lack a way to encode temporal unfolding; to register the passage of time, it must be translated into perceptible spatial traces. As disintegration on site escapes control, the drawing sheds its role as a set of prior instructions and takes on another task: pressure from external forces, stray noise, and material erosion begin to interweave and are written back into the page. The blueprint no longer tells the work where it should remain. It begins to record, step by step, how the work leaves its original position.

Once the blueprint begins to register degrees of damage, it no longer points toward an endpoint where everything converges on a result. It opens instead into a ruin dispersing toward the unknown, catching collapse and time as they pass. A conventional score disciplines sound through standardized notes and durations; the blueprint of ruinwork gives the nodes and lines on the page a more open reading. Anchors, load paths, and elevations are no longer promises of stability, but latent markers of stress, displacement, and spatial deformation about to break into anomalous sound. When cold geometric lines become thresholds for anticipated fracture, the blueprint finally crosses the boundary of engineering and changes into a graphic score for the rhythm of destruction.[^16]

Yet as the work’s disintegration drifts away from its planned trajectory and the original mechanical logic can no longer account for the wreckage at hand, the blueprint falls from “instructor” to “the thing being recorded.” Following the collapse, the Ruinwright draws the actual trajectories of falling components and the locations of sound sources back onto the page. Dust, water stains, and corrosion spreading across the work are likewise surveyed and marked. These recorded traces of physical decay force their way into the abstract blueprint, twisting and deforming the once-rigorous language of engineering through repeated staining and redrawing.

Natural erosion continues to rewrite the drawing throughout the Folly Series’ “performance,” turning it into a palimpsest of accumulated traces. Once fixed instructions have been covered by new trajectories, the blueprint that once presumed to choreograph the ruin completes its own ruination as well.

## Tenth Tenet: The Meaning of Futility

At the technical level, ruinwork operates much like conventional architecture, yet in motive it retreats into a realm of pure solitude: it is often carried forward quietly by me alone, deep inside a ruin. All social instructions have been stripped away—no client demand, no supervisor’s intervention, no calculation of capital return. Every concern contracts toward the physical reality of the ruin itself. Here, building is no longer a way to sell “shelter,” but a way to give concrete form to a fleeting process of destruction and lead it toward a carefully choreographed overturning. The final collapse of the Folly Series may appear to be triggered by years of accumulated external forces, but in truth the seed of destruction was buried on the first day of construction. Everything cultivated through ruinwork does little more than follow the irresistible laws of nature and escort the work toward the irreversible edge of disintegration.

When secular society measures the value of labor through “continuance” and “output,” pouring effort into a work destined for destruction seems to offer no return at all, almost an absurd exercise in futility. This utilitarian measure arrogantly stakes “beauty” on solidity and permanence, while readily condemning breakage, weathering, and disappearance as “failure.” The meaning of ruinwork lies precisely in staying within the fissure where that value system breaks: listening, in the ruin, to the material reality exposed after function has been exhausted, and testing the last load-bearing force a structure releases as it nears failure. The Ruinwright deliberately yields part of their labor to the dissipation of meaning and value, enacting an almost obstinate belief: anything that has received concentrated attention deserves unquestionable respect, even if it can never reach eternity.

The “meaning of futility” preserves dignity for labor that cannot be settled by return. Even if destruction is the work’s unavoidable destination, that does not erase the weight of bodily experience involved in making it, nor the aftershock left in the mind by witnessing collapse. Such labor does not need eternity to prove its value. If it truly took place in this unsupported ground, it is worth carrying through with everything we have.

## The Boundaries of the Ruinwright

Modern ruins cannot simply be absorbed into aesthetic or utilitarian systems of value, because they are not shaped by nature and time alone. The wounds of war, engineering accidents, and failures of decision all leave material consequences that cannot be worn away; traces of labor and human tragedy remain entangled with the wreckage in the same space. Weathering will alter the form of the remnant, but it has no right to erase responsibility for the historical events that produced it. Ruinwork must therefore hold to an ethical boundary: it refuses to turn real catastrophe into an abstract beauty of destruction, and it cannot allow aesthetic experience to conceal responsibility and trauma that still demand to be confronted.

Here, art sharpens the sensitivity with which a site is perceived, while architecture provides rational means for reworking matter and space. Yet when the Ruinwright enters the ruin carrying both, “restraint” becomes a discipline that must be kept. To possess the ability to rearrange matter and extract meaning is to require an even firmer suppression of the urge to “reshape” the ruin. One should not rashly interrupt the slow takeover already underway through weathering and erosion, nor impose our limited understanding of beauty upon a process of disintegration that has not yet taken form. We must also remain wary of the language we build ourselves: once concepts begin to summarize the ruin in its place, ruinwork risks becoming another layer of “second nature.” Theory can offer a path into the site, but it gives us no right to look down upon it.

Recognizing this limit, the Ruinwright respects both nature’s long, ungovernable force and the enormous costs sedimented by human order, and enters alone into the ruin’s still-unfinished collapse to face the site’s loss of control and danger: allowing sharp fragments to cut the palm, passing through thorns, wading into mud. Only by casting the body into this danger for long periods of surveying and listening can one truly take up the ruin’s residual sound.

The Ten Tenets do not exhaust the understanding of ruins. They are only provisional rules established through a handful of practices in the present, and they will continue to revise themselves through future exploration. In the ongoing study of ruins, what ruinwork reveals no longer remains confined to historical traces and causes of destruction. It also includes the question of why I am drawn to them again and again, why I keep returning to these remains, and why I am willing to stay so long in this boundless desolation.

# The Broken Frame — From the Picturesque to the Sublime

At first, ruins often enter aesthetic vision through the gentle appearance of the “picturesque.” Under the shared erosion of time and nature, an intact building loses its polished surface and moves toward damage, yet among rubble, moss, and mottled weathering it can produce a fleetingly romantic posture. Each time we stop to admire a ruin, the “picturesque beauty” we capture is, in essence, only a composition accidentally frozen within the process of destruction.

## Picturesque Beauty: Bringing the Ruined Scene into the Frame

Picturesque beauty not only extracts from nature’s ceaseless change a view “fit for a picture”; it also answers a modern fascination with ruined landscapes torn by time, full of incompleteness and abrupt change. In pursuing the picturesque, the viewer longs to peer into loss of control and danger, yet must also wrap them in layers of romantic filtering and retreat into a safe aesthetic experience. Through this psychological distance, an invisible “frame” is quietly erected: damage is brought inside the frame, danger is temporarily tamed by composition, and destruction hardens into a landscape outside oneself, as though that instant of collapse took place only within the picture.[^17]

Historically, the artificial ruins—the follies—of traditional European gardens sought to domesticate damage, collapse, and natural encroachment into a sequence that could be designed and controlled for viewing. They compressed vast, prolonged decay into the miniature landscape of the garden, attempting to hold picturesque beauty at the most pleasing instant of contemplation.

The frame that keeps danger at a distance has not disappeared in modernity. It has been internalized as a subtler, landscape-making gaze. This way of looking intervenes in a site that is still disintegrating and tries to flatten a long collapse into a visual cross-section that can be extracted at will, giving wreckage a false stability. Every attempt to freeze a ruin may seem to preserve a historical trace, yet it also drains weight from space: tangled distances, gravitational threat, and passing time—the things a body must negotiate on site—are brutally reduced to a safe, flat image. What remains in public memory is therefore often the still instant most suited to viewing, while the physical space-time that has to be measured with exertion and felt through bodily risk is stripped away.[^18]

Real ruins, however, do not obey this false frame. Even when natural damage is deliberately guided into surviving structure, we cannot stop or reverse the erosive force that continues to strip away flesh and bone. A single storm, or the loosening of one load-bearing node, can wrench the ruin away from the perfect state most suited to viewing. Once we understand that the picturesque form cannot be fixed, and that human attempts to preserve it cannot arrest the ruin’s self-erasure, collapse arrives with actual physical weight and smashes the stillness of the picture.[^19]

The channel of looking and the safe distance sustained by picturesque beauty collapse with it. The sublime finally appears from beyond the broken frame: the form before us may be swept away by the next wave of destruction, and the viewer’s formerly secure sense of safety begins to shake under this pure physical violence.[^20]

The “Broken Frame” (fig. 1) gives concrete form to this failure of aesthetic distance. From the moment the frame itself begins to become ruin, it loses the capacity to hold fleeting change still as landscape. With everything once contained inside the frame now absent, the damaged skeleton that had only marked a boundary turns into the object of the gaze. Its exposed breaks and misaligned layers force the eye inward, toward the lethal force that tore everything apart.

## The Folly Series: Releasing the Frame

Traditional classical gardens often used “borrowed scenery” to draw nature into an order of viewing: nature, tamed, was carefully selected and cut into a framed scene. But when humanity’s self-proclaimed “domestication” finally fails under time, and architectural order collapses under natural erosion, the relation between subject and object reverses irreversibly. The wildness once held outside the frame seeps through cracks into the building and, through continued stripping and counter-erosion, takes control of space back from within.

The Folly Series begins from this reversal. Intervention starts from the condition of ruin; nature is drawn back inside along existing cracks, where it works against the shell of human-made order and brings a slow deconstruction to the building. The Folly Series also borrows the name of the garden folly, the “artificial ruin,” yet strips away almost all of its picturesque warmth and amplifies instead the destructive force that cannot be controlled. The “corpse of an object” once arranged for human viewing no longer remains a safe scene; as it continues to disintegrate beyond control, it forces the viewer to confront their own smallness.

To reach this point, the Ruinwright plants the “seed of disintegration” directly into the work’s underlying logic of construction, making destruction part of the work itself. Structures poised out of balance and materials scored in advance with fracture lines embed possible routes of loosening and collapse within a state of suspension, so that the entire installation, even while still standing, remains charged with the physical tension of an impending fall.[^21]

Against the contemporary tendency to freeze experience into static form, the Folly Series offers a physical counterstroke. It refuses to treat any preservable visual state as the complete experience of the work; what it offers instead is a process that remains in disintegration. The viewer moves, waits, and listens inside it, while material and structure continue to redistribute themselves through time. No single attempt to take in the whole can stand in for entering the site again.

Ruinwork therefore no longer treats the preservation of experience as its endpoint. It produces a site that can exist only through real-time movement and the passage of time. Here the body and perceived space fully coexist. We are no longer detached viewers standing outside the work, but bodies enveloped by a space that is itself coming apart.

Within this preconfigured disintegration, the unavoidable weakening of the human-made object becomes impossible to miss. In losing control, we rediscover an anxiety bound to survival and touch a form of the sublime that passes beyond the safe boundary of the picturesque to confront material disorder itself. We are thrown into a suspended zone thick with danger: the outline of space remains, but the shell of safety and shelter has already collapsed with the sound of fracture.

## Ruins of the Mind: A Fractured Frame

The Folly Series pushes the viewer toward the leading edge of a loosening frame. The picturesque observer who first lingered before ruins was accustomed to sealing destruction inside the damaged image, preserving the safety of life outside it. That boundary could feel secure because second nature not only assigned order and destination to things; it also marked out for the human being an apparently immovable position as subject within the world.

Only when the cracks in the wall visibly spread beneath one’s feet does collapse reveal itself as a physical violence still moving forward. Once the viewer understands that the same erosion could occur in any familiar place, the sublime in nature breaks through the aesthetic boundary and begins to consume, in reverse, the order by which the viewer understands the world. In this comprehensive loss of ground, the human space on which life depends collapses inside consciousness, while objects once carefully cherished lose their weight in an emptiness that answers no one. Familiar architecture becomes a “lost space.”

As the grand narratives attached to space withdraw, the psychological shelter provided by crowds and everyday life evaporates with them. This becomes a brutal form of “homesickness while still at home” (solastalgia): home remains where it was, yet can no longer coincide with memory. It is familiar enough to be recognized as home, yet too strange to carry the years one did not live through. Here, a person is forced to face histories in which they did not participate and countless possible lives that came to nothing. The absolute solitude of this encounter arises from a real and silent absence: familiar life has left the stage, while the cold space remains.

The inner sublime erupts precisely from this deprivation in which distance can no longer be maintained. Once observer-like mourning is gone, “lost space” forces viewer and wreckage into the same irreversible physical time, confronting us with the fact that we too cannot escape disappearance. Things before us that still possess weight and warmth will eventually decay into unregarded residue on a historical cross-section; when the same fate reaches the self, the pressure of helplessness spreads from the past into the present. Under the void’s pressure on both body and mind, one instinctively clenches the hands, trying to pull from emptiness one last tangible sense of continuing to exist.

## A Mirror of the Modern Mind: The Phantom of the Empty Frame

Within this unsupported void, consciousness becomes unusually clear. Once we understand that matter cannot return to the past and time cannot reverse, the one who perceives their own smallness and continues to respond after meaning has dispersed is still the same “I.” Will appears here as the act of affirming oneself within finitude and deciding how to continue. One recognizes oneself again: small, yet real; finite, yet still continuing.

The damaged beauty of the “Broken Frame” thus becomes a mirror for the mind. When lost space withdraws every familiar response, and we enter this solitude and near-absolute self-scrutiny, a question begins to press back upon us: beyond the faces we have passively formed in order to accommodate the world, what still belongs to us?

The fissure in the ruin symbolically breaks through this false mirror woven from order: it pierces not only the shell of the object, but also tears open the gap where the “world of identity” fails to mesh with reality itself. Solitude does not return a purer self to us. It simply withdraws, with some violence, the voices of order that normally answer on our behalf, allowing desire, fear, and impulses not yet discovered to rise again along the cracks of disintegration.

# Ruin Imagery — Where Desire Goes

Born into modern society, we have inherited ways of judging things through order, efficiency, and use, and internalized them into an almost instinctive gaze. The modern ruin is the break left by that logic. Once value disappears, the utilitarian gaze reaches for purchase and finds none in a remnant from which no profit can be drawn. Yet when “beauty” strikes from the fracture in that instant, we do not simply leave; we continue along the crack, looking for the source of the desire that made us look.

## The Broken Prism: Reading the Fracture Plane

If the mirror of the “Broken Frame” notices a mismatch when the gaze turns back upon itself, the “Broken Prism” looks through that mismatch and begins to reveal how “second nature” refracts a disorderly reality into legible spectra. Ordinarily, the glass that performs the refraction is scarcely noticed. Only once the prism breaks do its actual form, stress, and fragility appear at the fracture. A ruin works in the same way. When the order and maintenance that sustain the surface are cut off, matter reappears as weight, corrosion, and lifespan; the loosening of a single node can expose an entire structural system. By the same logic, a failed building can pull into view the civilizational structure that once sustained it.

The broken prism grants no omniscience. It only lets relations suppressed beneath an intact surface appear at the break. Its beauty therefore remains in a dislocation that is difficult to settle: the whole image collapses, yet the fragments acquire new reflections. Drawn to this reflected light, the viewer encounters not a romantic explanation laid over damage, but a poetry that erupts from the materiality of the break itself.[^22]

Fear of collapse and a cool kind of consolation occur together in this tremor of beauty. As the former city continues to fail, it exposes its material body and reflects the fragility of the order people built. The tension not yet exhausted after destruction cannot be fully accounted for by utilitarian measures, nor can the brilliance that sometimes appears within destruction. The ruin thus reveals the finitude of human-made things in both life and intention: we want meaning and beauty to endure, yet some ideals generate new tragedies in the act of being realized, while others lose their echo when the people or structures that carried them disappear.

## The Ruin Romantic: Tenderness of Fragments

Looking inward along the crack, a deeper tragedy of modern life comes gradually into view. The operating order that provides shelter and predictability has also, almost invisibly, compressed the room in which contingency and perception can breathe. When utilitarian standards of what can be measured, verified, and used dominate judgment for too long, experiences that resist quantification are pushed to the edge. The ruin gives these marginal experiences a heavy physical weight. In disintegration, it confirms a tragic condition of modern life: the capacity to feel beauty has not disappeared, yet it is forced to draw from pain and destruction themselves.

The desire drawn toward destruction, unable to resist moving closer to ruins, is what I call the “ruin complex.” It is fascinated by time’s ruthless carving of matter and by the violent entanglement of decay and new growth within the same form, lending disappearance a melancholy attraction that is difficult to refuse. What we recognize in the wreckage is not cheap tenderness but an almost obstinate form of care: a longing for the residual warmth of a world not yet entirely gone, and a determination to stop for things that have lost their use. Such care cannot reverse disappearance, but it forcibly draws the remnant into the present moment of our own existence and lets us confirm, through it, that we are still capable of feeling beauty.[^23]

This care for incompleteness ultimately becomes a starting point for building again on a narrative wasteland. Scattered remains resemble broken lines of verse, preserving one last trace of warmth for those still trying to make out a contour among ruins. The Ruin Romantic takes position in the fissure between destruction and survival: it wants to sacrifice every residual force to the instant of collapse, yet also tries, futilely, to stitch some glimmer of the past from fragments. Fragments cannot restore a lost age, much less fulfill the promise of a perfectly completed “redemption.” Yet before an irresistible disappearance, they refuse nihilism’s total nothingness and allow this desolation to become a place from which beauty can again be discovered, affirmed, and created.[^24]

## The Bearing of the Fool: A Utopia of Fragments

Once the desire to rebuild has receded, the ruin remains suspended in a time that resists resolution. The broken walls have not returned to life, yet neither have they found an ending in collapse. Amid the sustained tension woven from splendor not yet dispersed, unrealized wishes, and belated destruction, the ruin rises from the background of the story and becomes its narrative subject. It wakes from a long suspension and neglect, retakes the center of attention through the violent transformations born within destruction, and provokes an almost hallucinatory expectation—as if something inside that mixed body has not yet been spent and is waiting to be found.

To approach the ruin at this point is to encounter pity, curiosity, and the thrill of looking where one perhaps should not, all tangled together. In that expectation, we search among mixed fragments for what has been concealed, while feeling deeply drawn to the loosening of order and the failure of established form. This exploration exceeds everyday common sense and utilitarian purpose. It lets fragments that once had nothing to do with one another form relations through chance encounters, and it releases a creative desire long held down. Here, exploration leaves weightless fantasy behind and cuts directly into the reality lodged in the ruin’s physical weight: facing, among the wreckage of massive things, the force that tore order apart; experiencing the withdrawal of the world in abandoned corners; even accepting bruises and pain as an unspoken cost of entering this wilderness. It is after this prolonged negotiation and listening that making reaches things long hidden beneath the surface and waiting to be awakened. They have been forming in this desolation for a long time and already possess their own narratives and bodily attitudes. Ruinwork does not sew fragments back together to restore the past. It follows the force that has sedimented there, reconnecting residues so they answer the expectation still unspent deep within the mixed body, and allowing dormant forces to reappear in the encounter of the present.

In response to this listening and answering deep within the texture of wreckage, the Ruinwright enters the site in the figure of the “Fool.” Here, the Fool is a posture that deliberately departs from social utility and the discipline of identity. In the desolation of the ruin, he looks for freedom among possibilities not yet exhausted by established conclusions and reawakens the most primitive impulse to build. Some resistance to the existing order may remain at first; but after long experience of this “freedom with nothing to possess,” what remains is the sheer pleasure of discovery and creation itself.

The Fool is the role temporarily assumed by the Ruinwright; the Folly is the material form taken by that performance. The former enters the wilderness without being completely disciplined by utilitarian measures. The latter, as an act of “folly” indifferent to gain or loss, gives material form to an action that knows it cannot last and responds anyway, condensing into a brief stay within a “bearing of the Fool.” It is a kind of Ruinwright’s stubbornness: knowing form cannot resist time, yet insisting that something not yet in existence should stand, however briefly, and be given a dignified appearance.[^25]

The Fool walks toward the cliff so that potential is not exhausted in advance by experience and conclusion. On this fleeting stage, the real desire behind looking at ruins can finally take material form, giving that desire, in return, a place on which feeling can be spent. Above the ruin, the Fool’s step remains light—not because he is ignorant, but because he knows clearly that nothing beneath his feet is guaranteed, and has not forgotten the weight those stones once bore. In this end-of-world landscape, he chooses for himself what to believe, and in a place without support continues to walk, and continues to create.[^26]

# Final Resonance — Playing After the Curtain Falls

Second nature resembles a tightly run theater in which people and things keep to assigned scripts. But when the chain of order breaks, abandoned ruins become the backstage left behind after the theater withdraws. The larger drama can no longer assign each thing its place; functions and identities lose their settings. The ruin does not hurry to provide a new answer. It simply exposes the wreckage left by the collapse of order as it is—a chaotic place not yet classified or gathered back under reason, where everything retains an unnamed, almost primordial appearance.[^27]

In this groundless field of freedom, the Ruinwright finds a stage on which to release a “madness” of their own. This madness is not mindless destruction. In the backstage of failed order, it means deliberately taking on the role of a fool who does not calculate gain or loss, resisting second nature’s instinct to annex the unknown and smooth away strangeness. The Ruinwright delays rational judgment so that the loss of control, fear, and sublimity awakened by the unknown can remain for a while. In that brief departure from order, we can draw closer to a chaos that has not yet taken shape and slowly build our own understanding and rules through sustained negotiation with the ruin. The Folly Series becomes, within this lucid madness, a material projection through which the impulse toward ruination can be released.[^28]

## Postdramatic Space: Backstage

Many modern ruins abandoned in the wake of development and expansion remain in the afterwash of the wave called “progress.” Their time appears to have stopped only because they have fallen out of the viewpoint that races along with that wave. Seen from the ruin itself, however, the stranded wreckage was never carried forward by progress. The speed and inertia of the passing wave did not take it away; they tore it into damaged bodies. Here, the ruin continues to change. Aftershocks and consequences that did not end when the wave receded replace the image of life before abandonment and become the narrative subject of the space in the present.

Within this derailed time, modern remnants retain an unfinished disintegration. Past upheaval lodges in fractured surfaces, while unstable residual structures continue to push the event into the future. Among walls and wreckage stranded by the current of the age, a distinctive “postdramatic” condition emerges: there is no script prescribing the future, and no attempt to restage the past. Residual stress and invasive natural forces entangle fragments in moment-to-moment change, producing a stage and a site-specific aesthetics that belong to the ruin itself.[^29]

If a ruin is the form architecture leaves at the far end of time, the Folly Series writes that endpoint into its own construction in advance and enters it through self-disintegration. The work carries a tendency to dismantle the form of its own death, and in doing so begins to see from the position of a “symbolic remainder.” Built from the signs of civilization, it gradually strips off that skin as it breaks apart and remains in civilization’s interior as a body that has lost civilization’s appearance, tearing open the once-seamless surface of symbolic order. The viewer, placed within this act of looking, can likewise step partly outside the narrative of order and watch how civilization moves from construction toward dissolution.[^30]

## Becoming an Instrument through Disintegration: A Deconstructive Performance Method

The ruin’s most immediate image is the fragment, but its most bodily experience often arrives as sound. During habitation, a building maintains a stable silence. Yet when that stability finally reaches the day it can no longer hold, a sudden crack—perhaps from a corner beyond our sight, perhaps from deep inside the structure—breaks the long quiet. The anomalous sound punctures the established order of space because it belongs to no timbre we can easily place within ordinary experience.

Sound draws us back to the site more readily than image does, not because it is somehow more real than vision, but because it cannot exist outside time. A fracture must happen, travel, and decay. As the listener tracks its source, they also wait for the next anomalous sound and continually recalibrate their distance from the structure. Ruinwork brings sound into the work not to add another layer of representation, but to use sound’s dependence on time to turn perception back into an experience that continues to happen. The structure is changing; sound appears, fades, and disappears with it; the body is forced to remain with that change in a present where collapse is not yet over.

If “not sounding” can itself be understood as an instruction of order, then a building in ordinary use is a made thing instructed to “remain silent.” A conventional musical instrument combines otherwise silent materials with a fixed order, maintaining a strict set of relations in order to produce predictable tones. The logic of the ruin runs the other way. Material is never absolutely silent; what we experience as architectural silence is the long-term restraint of vibration and displacement by architectural order. Once the building moves inevitably toward ruin, it must pass through collapse, and in coming apart it must sound. Matter released from order begins to reveal its own acoustics; collapse itself becomes the instrument’s real performance.

To restage this process of stripping away order and releasing sound, the Ruinwright connects dispersed fragments through a secondary transmission structure. This intervention does not tame sound. It gives stresses hidden deep inside the structure a body through which to vibrate. The Folly Series thus appears as an instrument “still played by collapse itself after order has left the stage.” Although the mechanical system is visibly shaped by rational design, what it produces is abrasive and unruly. For the first time, the absurdity that follows broken order acquires a physical impression that can be heard in the body: its meter is no longer controlled by human intention. It dissects the death of architecture directly and extends that dissection toward the deeper structure of order itself.

## From Detuning to Return: The Note That Burns Itself Out

Whether order remains intact or falls apart, physical materials never cease to vibrate. What we have learned to recognize as a musical instrument is already held together by two forms of discipline and arrangement. The “musical” is established through cultural inertia and habits of listening, carrying narratives shaped by order; the “instrument” is defined by utility and structural performance. Once both frameworks lose their hold, what remains is the roughest physical fact of the site: sound itself—noise before classification, not yet domesticated by any system.[^31]

Every musical instrument relies on tuning and structural constraint to hold material and force within a precise balance, allowing vibrations produced through expenditure of energy to fall inside a perceptual system accepted by human culture. When erosion, deformation, or loss of tension occurs, sound drifts across the delicate boundary maintained by listening habits and tonal systems and is judged to be “out of tune”—an error that exists only under particular human rules and habits. Strip the situation back to the material level, however, and the component is simply loosening itself from imposed restraint and moving toward another, more natural physical state. Ruins undergo the same transformation.[^32] Once social function and cultural narrative withdraw, the institutional order that once arranged material begins to dissolve, but the components themselves do not stop acting. Under gravity and weathering they continue to collide, vibrate, and fracture, releasing sounds that have slipped free of arrangement.

The Folly Series constructs precisely such a “collapsing instrument,” one that passes step by step through breakdown and disintegration on site. It makes sound through fracture, while irreversibly consuming itself in the performance and gradually losing the rules that once made it an instrument. The moment we notice this detuning, we are abruptly stripped out of familiar experience and pulled into an absolute present, forced to face sound before established meaning or concept has touched it. In this unrepeatable performance, playing is the full course of disintegration. Sound approaches its limit through expenditure, waiting only for the final instant when the structure tears to its end and cuts across the present.

## Collapse at the Threshold: A Self-Destructive Finale

As the performance nears its end, stage and instrument converge in the same body on the verge of dissolution. The Folly Series faces the energy of destruction with its own structure, allowing the order that sustained it to be completely dismantled at the ear through sounds that are themselves coming apart. The work’s destruction enlarges the cracks in order to a material scale that can be bodily felt; through this destruction, the viewer asks whether they have touched something closer to the real. The body losing its form also turns back toward us, reflecting a final limit that we share.

To make a ruin from intact materials, then let it move deliberately toward destruction and release unordered noise through collapse—only an act that looks nearly “mad” by ordinary utilitarian standards can cut a finite opening into the dull surface of order and allow the randomness long flattened by the system to appear in public. Measured by social reality, the decision to stake the work itself carries a certain finality. It makes the “irretrievable” into a severe measure. The Folly Series takes on this irreversibility of self-destruction, staking its physical body on a decisive confrontation and using the defeat of collapse to tear through the disguise of symbolic order, exposing its illusion and cost among the rubble.

When accumulated force finally crosses the threshold, the last vibration condenses into a “self-destructive final resonance.” Sound breaks through discipline in an instant, because no existing note can contain the raw noise released by disintegration. The moment of sounding is also the moment the sounding structure comes apart. This simultaneity severs the note, at birth, from the possibility of ever appearing again and turns it into an unrepeatable material event. The stage withdraws at the instant the performance is complete; the rules that sustained instrument and stage collapse with the structure. This final resonance does not drive destruction toward a sentimental dramatic climax. It simply lets the work arrive, quietly, at the terminal state of ruination written into its fate from the beginning.[^33]

# Epilogue — After Ruinwork

When the “self-destructive final resonance” finally sinks into silence, what remains on site is not only dismantled wreckage, but a lingering sense of detachment and vacancy that spreads through the mind. Our fascination with this self-destruction inadvertently tears open a deeper exhaustion of the postmodern city: when the systemic breakdown around us cannot be resolved, we learn to live alongside decay, drawing from ruins a kind of lucid despair through a “detached acceptance.”

This romanticized immersion in decline is born from a fracture in the psyche. It places the inner life in absolute solitude, tries to reduce the world to ruin, and takes destruction as a paradoxical release. Once this immersion in failure hardens into a psychological norm, nihilism begins to grow at the threshold between despair and aestheticization. The true danger of nihilism lies in mistaking destruction for the only honest response to falsehood. If reality can be proven only through breaking things, action must become ever more extreme, until negation itself becomes a new rule and traps the person again inside a prison repeatedly reinforced by self-destruction.[^34]

Yet beyond this negative logic of destruction and repeated negation, ruins disclose another romantic possibility. We still stop before things that have lost their use. We still find beauty in fragments that can offer no return. We may even give time to something we know cannot last. These experiences neither conceal the absurd nor repair reality, but they show that human sensitivity has not been completely occupied by utility, identity, and signs. Here beauty sheds the color of escape and becomes a kind of innocence in bodily contact with what is before us: after seeing through calculation, we still choose to return, opening ourselves again before the thing is taken back into the law of utility.

With this resolute openness, ruinwork no longer claims redemption, nor does it remain intoxicated by self-destruction. It turns the hammer instead toward the limits of the body, breaking apart boundaries fixed by rules. When material constitution and the image of identity are both stripped away, the ruin tears through the false infinity projected by human-made order. Yet within this finite damaged body, the inexhaustible continuity of nature and matter becomes visible again. Beauty erupts from this materiality and residual force as they break through restraint.

The human body, life, and capacity all have limits, and we need not flee from them. Precisely because time and existence end for us, where we choose to give them becomes a genuinely autonomous decision. The essence of ruinwork is, through each event of ruin, “to give beauty to imperfect destruction.” This beauty never excuses destruction, nor does it aestheticize the pain behind it. It shows only that within a finite life, we once stood bodily at the site of destruction and affirmed existence through the act of making.

[^6]: “Anthroposcene” is a working term reused and redefined in this essay; it is not the geological “Anthropocene.” Noel Castree has used Anthroposcene to refer to the networks of knowledge, institutions, and publications formed around the Anthropocene. My usage is different: the Chinese term “人类层” (“human layer”) uses “layer” to refer to a continuously accumulating human-made reality produced through long periods of construction, use, abandonment, and recording. Buildings, infrastructure, waste, and traces of everyday life overlap within it, while modern remnants remain on its surface as residues not yet fully absorbed into history. In material terms, this usage is adjacent to discussions of the archaeosphere, anthropogenic deposits, and technostratigraphy. See Noel Castree, “Changing the Anthropo(s)cene: Geographers, Global Environmental Change and the Politics of Knowledge,” Dialogues in Human Geography 5, no. 3 (2015): 301–316, https://doi.org/10.1177/2043820615613216; Matt Edgeworth et al., “Diachronous Beginnings of the Anthropocene: The Lower Bounding Surface of Anthropogenic Deposits,” The Anthropocene Review 2, no. 1 (2015): 33–58, https://doi.org/10.1177/2053019614565394; Jan Zalasiewicz et al., “The Technofossil Record of Humans,” The Anthropocene Review 1, no. 1 (2014): 34–43, https://doi.org/10.1177/2053019613514953.

[^7]: “Ruin lexicon” and “ruin lexicology” are working concepts proposed in this essay. The former refers to the Ruinwright’s personal vocabulary formed through long periods of wandering; the latter refers to the method of identifying, combining, and indexing these semantic units. Names compress structural condition, sedimented time, environmental intervention, and perceptual experience into semantic relations that can continue to expand. They do not refer to an established branch of linguistics.

[^8]: Here, “wasteland” refers to the condition of the modern ruin before it has been placed by history, while the consequences of destruction continue to shape it.

[^9]: I use the English word relic for “遗存” in order to emphasize an object that, after losing its original use, continues to carry cultural, mnemonic, and material traces. This is a working translation used in this essay, not the only archaeological translation of relic.

[^10]: “Interstitial garden” is a working concept proposed in this essay. It draws from guerrilla gardening’s informal use of idle and neglected spaces, but does not inherit the element of unauthorized intervention. The structural interventions described here remain bounded by the permissions, safety requirements, and memory of place discussed above. See Richard Reynolds, On Guerrilla Gardening: A Handbook for Gardening without Boundaries (Bloomsbury, 2008).

[^11]: “Brutalism” here is a reinterpretation made specifically in relation to ruined structures and is not equivalent to the full architectural-historical definition. On early discussions of the ethics, form, and materiality of New Brutalism, see Reyner Banham, “The New Brutalism,” Architectural Review 118, no. 708 (December 1955): 354–61.

[^12]: The “force-flow view” is a working concept proposed in this essay. “View” names a basic way in which the Ruinwright observes and understands things: architecture and ruins are treated as relational fields in which forces continually accumulate, transmit, redirect, transform, and dissipate. The displacement of one component alters the whole through its connections, so collapse appears as a continual rewriting of load paths.

[^13]: The “destructive test body” is a working concept proposed in this essay. It refers to a finite test body constructed separately by extracting a set of key conditions from a building’s materials, connections, mass, and force relations. It is used to observe failure; it does not substitute for the building itself, nor does it justify arbitrary damage to actual remnants.

[^14]: In the “Travel Souvenirs” section of One-Way Street, Walter Benjamin writes of the Alcázar of Seville that human movement is absorbed by the “soundless tumult” formed by ornament. I extend this spatial experience to the process by which cracks, plants, and nonhuman forces reoccupy ruins. Walter Benjamin, One-Way Street and Other Writings, trans. Edmund Jephcott and Kingsley Shorter (New Left Books, 1979), 82.

[^15]: “Liminality” originally refers to a transitional stage in which a subject has left a former identity but has not yet entered a new one. See Arnold van Gennep, The Rites of Passage, trans. Monika B. Vizedom and Gabrielle L. Caffee (University of Chicago Press, 1960), 10–11; Victor Turner, The Ritual Process: Structure and Anti-Structure (Aldine Publishing, 1969), 94–130. I extend the concept here to the spatial condition of the ruin.

[^16]: Graphic notation developed intensively in experimental music in the mid-twentieth century. See Earle Brown, December 1952, in Folio and 4 Systems (Associated Music Publishers/G. Schirmer, 1952–54), https://earle-brown.org/work/folio-and-4-systems/; John Cage, Fontana Mix (Henmar Press, 1958), https://www.wisemusicclassical.com/work/69276/Fontana-Mix--John-Cage/. The two works respectively show how open graphic forms and transparent layers can organize multiple realizations.

[^17]: My use of the “picturesque” combines two strands of eighteenth-century British aesthetics: William Gilpin’s definition of a particular beauty “fit to be made into a picture,” and Uvedale Price’s emphasis on roughness, sudden variation, and irregularity. William Gilpin, Three Essays: On Picturesque Beauty; On Picturesque Travel; and On Sketching Landscape (R. Blamire, 1792), 3; Uvedale Price, An Essay on the Picturesque, as Compared with the Sublime and the Beautiful (J. Robson, 1794), 44–45.

[^18]: Robert Morris, “The Present Tense of Space,” Art in America 66, no. 1 (January–February 1978): 70–81. This section extends the problem of viewing in the “Broken Frame” from Morris’s discussion of spatial experience, time, and photography. Morris argues that photography tends to convert continuous spatial experience into static images, whereas spatially oriented works must be experienced by placing the viewer within them, through bodily movement, changing distance, and duration. I use this as a point of departure for understanding the ruin as an experience unfolding in the present.

[^19]: On the relation among travel, ruin-viewing, and artificial garden ruins in eighteenth-century British picturesque aesthetics, see Malcolm Andrews, The Search for the Picturesque: Landscape Aesthetics and Tourism in Britain, 1760–1800 (Stanford, CA: Stanford University Press, 1989), 41–50.

[^20]: My discussion of the “sublime” draws on Edmund Burke’s analysis of fear, danger, and aesthetic distance, together with Immanuel Kant’s account of the mathematical sublime, the dynamical sublime, and the faculties of the subject. Edmund Burke, A Philosophical Enquiry into the Origin of Our Ideas of the Sublime and Beautiful, 2nd ed., ed. Paul Guyer (Oxford University Press, 2015), pt. 2, secs. 2 and 7; Immanuel Kant, Critique of Judgment, trans. Werner S. Pluhar (Hackett Publishing, 1987), §§25–29.

[^21]: Price uses the example of a Greek temple to describe how time, through stains, accretions, moss, and loosened stones, transforms a smooth and complete building into a picturesque ruin. Uvedale Price, An Essay on the Picturesque, as Compared with the Sublime and the Beautiful (J. Robson, 1794), 44–45.

[^22]: Benjamin argues that, under allegorical vision, images become fragments and runic script and the false appearance of wholeness disappears; he also compares allegory in the realm of thought to ruins in the realm of things. I use this as a theoretical coordinate for the “Broken Prism”: once the appearance of the whole collapses, concealed relations can emerge from fragments. Walter Benjamin, The Origin of German Tragic Drama, trans. John Osborne (Verso, 1998), 176–78.

[^23]: I use “ruin complex” for the German Ruinenlust, referring to the pleasure, melancholy, and desire intertwined in looking at remains, while retaining a critique of this aestheticizing tendency. Such fascination must stop before the reality of other people. If a ruin still carries concrete loss, displacement, disaster, or unresolved responsibility, the viewer cannot simply empty it into a landscape made only for the sublime. Beauty does not erase provenance, and feeling cannot claim unconditional freedom from someone else’s loss. See Carlos López Galviz et al., “Reconfiguring Ruins: Beyond Ruinenlust,” GeoHumanities 3, no. 2 (2017): 531–53, https://doi.org/10.1080/2373566X.2017.1374874.

[^24]: The “Ruin Romantic” is a self-named attitude of viewing and making, not an established movement in art history. It describes a position that remains willing to preserve feeling and the impulse to build amid incompleteness, destruction, and new growth.

[^25]: James Stevens Curl and Susan Wilson, The Oxford Dictionary of Architecture, 3rd ed. (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2015), s.v. “folly.” From the architectural and everyday meanings of the word, I further develop the “bearing of the Fool.” The Fool/Folly pairing below is a creative translation within this essay and is not presented as an established relation in architectural history.

[^26]: I borrow only the image of the Fool walking toward the cliff as a role-model for creative potential not yet fully delimited by existing experience and value systems. This does not advance any claim about tarot divination or systems of religious belief. See Arthur Edward Waite, The Pictorial Key to the Tarot (London: William Rider & Son, 1911), pt. 2, “0. The Fool.”

[^27]: For the classic framework that understands everyday social interaction through performance, roles, and front/back regions, see Erving Goffman, The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life (Garden City, NY: Doubleday, 1959), chap. 3, “Regions and Region Behavior,” 106ff. I borrow only the neighboring theatrical metaphor and redirect it toward the failure of reciprocal roles within second nature and ruined space.

[^28]: The “madness” described here does not refer to a clinical or psychiatric condition, nor does it invoke an established philosophical or psychological concept. It is a working description of a creative state in which the subject, while retaining self-awareness, temporarily departs from established roles and everyday judgment, allowing action to enter positions that ordinary order would not normally contain.

[^29]: I borrow Hans-Thies Lehmann’s concept of the “postdramatic” to emphasize how, once the narrative center recedes, space, bodies, sound, and material events acquire relatively independent modes of presentation. I extend the concept here to the continuing aftereffects of modern ruins; this is not intended as a complete definition of postdramatic theory. Hans-Thies Lehmann, Postdramatic Theatre, trans. Karen Jürs-Munby (Routledge, 2006).

[^30]: “Symbolic residue” refers to hard remains born from civilizational order and systems of function that, in decay, shed their symbolic exterior and can no longer be fully absorbed by social rules. Žižek treats such residues, detached from the everyday ideological network, as “the product and leftover (déchet) of the process of symbolization itself”; their presence pierces back through the fantasy of a complete civilizational order. See Slavoj Žižek, The Sublime Object of Ideology (London: Verso, 1989), 169–175.

[^31]: From Pierre Schaeffer’s discussions of the classification of sound, listening habits, and cultural institutions, I derive the working proposition that “the essence of the instrument is noise.” The phrase is not a quotation from Schaeffer. See Pierre Schaeffer, “Sound and the Century: A Socio-Aesthetic Treatise,” trans. and intro. Donna Zapf, Vanguard 9, no. 1 (February 1980): 6–12.

[^32]: This passage draws on Ryuichi Sakamoto’s reflections on the “tsunami piano”: what people call “detuning” can also, at the scale of materials, be understood as wood and metal leaving human tuning and moving toward another material state. See Ryuichi Sakamoto: Coda, directed by Stephen Nomura Schible (Cineric and Borderland Media, 2017), film; see also Kevin Nguyen, “Themes and Variations: An Interview with Ryuichi Sakamoto,” GQ, July 18, 2018, https://www.gq.com/story/ryuichi-sakamoto-interview.

[^33]: See Mauricio Kagel, Konzertstück: für Pauken und Orchester (C. F. Peters, 1995); Matheus Augusto Ribeiro and Antenor Ferreira Corrêa, “Análise da ampliação de possibilidades tímbricas no Concerto para tímpanos e orquestra de Mauricio Kagel,” Per Musi 24 (2023): 6, 14, https://doi.org/10.35699/2317-6377.2023.42088.

[^34]: On rebellion becoming a new form of confinement when negation is absolutized, and on moving “beyond nihilism” without making destruction the final logic, see Albert Camus, The Rebel: An Essay on Man in Revolt, trans. Anthony Bower (New York: Vintage International, 1992), especially the sections “Metaphysical Rebellion” and “Beyond Nihilism.” I use “the circuit of nihilism” as a working description for the cycle in which destruction is reabsorbed and then answered by the search for an even more violent rupture; it is not intended as a complete statement of Camus’s theory.
