# Preface — After Architecture

Architecture shapes life almost without a sound. Long before we truly understand space, we have already internalized doors, windows, walls, and other basic architectural elements as common sense, and learned to inhabit them with ease. Since industrialization, people, architecture, and order have increasingly joined into a continuously operating system of life, together weaving what I call “second nature.” We are born into it, labor, build, and seek shelter within it; over time, we begin to take this highly designed system for the world’s original face.[^2]

As second nature continues to expand, rationalism gradually draws what was once unknown, dangerous, and uncontrollable in nature into the reach of transformation. Because “control” and “predictability” once proved their effectiveness in defending the threshold of survival, people came almost as a matter of course to elevate this reason into a measure for everything. Under its pervasive rule, the surplus space in which life once had room to breathe is quietly flattened; the contingencies and trial-and-error of natural growth are excluded in advance by calculations of efficiency. Even care, at this point, can imperceptibly harden into discipline.

Once we entrust everything bound up with life to second nature, the stability of the system acquires an almost inviolable status. The separation between human beings and nature is no longer merely physical distance, but a boundary of power drawn by order in order to reinforce itself. Rationalization keeps patching the world before us, presenting a continuity maintained by force as though it were indestructible. In the end, we become caught in a net of our own weaving: we lose not only the courage to depart from the rules, but increasingly the ability to imagine that any departure from the given track is possible at all.

And yet, the ruin appears.

It emerges along cracks long accumulated in the darker folds of civilization, taking form wherever order can no longer sustain itself. As natural erosion steadily strips away the shell of order, the costs left unreckoned beneath its orderly surface finally become visible to the eye. The measures that once organized life, weighed value, and allocated resources now fall coldly back upon their own creations, deciding which are still worth caring for and which are to be exiled into time and left to spend themselves away. The cost of order is no longer hidden in unknown consequences; it is exposed on the remnants of order itself.

Ruins briefly lend us the gaze of the “Angel of History,” compelling us to look back at the narrative that calls itself civilization and progress. With our backs to the future and our faces turned toward what has already happened, we see behind the spectacular achievements the things cast aside and the ideals cut short. The storm called “progress” still drives us blindly forward, while wreckage continues to gather beneath our feet and behind us. Once we grow accustomed to filing these costs under the losses supposedly necessary to development, the road of modernity becomes, inevitably, a road paved with ruins.[^3]

## Modern Ruins

The ruins accumulating along modernity’s road gradually form a new terrain. In the second half of the twentieth century, New Topographics photography turned its lens away from idealized nature toward landscapes altered by human beings, recording with a cool, restrained gaze the ways modern life marks the ground. The chill of these images grows precisely from second nature: human order has profoundly transformed the environment, yet through sheer familiarity has receded into a landscape waiting to be deconstructed. When this austere way of looking extends to modern ruins, the costs long pressed into the background by the dominant melody of “progress” begin to surface. The more a building once stood for stability and permanence, the more piercing its silence becomes after it falls into ruin.[^4]

Modern technology produces this new terrain at an unprecedented speed. Surging demand for space drives construction ever faster, allowing buildings to proliferate with expansion, while caring for them one by one remains difficult. Records of buildings accumulate in ever greater quantities, yet what truly settles into collective memory does not increase with them. The landscape of civilization is therefore studded with remnant structures whose forms remain while the living contexts that once sustained them have already been stripped away. Faced with these remnants latent all around us, we too have grown accustomed to keeping our distance from the death of our own creations. Forgetting here degenerates into a disguise worn by reason: reason is no longer used for reflection, but becomes a means of ending reflection, constructing a structural ignorance that screens off consequences. Immersed in the logic of efficiency and progress, people can even cloak violent destruction in the language of a “necessary evil” and accept it as an inviolable law of development.

Modern ruins, however, do not pass into silent dissolution on the day they cease to function. They carry an abrasive untimeliness of their own: these creations often die too early, marked for rapid erasure before time has even given them the patina of age. It is precisely this temporal break, this premature death, that makes their destruction impossible to dress up as a melancholy or spectacular sight. Their cool material surfaces bear, without ornament, the long aftershock of abandonment. As an “unfinished reality,” the modern ruin remains disturbingly close to us: the people and memories attached to it may not have fully disappeared, and consequences not yet reckoned with continue to ferment beneath the surface. Before this disintegration has settled into history, any conclusion drawn too quickly arrives too soon. We have no other way forward than to seek understanding through physical proximity—first recognizing the structures, traces, and anomalous sounds that remain on site, and only then allowing judgment to enter memory.[^5]

Countless remnants lie scattered across a human depositional layer, residues of a human-made order that continues to accumulate. They are neither wholly forgotten nor easily admitted into memory.[^6]For me, a ruin is the shape that absent time grows upon the ground: a history in which I did not take part, a homeland that no longer coincides with memory, yet still remains in the world through its surviving form. Its fragments and broken narratives cannot restore the past, but they can keep me from being absent as it continues toward disappearance.

## Ruinwork and the Ruinwright

Faced with these material bodies that continue to decay in the course of their departure, the Ruinwright takes over the process of their disintegration. I call this creative practice that begins after architecture “ruinwork.” It works from the structures and residual forces still present on site, treating the very process of “becoming ruin” as a mode of construction. It does not seek permanence; it writes into the design the work’s fate of gradually converging with its environment and coming apart. The Folly Series is ruinwork given concrete form: the day a work is completed merely fixes it, for an instant, in a posture already collapsing; everything that follows is yielded to time.

The Ruinwright is an identity of intervention that has settled out of my long study of ruins, as well as a methodology for practicing ruinwork. It seeks no worldly professional qualification; it is closer to a private law an independent researcher sets for their own practice. When architectural collapse breaks apart the discipline embedded in real space, imaginings that have nowhere to stand within ordinary order finally gain room to be practiced. In this wilderness stripped of established rules, my research and making have at last found a precise place to land. Only here do I dare to grant this untamed, self-growing craft the title of wright.

# Ten Tenets of the Ruinwright

The “Ten Tenets of the Ruinwright” are creative principles continually distilled through the making and revision of ruinwork, as well as a working index embedded within the text. Drawn from concrete practice, they can be consulted independently, called upon repeatedly, and tested again at the next site. Their movement begins with looking at wreckage and stepping into it, then proceeds through material fracture, the dissipation of force, and the residual sounds of structure, before arriving at the inevitable end of every made thing: how the Ruinwright exercises judgment within that fate of disintegration. Some tenets are distilled from specific techniques; others are only provisional conclusions that hold within the practice as it stands today. To set them down as tenets is to leave future journeys a set of coordinates that can be taken up at any time—and rewritten at any time.

## First Tenet: Ruin Lexicology

When the expansion of second nature exceeds the limits of individual perception and understanding, many human-made things enter our lives and preconfigure spatial common sense before they ever grant us “access.” Day after day we face the sealed shells of these systems, cut off from how they actually work, yet accepting their existence through an illusion of self-evidence. Only when that veil of common sense is shattered, and the made thing falls back into an exposed state of ruin, are we forced to step through the torn opening and undertake a belated act of recognition.

Precisely because no two ruins disintegrate in the same way, “ruin lexicology” becomes the starting point of this rediscovery. Entering an unfamiliar remnant, the Ruinwright sets aside old names that have already ceased to function and confronts the building’s exposed physical reality as it is now—asking whether it is still bearing load, beginning to lean, or already pierced by plant roots. When the generic formula of “original name + ruin” can no longer contain these living differences in transformation, a new form of naming from the ground up becomes the beginning of rebuilt knowledge. It borrows the rigor of natural-history classification, yet what it tries to anchor are objects always in collapse and evolution, never to recur in exactly the same form.[^7]

To anchor the reality of collapse, one must face the void that appears when common sense fails wholesale. Once a building breaks its promise of shelter and use and recedes into rough, nameless matter, a person can no longer draw instinctive reassurance from space. To stand there is to be thrown into wilderness at night, stripped of orientation and support. Yet it is precisely this dangerous weightlessness, together with prolonged confrontation on site, that forces the Ruinwright to temper a pair of “wasteland eyes”: no longer relying on false common sense, but reading directly from surviving structure and environmental change where load is still carried and where imbalance is approaching. The direction of a crack, the depth of corrosion, and the paths taken by water, wind, and plants physically mark what this body has been through, what is happening to it now, and where a fatal collapse may emerge.[^8]

Through long wandering and repeated acts of recognition, the fine differences produced by collapse become increasingly legible. New words grow ferally from the rubble like weeds, eventually gathering into a “ruin lexicon”—an index of remnant morphemes woven from measurement, walking, and memory. Within it, four kinds of morphemes—land, architecture, physical condition, and natural evolution—overlap, allowing each name to wrap tightly around the site’s changing image. Through this vocabulary generated from inside the ruin, we anchor chaos and danger that lie outside ordinary common sense back onto our own map of knowledge. Isolated places of abandonment begin to relate to one another, together sketching the real geography of a world undergoing ruination.

## Second Tenet: An Archaeology of Things Close at Hand

After a building loses function and maintenance, it does not immediately become nameless matter. In modern ruins, this condition is especially stark: destruction arrives among things almost within reach, and the objects left behind are not yet old, yet have already become remains. They still carry the patina and wear of ordinary life—a familiarity we can recognize with ease, though it may belong to no memory we personally lived. They are left awkwardly suspended between “everyday refuse” and “cultural heritage,” slipping from civilization’s triumphant narrative track and leaving only a faint trace at the fracture line of an era.

The Ruinwright looks at these near-at-hand remains with the cool gaze of “archaeology,” asking why things once bound up with daily life were abandoned at a particular moment. We read in the wreckage a cross-section of an era cut short by force, recognize past negotiations in scratches and peeling finishes, and follow the accumulated dust backward toward the event that made life stop abruptly. The respect produced by such looking does not attempt to reconstruct a shattered past into a warm, complete picture. It is simply a lucid act of witness: to face incompleteness, acknowledge that the lives now withdrawn once truly existed, and grant them one solemn look before they disappear entirely from memory.[^9]

This restrained act of “recognition” enters ruinwork as practice. Even if ruinwork takes over the collapse of a ruin, it must leave gaps in the narrative exactly where they are, refusing to assemble scattered traces into a complete private story. Once a fracture is filled in by the imagination of those who came later, the remnant’s own heavy silence is easily covered by cheap lyricism. Ruinwork therefore does not try to restore an object’s former function or reconstruct the illusion of earlier life. The work returns instead to the narrative and physical cross-section itself, allowing the form and wounds of the wreckage to reappear and giving weight again to time that has been sealed away. Deep within the ruin, it suspends only a “spatiotemporal anchor” that makes the viewer pause, so that through this shell one may notice again the people who once built, used, treasured, and finally left these things behind.

## Third Tenet: Borrowing the Ruin

When a building is intact and strong, human beings are accustomed to taming nature beyond the boundary and drawing it into their own order through “borrowed scenery,” displaying the ease with which human intention claims mastery over nature. Once the structures that sustain that order break, however, subject and object rapidly change places. The view once framed outside the window seeps indoors through cracks; the window that once separated inside from outside becomes a passage through which nature moves back in and retakes the ground.

To “borrow” means that intervention must depend on the structures, discarded materials, and ecological networks already present on site. The idea of “borrowing the ruin” begins here. Traditional gardens use the wildness of nature to set off the dignity of order; borrowing the ruin reverses the relation, using remnants already eroded by nature to expose the vulnerability of the made thing and the arrogance it once carried. The Ruinwright reads the remnant against the path by which nature has retaken space, reading through its broken skeleton the layers of history and aspiration built into it. Once the view is released from a human center, what hangs suspended is not only a narrative of decline but the material structure of the wreckage itself: the people who once raised everything upward are gone, yet the mechanical resistance invested in the building has not been fully spent. The ruin is caught in mid-fall, held in a suspension that cannot land.

The perspective of “borrowing the ruin” ultimately becomes, at the physical level, an extremely humble practice of ruinwork. We borrow not only nature’s erosive point of view, but also its methods. The Ruinwright refuses to extract new bone and blood from the earth without need; like water and plant roots, the Ruinwright reaches into the wreckage along the paths already opened by disintegration. From abandoned brick and cracked concrete, we identify residual forces not yet spent and borrow them as new support. The action begins by “borrowing matter” and settles, in the end, into “borrowing force”: following the drainage inclines formed by the terrain, small ecological zones evolving in wall joints, and the parallel spread of roots and cracks. The site’s material conditions continually constrain and rewrite the Ruinwright’s movements, making intervention a genuine form of “interstitial gardening”—seeping into gaps left by planning and system failure, using existing structure as a frame and residual material as components, never attempting to cover a collapsing remnant with another grand order.[^10]

Since it is a “borrowing,” it is destined to be “returned.” Anything that stands by relying on residual force at the site already contains the knowledge that its existence is temporary. When a phase of borrowing the ruin comes to an end, the Ruinwright must withdraw with dignity. Exploration and intervention here are governed by a severe ethical boundary: an unattended ruin is not a lawless zone exempt from moral responsibility, and the name of art cannot turn unauthorized dismantling or damage into “coexistence.” Even when intervention is permitted, any additional load imposed on the site must be kept to a minimum, and departure should leave behind only traces capable of coexisting with nature. Finally, as human intention withdraws, the spatial relation between subject and object yields one last time: the site is returned in full to nature, receding again toward the wilderness that once existed without us.

## Fourth Tenet: Brutal Materiality

Modern ruins often appear as exposed concrete frames. Exterior finishes that once concealed interiors and softened boundaries are the first to mold, peel, and fall away under weathering, until only the bones used for bearing load and dividing space remain. This irreversible stripping is like tearing away the false skin of “second nature,” exposing the most direct posture of structural force. Ruination inadvertently produces the aesthetic appearance of “Brutalism”; in fact, it simply returns architecture to a physical fact governed by gravity, corrosion, and lifespan.[^11]

Within these exposed, brutal structures there often condenses a powerful modernist impulse: once people mastered precise structural calculation and synthetic materials, they began to believe they possessed the power to command space. That precision displays the confidence of technological reason, but also reveals its desire to bring the environment completely under human order. Yet when the same frame becomes a ruin, this “brutality” develops an opposite tension—matter begins to struggle free of the will imposed upon it.

The “brutality” of the modern ruin is born from this fracture of contradiction. The frame left by precise calculation still stands, yet the materials that compose it have once again become subject to gravity, corrosion, and microscopic fracture. A highly developed technological inheritance and the most primitive, blind physical forces collide within the same damaged body, giving the remnant an untamable wildness. As the frame that once embodied order begins to fail under erosion, the “brutality” revealed by the fallen skin deepens into what I call “brutal materiality”—the naked body exposed after control withdraws. When ruined walls lean and force falls out of balance, rational calculation must face the weight of matter itself, the error inherent in materials, and a finite lifespan. Reason has not failed in the ruin; it can simply no longer stand above material reality.

Ruinwork therefore strips away added symbols and formal embellishment, allowing the work to carry, nakedly, the physical traces of how it was formed, how it bears force, and how it will eventually decay. The “brutality” here is not an aesthetic accessory attached to scenes of dilapidation. It arises from uncertainties deep within matter that geometry can never fully contain. Where are the true load-bearing limits of the modern materials in which humanity once took pride? Where will they resist, and where will they yield? The answer does not exist in drawings or calculations. It can only emerge from the materials’ actual properties through the prolonged struggle and confrontation of collapse.

## Fifth Tenet: The Force-Flow View

The displacement and disintegration of ruins under force are not accidental failures of a system. Traces of structural decay do more than signal the impossibility of restoration; the cracks that spread through them act like pointers tracing back toward the origin of destruction. Under continuous natural erosion, weak nodes and material fatigue make the precise moment of fracture unpredictable, yet the points at which forces accumulate and stress will ultimately concentrate are often unmistakable. Destruction, then, carries from the moment of construction a causal chain that can be traced backward through the structural remains.

Collapse inside a ruin is rarely a local, isolated event; it moves through chain reactions and implicates the whole. Fragments that fall violently often form new overlaps, braces, and suspended contacts by chance, assembling a damaged but still operative system of force transmission. From this, the Ruinwright establishes the “force-flow view”: broken walls, hanging beams, and leaning columns are no longer seen as static dead matter, but as a dynamic field of relations through which residual energy continues to pass and dissipate. Even in debris that appears still and balanced, countless connections and cracks alive with hidden currents have already inscribed the long process of destruction into the structure itself.

When ruinwork connects its mechanical transmission devices to these active or taut relations, otherwise invisible internal stresses are translated into observable displacement, vibration, and fracture. The apparatus takes on the task of tracing causality: it gives form to latent force flows and makes the resistance buried in matter legible along their paths. Yet this connection is never a manipulation from above. Once ruinwork takes up the ruin’s residual forces, it is irreversibly drawn into the same fate of disappearance. The Ruinwright may appear to arrange fragments, but in fact is caught within the same causal web of events: a seemingly trivial change can reshape a local field of tension, and even the smallest error can rewrite the outcome of the entire structure.[^12]

## Sixth Tenet: The Destructive Test Body

Beneath the illusion of stability—even permanence—woven by “second nature,” destruction is often abstracted into a kind of inevitable metabolism. We have not learned to resonate with the disappearance of our own creations; instead, we become numb to the process by which they decay. This numbness comes from a mismatch of perception: microscopic fatigue and damage in materials unfold too slowly for the human eye to register, while catastrophic collapse at the other scale is so violent that the physical energy it releases can crush the human body with ease. Between “imperceptible micro-change” and “unbearable rupture,” we lose any real bodily sense of a building’s lifespan. The “destructive test body” is born from this gap. It forcibly compresses a long, hidden process of decay into a medium-body capable of real-time feedback, returning destruction to a scale the eye and nervous system can directly perceive.

The “destructive test body” extracts key relations of material, joints, mass, and gravity from a ruin and reconstructs them as a body made for destruction. Displacement markers, load sensors, strain gauges, and vibration pickups serve as “sensory prostheses” that take risks in place of the human body. They translate hidden structural injuries invisible to the eye, and lethal tensions the body cannot safely approach, into visible and audible wounds, turning structural disintegration into a physical “lingchi” that can be watched.[^13]

Just as human beings have used the figure of the android to ask where the boundary of “what makes us human” lies, the destructive test body probes the ultimate limit of second nature. It carries the structural logic on which second nature depends, yet precise calculation drives that very logic toward the threshold of collapse, allowing the test body to approach—and step across—the boundary of death that human-made things are not meant to touch. When the reason that creates order exposes, with its own hands, that order is not permanent, the test body becomes a brutal mirror: in its wreckage we see the finitude that neither human-made things nor the human subject can overcome.

Ruinwork turns this testing of finitude into a central creative method. In the Folly Series, the movement toward disintegration is placed at the endpoint from the very beginning of design. Once the planned loosening finally breaks open, environmental intervention and uncontrollable error rapidly take over, dragging the collapsing remains beyond what can be calculated or foreseen. Unscripted contingency flowers through the tearing, and a brutal beauty arrives with it. This beauty does not stand opposed to reason; it erupts precisely in the ruin’s “final resonance,” where reason has spent itself and can no longer answer on behalf of life.

## Seventh Tenet: Return to the Suspended Realm

Modern ruins often resemble shells whose souls have died before their bodies. Complex spatial frameworks once pushed to the limits of human use lose guidance, light, and occupation, and become dark, unfamiliar labyrinths. Doors no longer indicate entrances; corridors no longer guarantee arrival. Broken partitions and stairs that end in midair betray the promise of shelter and strip the building of the safety it was meant to provide. These remains cannot reassemble the whole that has disappeared; instead, isolated limbs make absence newly visible. The wanderer moves through them facing structures that have lost meaning yet still carry load. With people gone, these “residual structures” turn from background into agents and establish a posthuman spatial order.

The ruin, evolving on its own, gives off an attraction charged with pressure. It seizes the gaze and draws the viewer into an abyssal counter-gaze, unsettling the assumed boundary between person and space. The building no longer receives its visitor hospitably; whoever enters must move carefully under the threats of leaning and falling. The “central position” human beings once imagined themselves to occupy proves, at the scale of the ruin, to be only a temporary vacancy left by time. Ornament, cracks, and plants begin to reorganize space according to their own rules, while human activity is in turn absorbed without mercy by the site. Suspended between the human-made and the natural, between former shelter and present abandonment, one is left without ground. From this weightlessness, the ruin’s “liminality” emerges.[^14][^15]

Ruinwork brings detached fragments back into contact and returns the gaze to a space that has already lost its human center. Scattered remains are joined again, but although the fragments carry memories of the past, they can no longer fit into a living context. They are assembled into an unfamiliar body that holds a grotesque and fragile balance under the continuous pull of gravity and corrosion. At first it appears as a hard foreign object wedged into the site; under the environment’s continued consumption, however, it becomes tightly entangled with the texture of the ruin, eventually mutating into a “spatial monster” that can no longer be peeled away from the site.

Yet the most conspicuous intruder on site is not this constructed body. Once the human position as spatial subject has been stripped away, the person who still insists on searching for coordinates and anchoring a sense of familiarity becomes the truly strange and untimely presence. Person and space are left together in a state that cannot land. It is at this moment, when both subject and object have lost their footing, that the “suspended realm” appears.

Faced with this suspension of the subject, the Ruinwright refuses to rebuild any false sense of command. Once placed on site, the work is already prepared to be exiled, estranged, and carried away from its original intention. Its fragments anchor memory toward the time before destruction, while its grotesque body pushes the gaze toward a sealed future—yet neither end can ever be reached. I build the Folly Series precisely because the shape of the other side cannot be foreseen. The work does not prepare an answer for the unknown; it merely lets familiar things, once stripped of their old relations, condense briefly into a nameless body before surrendering it without reserve to time.

## Eighth Tenet: Dissipation into Sound

When a dull cracking sound bursts from deep within a load-bearing structure presumed to be permanent, it instantly breaches a psychological line of defense—a condition that might be called “civilizational dissonance.” As a precursor to collapse, the anomalous sound is the first path by which that dissonance reaches the senses: the building has not yet physically fallen, but the shelter and safety it held in the mind have already collapsed.

This bodily unease comes from irreversible material imbalance within the ruin. Residual tension and gravity are released through friction and fracture and converted into vibrations in the air; the echo of dripping water and the howl of cross-drafts interweave into sonic traces of erosion. These sounds mark the structure’s continuing death after it has left human order behind, and at times seem to send an uncanny summons beyond the ruin itself. The space keeps people out through the threat of collapse, yet seizes the mind through hearing and pulls passersby back into a force field not yet spent.

Ruinwork inscribes this paradoxical listening experience back into the structure of the work. The Folly Series is set within an irreversible process of collapse, attempting to guide residual aftershocks in the remnant into another sounding construction already charged with tension. Precisely because these sounds violate the expectation that architecture should remain “silent,” passersby stop and listen, trying to locate where the fracture came from and when the next break will arrive. Listening to the ruin ceases to be passive reception and becomes an immersive tracing of destruction, driven jointly by fear and curiosity.

## Ninth Tenet: The Blueprint Becomes a Score

From the first stroke of design, ruinwork binds the movement of fragments to the sound of structural fracture, so that the work already carries the rhythm of collapse while still on the drawing board. When the Folly Series finally produces an actual sound field through distorted form and torn material, the engineering blueprint that once organized its mechanical construction is transformed into a latent acoustic score.

A conventional score disciplines sound through standardized notes and durations. A ruinwork blueprint gives its nodes and lines a more open reading: anchors, load-bearing axes, and elevations no longer promise stability, but become latent markers of stress, displacement, and spatial deformation about to produce an “anomalous sound.” When cold geometric lines become threshold lines forecasting fracture, the blueprint crosses the boundary of engineering and changes into a graphic score of destruction’s rhythm.[^16]

Yet as the work’s disintegration gradually departs from its predicted course, and the original mechanical principles can no longer be mapped neatly onto the wreckage before us, the blueprint falls from “instructor” to “recorded object.” While tracking collapse, the Ruinwright redraws into the plan the actual trajectories of falling components and the locations of sound sources. At the same time, dust, water stains, and corrosion spreading across the work are surveyed and marked onto the drawing. These recorded traces of physical decay force their way into the abstract blueprint, twisting and deforming once-rigorous engineering symbols through repeated staining and redrawing.

Natural erosion continues to rewrite the drawing during the “performance” of the Folly Series, turning it into a palimpsest layered with traces. Once fixed instructions are covered by new trajectories, the blueprint that once presumed to choreograph the ruin ultimately undergoes ruination itself.

## Tenth Tenet: The Meaning of Futility

At the technical level, ruinwork operates much like conventional architecture, yet in motive it retreats into a realm of pure solitude: it is often carried forward quietly by me alone, deep inside a ruin. All social instructions have been stripped away—no client demand, no supervisor’s intervention, no calculation of capital return. Every concern contracts toward the physical reality of the ruin itself. Here, building is no longer a way to sell “shelter,” but a way to give concrete form to a fleeting process of destruction and lead it toward a carefully choreographed overturning. The final collapse of the Folly Series may appear to be triggered by years of accumulated external forces, but in truth the seed of destruction was buried on the first day of construction. Everything cultivated through ruinwork does little more than follow the irresistible laws of nature and escort the work toward the irreversible edge of disintegration.

When secular society measures the value of labor through “continuance” and “output,” pouring effort into a work destined for destruction seems to offer no return at all, almost an absurd exercise in futility. This utilitarian measure arrogantly stakes “beauty” on solidity and permanence, while readily condemning breakage, weathering, and disappearance as “failure.” The meaning of ruinwork lies precisely in staying within the fissure where that value system breaks: listening, in the ruin, to the material reality exposed after function has been exhausted, and testing the last load-bearing force a structure releases as it nears failure. The Ruinwright deliberately yields part of their labor to the dissipation of meaning and value, enacting an almost obstinate belief: anything that has received concentrated attention deserves unquestionable respect, even if it can never reach eternity.

The “meaning of futility” preserves dignity for labor that cannot be settled by return, while also drawing an ethical line that cannot be crossed. To accept destruction is not to worship it blindly; acts of arbitrary damage cannot be pardoned merely by invoking “art.” Even if destruction is the work’s unavoidable destination, that does not erase the weight of bodily experience involved in making it, nor the aftershock left in the mind by witnessing collapse. Such labor does not need eternity to prove its value. If it truly took place in this unsupported ground, it is worth carrying through with everything we have.

## The Boundaries of the Ruinwright

Modern ruins cannot simply be absorbed into aesthetic or utilitarian systems of value, because they are not shaped by nature and time alone. The wounds of war, engineering accidents, and failures of decision all leave material consequences that cannot be worn away; traces of labor and human tragedy remain entangled with the wreckage in the same space. Weathering will alter the form of the remnant, but it has no right to erase responsibility for the historical events that produced it. Ruinwork must therefore hold to an ethical boundary: it refuses to turn real catastrophe into an abstract beauty of destruction, and it cannot allow aesthetic experience to conceal responsibility and trauma that still demand to be confronted.

Here, art sharpens the sensitivity with which a site is perceived, while architecture provides rational means for reworking matter and space. Yet when the Ruinwright enters the ruin carrying both, “restraint” becomes a discipline that must be kept. To possess the ability to rearrange matter and extract meaning is to require an even firmer suppression of the urge to “reshape” the ruin. One should not rashly interrupt the slow takeover already underway through weathering and erosion, nor impose our limited understanding of beauty upon a process of disintegration that has not yet taken form. We must also remain wary of the language we build ourselves: once concepts begin to summarize the ruin in its place, ruinwork risks becoming another layer of “second nature.” Theory can offer a path into the site, but it gives us no right to look down upon it.

Recognizing this limit, the Ruinwright respects both nature’s long, ungovernable force and the enormous costs sedimented by human order, and enters alone into the ruin’s still-unfinished collapse to face the site’s loss of control and danger: allowing sharp fragments to cut the palm, passing through thorns, wading into mud. Only by casting the body into this danger for long periods of surveying and listening can one truly take up the ruin’s residual sound.

The Ten Tenets do not exhaust the understanding of ruins. They are only provisional rules established through a handful of practices in the present, and they will continue to revise themselves through future exploration. In the ongoing study of ruins, what ruinwork reveals no longer remains confined to historical traces and causes of destruction. It also includes the question of why I am drawn to them again and again, why I keep returning to these remains, and why I am willing to stay so long in this boundless desolation.

# The Broken Frame — From the Picturesque to the Sublime

At first, ruins often enter aesthetic vision through the gentle appearance of the “picturesque.” Under the shared erosion of time and nature, an intact building loses its polished surface and moves toward damage, yet among rubble, moss, and mottled weathering it can produce a fleetingly romantic posture. Each time we stop to admire a ruin, the “picturesque beauty” we capture is, in essence, only a composition accidentally frozen within the process of destruction.

## Picturesque Beauty: Bringing the Ruined Scene into the Frame

Picturesque beauty not only extracts from the ceaseless change of broad nature a view “fit for a picture”; it also answers a modern fascination with ruined landscapes torn by time, full of incompleteness and abrupt change. In pursuing the picturesque, the viewer longs to peer into loss of control and danger, yet must also wrap them in layers of romantic filtering and retreat into a safe aesthetic experience. Through this psychological distance, an invisible “frame” is quietly erected: damage is brought inside the frame, danger is temporarily tamed by composition, and destruction hardens into a landscape outside oneself, as though that instant of collapse took place only within the picture.[^17]

The artificial ruins—the follies—of traditional European gardens once sought to domesticate damage, collapse, and natural encroachment into a sequence that could be designed and controlled for viewing. They compressed vast, prolonged decay into the miniature landscape of the garden, attempting to hold picturesque beauty at the most pleasing instant of contemplation.

Real ruins, however, do not obey this false frame. Even when natural damage is deliberately guided into surviving structure, we cannot stop or reverse the erosive force that continues to strip away flesh and bone. A single storm, or the loosening of one load-bearing node, can wrench the ruin away from the perfect state most suited to viewing.[^18]

Expanding cracks, leaning members, and mottled corrosion record the continuous reorganization of forces. The surface may appear still, while structural change underneath never stops. Once we understand that the picturesque form cannot be fixed, and that human attempts to preserve it cannot halt the ruin’s self-erasure, “collapse” arrives with actual physical weight and smashes the stillness of the picture.

The channel of looking and the safe distance sustained by picturesque beauty collapse with it. The sublime finally appears from beyond the broken frame: the form before us may be swept away by the next wave of destruction, and the viewer’s formerly secure sense of safety begins to shake under this pure physical violence.[^19]

The “Broken Frame” (fig. 1) gives concrete form to this failure of aesthetic distance. From the moment the frame itself begins to become ruin, it loses the capacity to hold fleeting change still as landscape. With everything once contained inside the frame now absent, the damaged skeleton that had only marked a boundary turns into the object of the gaze. Its exposed breaks and misaligned layers force the eye inward, toward the lethal force that tore everything apart.

## The Folly Series: Releasing the Frame

Traditional classical gardens often used “borrowed scenery” to draw nature into an order of viewing: nature, tamed, was carefully selected and cut into a framed scene. But when humanity’s self-proclaimed “domestication” finally fails under time, and architectural order collapses under natural erosion, the relation between subject and object reverses irreversibly. The wildness once held outside the frame seeps through cracks into the building and, through continued stripping and counter-erosion, takes control of space back from within.

The Folly Series begins from this reversal. Intervention starts from the condition of ruin; nature is drawn back inside along existing cracks, where it works against the shell of human-made order and brings a slow deconstruction to the building. The Folly Series also borrows the name of the garden folly, the “artificial ruin,” yet strips away almost all of its picturesque warmth and amplifies instead the destructive force that cannot be controlled. The “corpse of an object” once arranged for human viewing no longer remains a safe scene; as it continues to disintegrate beyond control, it forces the viewer to confront their own smallness.

To reach this point, the Ruinwright plants the “seed of disintegration” directly in the work’s underlying logic, making destruction itself part of the work. Structures set off balance, materials scored in advance with fracture lines, and elements held in suspension embed multiple paths of loosening and collapse, so that even while the installation remains standing, it is saturated with the physical tension of waiting to fall.[^20]

This preconstructed disintegration becomes visible over time. Faced with the unavoidable weakening of human-made things, we encounter again the anxiety of survival within that loss of control, and encounter a sublime beyond the safe boundary of the picturesque—a sublime generated by material disorder itself. The psychic tremor of this “civilizational dissonance” carries us into an unstable liminal zone: the building still retains the shell of space, but it no longer promises shelter or safety.

## Ruins of the Mind: A Fractured Frame

The Folly Series pushes the viewer toward the leading edge of a loosening frame. The picturesque observer who first lingered before ruins was accustomed to sealing destruction inside the damaged image, preserving the safety of life outside it. That boundary could feel secure because second nature not only assigned order and destination to things; it also marked out for the human being an apparently immovable position as subject within the world.

Only when the cracks in the wall visibly spread beneath one’s feet does collapse reveal itself as a physical violence still moving forward. Once the viewer understands that the same erosion could occur in any familiar place, the sublime in nature breaks through the aesthetic boundary and begins to consume, in reverse, the order by which the viewer understands the world. In this comprehensive loss of ground, the human space on which life depends collapses inside consciousness, while objects once carefully cherished lose their weight in an emptiness that answers no one. Familiar architecture becomes a “lost space.”

As the grand narratives attached to space withdraw, the psychological shelter provided by crowds and everyday life evaporates with them. This becomes a brutal form of “homesickness while still at home” (solastalgia): home remains where it was, yet can no longer coincide with memory. It is familiar enough to be recognized as home, yet too strange to carry the years one did not live through. Here, a person is forced to face histories in which they did not participate and countless possible lives that came to nothing. The absolute solitude of this encounter arises from a real and silent absence: familiar life has left the stage, while the cold space remains.

The inner sublime erupts precisely from this deprivation in which distance can no longer be maintained. Once observer-like mourning is gone, “lost space” forces viewer and wreckage into the same irreversible physical time, confronting us with the fact that we too cannot escape disappearance. Things before us that still possess weight and warmth will eventually decay into unregarded residue on a historical cross-section; when the same fate reaches the self, the pressure of helplessness spreads from the past into the present. Under the void’s pressure on both body and mind, one instinctively clenches the hands, trying to pull from emptiness one last tangible sense of continuing to exist.

## A Mirror of the Modern Mind: The Phantom of the Empty Frame

Within this unsupported void, consciousness becomes unusually clear. Once we understand that matter cannot return to the past and time cannot reverse, the one who perceives their own smallness and continues to respond after meaning has dispersed is still the same “I.” Will appears here as the act of affirming oneself within finitude and deciding how to continue. One recognizes oneself again: small, yet real; finite, yet still continuing.

The damaged beauty of the “Broken Frame” thus becomes a mirror for the mind. When lost space withdraws every familiar response, and we enter this solitude and near-absolute self-scrutiny, a question begins to press back upon us: beyond the faces we have passively formed in order to accommodate the world, what still belongs to us?

The damage we see is not merely a set of cracks inscribed on objects in the external world. Within the worldview that has long shaped us, we habitually wrap both self and things in a garment woven from order and identity, treating the world as a mirror in which each part recognizes and locks into the others. The fissure of the ruin breaks through that mirror as an image: what it tears open is not only the object, but the gap where the “world of identity” fails to mesh with reality itself. Solitude does not return a purer self to us. It simply withdraws, with some violence, the voices of order that normally answer on our behalf, allowing desire, fear, and impulses not yet discovered to rise again along the cracks of disintegration.

# Ruin Imagery — Where Desire Goes

Born into modern society, we have inherited ways of judging things through order, efficiency, and use, and internalized them into an almost instinctive gaze. The modern ruin is the break left by that logic. Once value disappears, the utilitarian gaze reaches for purchase and finds none in a remnant from which no profit can be drawn. Yet when “beauty” strikes from the fracture in that instant, we do not simply leave; we continue along the crack, looking for the source of the desire that made us look.

## The Broken Prism: Reading the Fracture Plane

If the mirror of the “Broken Frame” notices a mismatch when the gaze turns back upon itself, the “Broken Prism” looks through that mismatch and begins to reveal how “second nature” refracts a disorderly reality into legible spectra. Ordinarily, the glass that performs the refraction is scarcely noticed. Only once the prism breaks do its actual form, stress, and fragility appear at the fracture. A ruin works in the same way. When the order and maintenance that sustain the surface are cut off, matter reappears as weight, corrosion, and lifespan; the loosening of a single node can expose an entire structural system. By the same logic, a failed building can pull into view the civilizational structure that once sustained it.

The broken prism grants no omniscience. It only lets relations suppressed beneath an intact surface appear at the break. Its beauty therefore remains in a dislocation that is difficult to settle: the whole image collapses, yet the fragments acquire new reflections. Drawn to this reflected light, the viewer encounters not a romantic explanation laid over damage, but a poetry that erupts from the materiality of the break itself.[^21]

Fear of collapse and a cool kind of consolation occur together in this tremor of beauty. As the former city continues to fail, it exposes its material body and reflects the fragility of the order people built. The tension not yet exhausted after destruction cannot be fully accounted for by utilitarian measures, nor can the brilliance that sometimes appears within destruction. The ruin thus reveals the finitude of human-made things in both life and intention: we want meaning and beauty to endure, yet some ideals generate new tragedies in the act of being realized, while others lose their echo when the people or structures that carried them disappear.

## The Ruin Romantic: Tenderness of Fragments

Looking inward along the crack, a deeper tragedy of modern life comes gradually into view. The operating order that provides shelter and predictability has also, almost invisibly, compressed the room in which contingency and perception can breathe. When utilitarian standards of what can be measured, verified, and used dominate judgment for too long, experiences that resist quantification are pushed to the edge. The ruin gives these marginal experiences a heavy physical weight. In disintegration, it confirms a tragic condition of modern life: the capacity to feel beauty has not disappeared, yet it is forced to draw from pain and destruction themselves.

The desire drawn toward destruction, unable to resist moving closer to ruins, is what I call the “ruin complex.” It is fascinated by time’s ruthless carving of matter and by the violent entanglement of decay and new growth within the same form, lending disappearance a melancholy attraction that is difficult to refuse. What we recognize in the wreckage is not cheap tenderness but an almost obstinate form of care: a longing for the residual warmth of a world not yet entirely gone, and a determination to stop for things that have lost their use. Such care cannot reverse disappearance, but it forcibly draws the remnant into the present moment of our own existence and lets us confirm, through it, that we are still capable of feeling beauty.[^22]

This care for incompleteness ultimately becomes a starting point for building again on a narrative wasteland. Scattered remains resemble broken lines of verse, preserving one last trace of warmth for those still trying to make out a contour among ruins. The Ruin Romantic takes position in the fissure between destruction and survival: it wants to sacrifice every residual force to the instant of collapse, yet also tries, futilely, to stitch some glimmer of the past from fragments. Fragments cannot restore a lost age, much less fulfill the promise of a perfectly completed “redemption.” Yet before an irresistible disappearance, they refuse nihilism’s total nothingness and allow this desolation to become a place from which beauty can again be discovered, affirmed, and created.[^23]

## The Bearing of the Fool: A Utopia of Fragments

Once the desire to rebuild has receded, the ruin remains suspended in a time that resists resolution. The broken walls have not returned to life, yet neither have they found an ending in collapse. Amid the sustained tension woven from splendor not yet dispersed, unrealized wishes, and belated destruction, the ruin rises from the background of the story and becomes its narrative subject. It wakes from a long suspension and neglect, retakes the center of attention through the violent transformations born within destruction, and provokes an almost hallucinatory expectation—as if something inside that mixed body has not yet been spent and is waiting to be found.

To approach the ruin at this point is to encounter pity, curiosity, and the thrill of looking where one perhaps should not, all tangled together. In that expectation, we search among mixed fragments for what has been concealed, while feeling deeply drawn to the loosening of order and the failure of established form. This exploration exceeds everyday common sense and utilitarian purpose. It lets fragments that once had nothing to do with one another form relations through chance encounters, and it releases a creative desire long held down. What takes place here is not free-floating fantasy but immersion and initiation in the real weight of the ruin: facing, among the wreckage of massive things, the force that tore order apart; experiencing the withdrawal of the world in abandoned corners; even accepting bruises and pain as an unspoken cost of entering this wilderness. It is after this initiation, in listening to and discovering the site, that making reaches things long hidden beneath the surface and waiting to be awakened. They have been forming in this desolation for a long time and already possess their own narratives and bodily attitudes. Ruinwork does not sew fragments back together to restore the past. It follows the force that has sedimented there, reconnecting residues so they answer the expectation still unspent deep within the mixed body, and allowing dormant forces to reappear in the encounter of the present.

In response to this listening and answering deep within the texture of wreckage, the Ruinwright enters the site in the figure of the “Fool.” Here, the Fool is a posture that deliberately departs from social utility and the discipline of identity. In the desolation of the ruin, he looks for freedom among possibilities not yet exhausted by established conclusions and reawakens the most primitive impulse to build. Some resistance to the existing order may remain at first; but after long experience of this “freedom with nothing to possess,” what remains is the sheer pleasure of discovery and creation itself.

The Fool is the role temporarily assumed by the Ruinwright; the Folly is the material form taken by that performance. The former enters the wilderness without being completely disciplined by utilitarian measures. The latter, as an act of “folly” indifferent to gain or loss, gives material form to an action that knows it cannot last and responds anyway, condensing into a brief stay within a “bearing of the Fool.” It is a kind of Ruinwright’s stubbornness: knowing form cannot resist time, yet insisting that something not yet in existence should stand, however briefly, and be given a dignified appearance.[^24]

The Fool walks toward the cliff so that potential is not exhausted in advance by experience and conclusion. On this fleeting stage, the real desire behind looking at ruins can finally take material form, giving that desire, in return, a place on which feeling can be spent. Above the ruin, the Fool’s step remains light—not because he is ignorant, but because he knows clearly that nothing beneath his feet is guaranteed, and has not forgotten the weight those stones once bore. In this end-of-world landscape, he chooses for himself what to believe, and in a place without support continues to walk, and continues to create.[^25]

# Final Resonance — Playing After the Curtain Falls

Second nature is like a theater running under strict control, with people and things keeping to their assigned scripts. But when the chain of order breaks, abandoned ruins become the “backstage” left after the theater has cleared out. The grand narrative can no longer assign places to things; the settings of function and identity fail at once. The ruin does not hurry to offer new answers. It simply exposes, intact, the mess left behind by the collapse of order—a chaotic place not yet classified or gathered back under reason, where everything retains an unnamed, original appearance.[^26]

On this ground of unsupported freedom, the Ruinwright finds an ideal stage on which to release this “madness.” This madness is not irrational destruction. In the backstage left by failed order, he deliberately “plays” the Fool who does not calculate gain or loss, resisting second nature’s instinct to absorb the unknown too quickly and smooth away its strangeness. The Ruinwright intentionally delays rational judgment, allowing the loss of control, fear, and sublimity awakened by the unknown to remain for a little longer. It is in this brief deviation from order that we can draw close to a chaos not yet given form, and slowly build our own understanding and rules through prolonged negotiation with the ruin. Within this lucid madness, the Folly Series becomes a material projection of the impulse toward ruination.[^27]

## Postdramatic Space: Backstage

Many modern ruins abandoned by development and expansion remain in the afterwash of the wave called “progress.” Time in the ruin appears to have stopped only because it has fallen out of the viewpoint rushing along with that wave. Seen instead from the ruin’s own position, the stranded remains were never carried forward by the current of progress: the speed and inertia of the wave failed to take them with it and tore them instead into wounded bodies. Here the ruin itself continues to change. After the wave recedes, unfinished aftershocks and consequences replace the old image that preceded abandonment and become the present narrative subject of the space.

Within this derailed time, modern remnants continue to hold an unfinished disintegration. Past upheaval remains lodged in fractured surfaces, while unstable residual structures keep pushing the event toward the future. In these broken walls stranded by the current of the age, a distinct “postdramatic” condition emerges: there is no script that presets the future, nor any demand to restage the past. Residual stress and invading natural forces entangle with fragments in moment-to-moment change, constructing a stage and an aesthetics of presence that belong to the ruin itself.[^28]

If the ruin is understood as the form architecture leaves at the far end of time’s current, the Folly Series writes that endpoint into its own construction in advance and enters it through self-deconstruction. It carries an impulse to dismantle the form of death itself, and in doing so takes on the viewpoint of “symbolic residue”: built from the signs of civilization, it strips away that outer layer as it disintegrates and stands in civilization’s interior as a remnant that has lost civilization’s appearance, piercing back through the symbolic order already being undone. The viewer, drawn into this act of looking, can likewise step aside from the narrative of order and watch how civilization moves, step by step, from construction toward dissolution.[^29]

## Becoming an Instrument through Disintegration: A Deconstructive Performance Method

The most immediate image of a ruin is the fragment, but its most bodily experience often comes through sound. During human habitation, a building maintains a stable silence. Yet when that stability reaches the day it can no longer hold, a sudden fracture may break the long quiet from a corner outside our sight or from deep within the structure. The anomalous sound that cuts through silence also cuts through established spatial order, because it belongs to no timbre that ordinary experience can easily classify.

If “not sounding” can also be understood as an instruction of order, then an inhabited building is a thing designed to “remain silent.” In essence, a conventional instrument consists of silent materials overlaid with a fixed order, and produces predictable timbre by maintaining a tightly controlled set of relations. The ruin works in the opposite direction. Material is, in itself, a physical body capable of vibration and sound; its silence during habitation was merely the effect of architectural order covering over matter. Once it cannot avoid becoming ruin, it must pass through collapse, and in disintegration it must sound. Matter released from order then reveals its own sound, and collapse itself becomes the true performance of the instrument.

To restage on site this process of “stripping away order and releasing sound,” the Ruinwright reconnects dispersed fragments through an added transmission structure. The intervention does not seek to tame sound; it gives the stresses latent inside the structure a body through which to tremble. The Folly Series thus appears as an “instrument still played by collapse after order has left the stage.” Yet within a mechanical system that seems rationally designed, the resulting sound is abrasive and disordered. The absurdity released when order breaks becomes, for the first time, something physically audible: its score is no longer governed by human intention, but cuts directly into the death of architecture and, through it, extends toward a deeper deconstruction of order itself.

## From Detuning to Return: The Note That Burns Itself Out

If physical material continues to vibrate whether order is intact or broken, then the categories we have built from auditory expectation, tuning, and music theory are themselves only extensions of order into hearing. Reconsidered through fracture, the Chinese term 乐器 (yueqi, “musical instrument”) reveals a double discipline and arrangement: 乐 (“music”) is established by cultural habit and learned modes of listening, carrying narratives organized by order; 器 (“implement” or “instrument”) is defined by utilitarian purpose and structural performance, belonging to function. Once both “music” and “instrument” lose these supports, what remains is the roughest physical reality on site: sound itself—the “noise” that precedes classification and has not yet been domesticated by institutions.[^30]

Every instrument uses tuning and structural constraint to hold material and force within a precise balance, so that vibrations produced as energy is spent can fall accurately into a perceptual system recognized by human culture. When environmental erosion, structural deformation, or slackening tension occurs, the sound drifts across the delicate boundary maintained by habits of listening and systems of tuning, and is judged to be a “detuning,” an error produced by a break in order. Yet that “error” exists only under specific human rules and habits. From the physical scale of the material itself, this is not damage at all, but a component leaving an imposed constraint and returning to another, more natural material state. The ruin undergoes exactly the same transformation.[^31]Once social function and cultural narrative withdraw, the institutional order that once arranged the material gradually falls apart, but the components themselves do not stop acting: under gravity and weathering they continue to collide, vibrate, and fracture, releasing sounds no longer under arrangement.

The Folly Series constructs precisely this kind of “collapsing instrument,” one that moves step by step through collapse and disintegration on site. It makes sound through fracture, while irreversibly consuming itself in the performance and gradually losing the rules that once made it an instrument. At the instant we notice this “detuning,” we are abruptly stripped from established experience and pulled back into an absolute present, facing a pure sound not yet stained by any predetermined meaning or concept. In this unrepeatable performance, the act of playing is the entire process of disintegration. Sound approaches its limit through consumption, waiting only for the final instant when the structure tears all the way through and cuts across the present.

## Collapse at the Threshold: A Self-Destructive Finale

As the performance approaches its end, stage and instrument have already merged into the same body on the verge of destruction. The Folly Series confronts destructive energy with its own structure and, through sound that is progressively coming apart, dismantles before our ears the wholeness of that order. The work’s destruction enlarges the fissures of order to a material scale the body can feel; the viewer, through this destruction, tests whether they have touched something closer to the real. The body that is losing its form turns back toward us as well, reflecting the final limit we share.

To turn intact material into ruin, send it deliberately toward destruction, and let disorderly noise erupt from collapse—only through an act that appears almost “mad” by the utilitarian measure of daily life can a limited breach be cut into dull order, allowing contingency long flattened by the system to become publicly visible. Measured against social reality, the decision to stake the work itself carries a certain finality; it makes “the irreparable” into a brutal scale of measure. The Folly Series borrows precisely this irreversibility of self-destruction, wagering its physical body in an ultimate confrontation and using a decisive collapse to force a powerful symbolic order to acknowledge its own illusion and its cost.

When accumulated force finally crosses the threshold, the last tremor condenses into a “self-destructive final resonance.” Sound pierces discipline in an instant, because no established note can contain the pure clamor released by disintegration. The moment of sounding is also the moment the sounding structure fails. This simultaneity means that the note, at the instant of its birth, severs itself from the possibility of ever occurring again and becomes an unrepeatable material event. The stage withdraws at the very moment the performance is completed; the rules that had sustained instrument and stage collapse with the structure. This final resonance does not push destruction toward a sentimental dramatic climax. It simply lets the work arrive quietly at the terminal state of ruination that had already been written into its fate from the beginning of construction.[^32]

# Epilogue — After Ruinwork

When that “self-destructive final resonance” finally sinks into silence, what remains on site is not only disassembled wreckage but a lingering sense of distance and emptiness spreading through the mind. Our fascination with this self-destruction inadvertently tears open a deep fatigue of the postmodern city: when systemic collapse around us cannot be resolved, people learn to coexist with decay and draw from ruins a kind of lucid despair through an “acceptance by detachment.”

This romantic immersion in decline is born from a psychic fracture. It places the inner life in absolute solitude and tries to reduce the world to ruin, taking destruction as a paradoxical form of “redemption.” When immersion in collapse hardens into a psychological norm, nihilism begins to grow at the border between despair and aestheticization. The real danger of nihilism lies precisely in mistaking “destruction” for the only honest response to falsity. If reality can be proven only through breaking things, action must become more and more violent, until negation itself becomes a new rule and imprisons the person again inside a cage repeatedly reinforced by self-destruction.[^33]

Yet beyond this negative logic of destruction and its cycle of refusal, the ruin still exposes another romantic possibility. People continue to stop before things that have lost their use; they can still feel beauty in fragments that promise no return, and may even devote time to something they know cannot endure. Such experiences neither conceal the absurd nor repair reality, but they show that human sensitivity has not been completely occupied by utility, identity, and signs. Beauty here is not an answer of escape, but an innocence that confronts what is present at close range: it shows that we can still approach the world bodily, without making use, obedience, or revenge the condition of that approach.

The “innocence” meant here is not a naïve lack of worldly experience. It is the decision to turn back after seeing through the calculations behind the rules—a capacity to reopen the mind before things are brought once more under utilitarian law. It knows clearly that the meanings attached to decaying things will eventually disappear, yet still chooses to stop for what has lost its value and to spend effort on labor that guarantees no return. This return of mind and body therefore becomes a profound awakening to existence. It does not presume to offer the world some ultimate redemption, and it has already moved beyond self-destruction driven by revenge. What it breaks instead are the limits of the body, and beyond them, the boundaries written shut by established rules. In this double initiation, where both material construction and the image of identity are stripped away, the ruin reveals the inner life and open possibility of matter itself. Beauty erupts after this stripping, from the infinite possibilities of imagination and creation.

The human body, life, and capacity all have limits, and we do not need to flee that finitude. Precisely because time and existence have an end for us, where we choose to pour them becomes a genuine act of self-determination. The essence of ruinwork is, through one event of ruin after another, “to give beauty to imperfect destruction.” This beauty never excuses destruction, nor does it beautify the suffering behind it. It simply proves that, within a finite life, we still witnessed beauty, chose beauty, and by doing so truly existed.
